# Sandhi is Everywhere



In this chapter, we will see one of the most important and interesting phenomena of Sanskrit without which no other grammatical study can be undertaken.

The presence of sandhi rules is a proof of the fact that Sanskrit was a spoken language and is meant to be a spoken language. Every language has sandhi rules. Sanskrit grammarians however were the first ones to explicitly recognize this phenomenon (in Sanskrit, of course) and codify its principles.

**Try this out :** Say the phrase ‘*dogs and cats*’ spontaneously and casually. Record it out in your smartphone and listen to it yourself again.

If you spoke English casually at native speed, you would have most probably pronounced it as *’dog**z** and cats’*, unless you were really careful.

**Try this out :** Now try saying the sentence ‘*Bottles are cheap*’ casually and record it.

Again, if you spoke English casually at native speed, you would have most probably pronounced it as something like ‘*bo**d**ul**z** are cheap*’.

Why did this happen? This is because, in the ‘**s**’ sound in ‘dog**s**’, the ‘**s**’ is preceded by ‘**g**’ which is a voiced sound (your Adam’s apple vibrates when you say “ggggggg”). The sound following the ‘**s**’ in ‘dogs’ is ‘**a**’ in the next word ‘are’ which also is voiced - all vowels are voiced as your Adam’s apple vibrates (your Adam’s apple vibrates when you say “aaaaaaahhh”). But the sound ‘**s**’ is unvoiced (your Adam’s apple does not vibrate when you say “sssssssssss”). So to pronounce the sequence of sounds ***g**,**s**,**a*** ****in ‘*do**gs a**re*’, first your vocal chord has to vibrate for ‘**g**‘‘ stop it for the ‘**s**’ and then start vibrating again for the ‘**a**’. This is really difficult gymnastics for your throat. It will be easier on your throat to vibrate for the ‘**s**’ as well. But ‘**s**’, when vibrated, becomes ‘**z**’ and hence you end up saying ‘*dog**z** are*’ even if you did not consciously intend to. Same for the next example ‘*bo**d**el**z** are cheap*’. So, we realize that while the sound ‘**s**’ in ‘dog**s**‘ is easy to pronounce in isolation, in the presence of other sounds, it may be rendered difficult. So, in English, you subconsciously pronounce it differently while you believe you have pronounced only ‘**s**’ as in *‘dog**s** are*’. Just that in Sanskrit, you realize this explicitly and codify it as a set of rules because the speakers of this ancient language were very conscious of their pronunciation and recorded sentences in writing exactly as they were pronounced in flowing speech (instead of writing words pretending as though they were pronounced in isolation).

In fact these kinds of assimilation is what characterises natural human speech from non-native speech. A non-native speaker who learnt a language by mostly reading finds difficulty in listening comprehension and the phenomena that makes it the most difficult to transition from reading comprehension to listening comprehension is these kinds of sound changes.

How is it that the person listening to me is still able to comprehend this ‘***wrong***’ speech of mine (with sound simplifications) even when words are not pronounced the way he/she thinks they must be? That is where the redundancy comes to play. It is the same reason *why yxx cxn stxll xndxrstxnd whxt x xm wrxtxng hxrx xvxn xf x hxvx rxplxcxd xll thx vxwxls wxth xn “x”!*

These kind of phonological simplification still renders language comprehensible because language is not a merely random gibberish of sounds but has some predictability inside it, because this predictability enables it to fill the gaps left out by phonological simplification. (like you filled the gaps and still understood what I had written in italics at the end of the previous paragraph, even if I have distorted all the vowels). So, our language has some redundancy that enables it to tolerate a reasonable amount of distortion. In fact, the file compression program on my computer can exploit this redundancy in the letter sequences of a meaningful document of English words and squeeze them and store any English document two to four times smaller than what it actually is. The technical branch of mathematics and computer science that deals with it is called information theory (like I removed all the vowels and presented in blue to you without you losing any information). So, speakers of the English language do this simplification and still render their speech comprehensible because any language, being a non-random sequence of sounds that express predictable meanings, tolerates this distortion caused by phonological simplification. So, with this, your articulation becomes easier without affecting the intelligibility of the listener.

But in writing the language, you write as if you pronounced each word faithfully and separately. Imagine if English people started writing in newspapers like *“Id iz rainin catz and dogz im mani nodan stedz of India wich’s causin lodz of trabul tu de citizenz”* } instead of “*It is raining cats and dogs in many northern states of India which is causing lots of trouble to the citizens”*. The news report written in blue is actually the faithful duplication of the sounds that would be produced by a speaker reading it out. But we find it weird because our mind sees and looks for words and not sounds when reading something. But still it is easily able to decode what is written because the reader is familiar with both the speech and the sounds of English so that we can work backwards and figure out what the words in isolation would be like based on our internal knowledge of how words typically are heard in many situations. But imagine a person who has never heard or spoken English and learnt it as a second language only through reading and writing texts. Then he will be baffled with the kind of writing in blue because he has not built an intuition of the typical way in which streams of sounds come out because he has not listened or spoken in the language at all. Now, reading a Sanskrit text is like reading the text in blue. Since the current learners of Sanskrit learn it only by reading and writing as they are not exposed to listening and speaking in the language, they find this a major hurdle in reading Sanskrit texts. So, Sanskrit speakers wrote faithfully what they spoke and had extensively studied how sounds of words in their language change in flowing speech when words follow each other and termed these changes as **sandhi** : which in Sanskrit, means ‘*confluence*‘.

So, we see that sandhi is there in all languages the moment when people are speaking it on the street but just that you do not see them in grammar textbooks of English or Spanish because this merging and blending of sounds in natural speech are treated as deviations because subconsciously we think we are pronouncing the words as though they were in isolation even though we speak and hear in the blended version.

> **The Sandhi rules of Sanskrit is all about how when two words meet,
> the last sound of the initial word adapts itself to merge smoothly and
> ease pronunciation of the initial sound of the word that follows it.**

A knowledge of Sanskrit Sandhi rules is essential for getting a firm logical grip on the structure of the language and appreciating its phonetic and grammatical subtleties. That is why it is introduced here relatively in the beginning itself here even though we have not yet covered many grammar topics still. Sandhi rules of Sanskrit operate to facilitate ease of utterance and continuity in the pronunciation of words by making the final letter of the initial word and the initial letter of the following word or morpheme, change so that they can blend smoothly together and can be pronounced easily in succession. This is really important because since our ancestors were orally memorising and transmitting voluminous amounts of texts, when they recorded it down, they wrote it exactly as they were pronounced.

Students of Sanskrit memorize huge tables of Sandhi rules but all of them can be easily assimilated if you remember this golden rule :

> The sandhi rules of Sanskrit are there ***to make you pronounce things
> easily***. So, the only way to master and internalise the Sanskrit
> Sandhi rules efficiently without getting frustrated is to ***hear the
> language rather than read it*****!**

# Phonological Study of Sanskrit Sounds



Since the sandhi rules of Sanskrit exist to make pronunciation easier, it makes sense to master the basics of phonology (the science of Sanskrit sounds) before we move on to sandhi.

## Vowels



Sanskrit boasts of 12 vowels. Actually there are 13 but the extra one is encountered only in very few words so that it is omitted here.

> अ आ
>
> इ ई ए ऐ
>
> उ ऊ ओ औ
>
> ऋ ॠ

The first eight vowels are arranged in four pairs and come as a short one and a long one. The long vowel is pronounced for a longer amount of time (normally twice as much as the short vowel), and is denoted by a line above the letter in English transliteration.

### अ - आ : Low



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!9--I!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa1f49d06-4c07-4ec3-80ca-ce30ffd0b959_1748x968.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!9--I!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa1f49d06-4c07-4ec3-80ca-ce30ffd0b959_1748x968.png)





These two pairs of vowels that differ only in duration are pronounced with the mouth wide open and tongue kept low as in pronouncing the ‘u’ in the English word ‘run’. That is why the dentist asks you to show ‘aaaah’ to make you expose all of your mouth by keeping the tongue low. So this vowel is called a **low vowel** in phonology.

### इ - ई : High & Front



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!e-uK!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1913d0e5-c3cb-4706-9f60-113770f3d614_1748x986.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!e-uK!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1913d0e5-c3cb-4706-9f60-113770f3d614_1748x986.png)





These vowels are pronounced with the mouth narrow and tongue kept high as in pronouncing the ‘i’ in the English word ‘kin’. The tongue touches the central roof of the mouth (called the palate) and the point where it touches the roof of the mouth is in front. So these pair of vowels are called **high front vowels**. The mouth is wide and short like a strip in this position. It faciliates smile and that is why some people ask you to say ‘cheese’ to make you smile while shooting a picture of you.

### उ - ऊ : High & Back



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Qjw_!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0a3a0182-c834-43de-aa79-c4c05bf12b0d_1760x990.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Qjw_!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0a3a0182-c834-43de-aa79-c4c05bf12b0d_1760x990.png)





These vowels are pronounced with the mouth narrow and tongue kept high as in pronouncing the ‘u’ in the English word ‘put’. The tongue touches the roof of the mouth and the point where it touches the roof of the mouth is in the back this time. So these pair of vowels are called **high back vowels**. The lips look rounded and narrow as if you were to kiss someone.

### ऋ - ॠ : Retroflex



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!1kpV!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F25f9264a-a1f3-493a-854b-c9b1edfd69fe_1752x976.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!1kpV!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F25f9264a-a1f3-493a-854b-c9b1edfd69fe_1752x976.png)





This pair of vowel sounds is very peculiar and unique to Sanskrit. It involves curling of the tongue backwards inside the mouth (called **retroflexion**) in linguistics. Such gymnastics of the tongue is found uniquely in Indian languages that characterises the Indian accent. The sound that comes out is like the “soft r” sound in ‘butter’ or like the German way of saying “r” (the Germans have a tough time pronouncing ‘r’ the normal consonantal way). All retroflex sounds in Sanskrit are written with a dot below the letter in English transliteration.

The eight vowels encountered so far are called **pure vowels** or **simple vowels** and occur in pairs as a short one and a long one.

### ए : Front, Medium



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5o4O!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F18a639cc-b462-40d3-9d5f-7600b417f53b_1758x984.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5o4O!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F18a639cc-b462-40d3-9d5f-7600b417f53b_1758x984.png)





This vowel is denoted as ‘e’ in the English transliteration and is pronounced like ‘e’ in ‘bell’. These non-simple vowels of Sanskrit are always long and hence the line is omitted as the longevity is understood. The reason as to why the vowels ‘e’ and ‘o’ in Sanskrit are always long is explained shortly in a future section on vowel sandhi. For now note that, for the vowel ‘e’, the tongue position is mid way like that of between ‘a’ and ‘i’. The tongue does not fully touch the roof of the mouth and is narrow but not fully touching the roof or completely closed. So, phonologically, ‘e’ is between ‘a’ and ‘i’.

### ओ : Back, Medium



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!_Ndg!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4efbdb4e-b1c3-427a-8e56-1dba8f838a36_1874x1048.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!_Ndg!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4efbdb4e-b1c3-427a-8e56-1dba8f838a36_1874x1048.png)





This vowel is denoted as ‘o’ in the English transliteration and is pronounced like ‘o’ in ‘bore’. The impure vowels of Sanskrit are always long and hence the line is omitted as the longevity is understood. Note that, for the vowel ‘o’, the tongue position is mid way like that of between ‘a’ and ‘u’. The tongue does not fully touch the roof of the mouth and is narrow and rounded but not fully touching the roof or completely rounded. So, phonologically, ‘o’ is between ‘a’ and ‘u’.

### ऐ & औ : Diphthongs



Next, come the dipthongs which are combinations of two vowels and are also hence always long as they contain the sounds of two vowels. Sanskrit has two dipthongs - ‘ai’ and ‘au’. ‘ai’ is pronounced as like ‘i’ in ‘kite’. ‘au’ is pronounced as like ‘ou’ in ‘mouse’.

Sanskrit has a pure nasal sound called the **anusvara** that is articulated purely in the nose and is denoted in English transliteration as ṃ. It is like the pure humming sound as in ‘*hmmm*’. (this is the only sound in the English transliteration that has a lower dot but without retroflexion).

## Consonants



Next, we come to the consonants of Sanskrit. They are arranged as per the place of articulation into five groups - velars, palatals, retroflexes, dentals and nasals.

### The Throaty Consonants



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tAxu!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2e8acc3c-d267-4699-a8f3-2af21ce812be_1760x990.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tAxu!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2e8acc3c-d267-4699-a8f3-2af21ce812be_1760x990.png)





There are five **velar consonants** in Sanskrit. They all have the same place of articulation in the mouth. They all originate through blocking the air flow as a result of the tongue touching the back of the roof of the mouth (called the velum) that is close to the throat. They are transliterated in English as k, kh, g, gh, ṅ.

The first letter here ‘k’ क् is pronounced as in English ‘keep’. The second letter ‘kh’ ख् is pronounced like ‘k’ except that with some pressure in the gut that leads to a puff of air. This is called **aspiration**. Note that the larynx (Adam’s apple) does not vibrate while uttering both of these sounds. Hence they are called **voiceless consonants.**

The third letter ‘g’ ग् is pronounced as in English ‘get’. The fourth one ‘gh’ घ् is pronounced like ‘g’ with a puff of air and aspiration. Note that the larynx (Adam’s apple) does vibrate while uttering both of these sounds. Hence they are called **voiced consonants**.

The fifth sound ‘ṅ’ ङ् is the nasal sound that originates in the velum and is pronounced as the ‘ng’ in English ‘sing’. It is voiced and unaspirated but the nose also is involved in articulating this sound and hence it is called a **nasal consonant**. So, we have

> k - voiceless, unaspirated, non-nasal  
> kh - voiceless, aspirated, non-nasal  
> g - voiced, unaspirated, non-nasal  
> gh - voiced, aspirated, non-nasal  
> ṅ - voiced, unaspirated, nasal

These velar consonants are very prominent in Vedic Sanskrit. Often, the velars and palatals (described next) exchange their places. For example, the verb root ‘वच् vac’ that means to speak has nouns like ‘वचन vacana’ where the velar ‘k’ changes to a palatal ‘c’. And the verb root ‘भज् bhaj’ that means to divide has noun forms like ‘bhakti, vibhakti (व्)भक्ति’ etc... that mean division where the palatal has become a velar this time.

### The Roofy Consonants



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mGNq!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc43f3c72-c65b-4bdf-be7e-ae40aaeaa0c4_1750x984.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mGNq!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc43f3c72-c65b-4bdf-be7e-ae40aaeaa0c4_1750x984.png)





There are seven sounds in this class. They originate through blocking the air flow as a result of the tongue touching the front of the roof of the mouth that is soft tissue (called the palate). The first five of them are as usual arranged as voiceless unaspirated, voiceless aspirated, voiced unaspirated, voiced aspirated, and nasal. They are transliterated in English as ‘c, ch, j, jh, ñ’. ‘c’ is pronounced as the ‘ch’ in English ‘children’. ‘j’ is pronounced as in English ‘joy’. The nasal of this palatal category has no counterpart in English but is there in Spanish and in French represented by “ng”.

The sixth sound “sh” is called a **fricative** (more standardly : ś) as it involves a hissing sound produced due to friction. It is pronounced like in English ‘shame’.

The seventh sound is called a semi-vowel or a liquid, and is pronounced as in English ‘y’ in ‘yes’.

So, we have

> c - voiceless, unaspirated, non-nasal  
> ch - voiceless, aspirated, non-nasal  
> j - voiced, unaspirated, non-nasal  
> jh - voiced, aspirated, non-nasal  
> ñ - voiced, unaspirated, nasal  
> ś - voiceless, unaspirated, fricative (sh)  
> y - voiced, unaspirated, semi-vowel

### The Curly Consonants



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Uq_Y!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F52397977-df3c-4c3b-82bc-9670e0168d09_1756x974.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Uq_Y!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F52397977-df3c-4c3b-82bc-9670e0168d09_1756x974.png)





These group of consonants are called **retroflex consonants** and it involves curling the tongue backwards as said already. This has no counterpart in English and European languages. They are all written with a dot below. The retroflex ṭ is pronounced as like ‘t’ in English ‘tip’ but European speakers do not curl their tongue all the way back in doing this sound whereas Indian speakers do.

Again they are arranged into voiceless unaspirated, voiceless aspirated, voiced unaspirated, voiced aspirated, nasal, fricative and semi-vowel. The English transliterations are ṭ, ṭh, ḍ, ḍh, ṇ, ṣ, r. Note that the sound ‘r’ is always retroflex even if it does not have a dot and the dot below it is reserved for its retroflex vowel.

So, we have

> ṭ - voiceless, unaspirated, non-nasal  
> ṭh - voiceless, aspirated, non-nasal  
> ḍ - voiced, unaspirated, non-nasal  
> ḍh - voiced, aspirated, non-nasal  
> ṇ - voiced, unaspirated, nasal  
> ṣ - voiceless, unaspirated, fricative (sh)  
> r - voiced, unaspirated, semi-vowel

### Voiced vs Voiceless



Put your fingers on your throat and say any vowel like “aaaaah” or “eeeeeh” or “ooooh” or “aaiii”. The throat is felt to be in vibration for vowels. Hence, all vowels are voiced sounds. Among consonants, try saying “kkkkk” and “gggggg”. You see that the only difference between these two sounds is that the former does not vibrate the throat whereas the latter does. So, among consonants, not all of these sounds are voiced. The first and second letter in each row of consonants are voiceless, and so are the fricatives in the last. The visarga is voiceless as well. All other consonants are voiced.

### Aspirated vs Unaspirated



The only difference between “k” and “kh” is the presence of aspiration which is a puff of air that is released when the consonant is uttered. The second and the fourth consonant in each row are aspirated and all others are unaspirated. The letter “h” is aspirated as it involves releasing a puff of air by definition.

Why are the vowels assigned “grades”? Let us examine closely. The logic behind such a classification is that, as the grade increases, the mouth opens more widely. Note that low grade vowels come in two pairs - a short one and a long one. Except अ, all other medium grade vowels are long. All the high grade vowels are always long.

> Pronounce the following vowels in sequence:   
> इ→ए→ऐ  
> उ→ओ→औ
>
> You will observe that the mouth opens more widely as you go up the
> grade. Sanskrit grammarians call the medium and high grade vowels as
> गुण and वृद्धि respectively.

One might wonder - where are the medium and higher grades for ऋ and ऌ? It turns out that they are compound sounds. So, in order to give the complete picture, I will present another table below which gives the medium and high grade of these vowels as well (in red) , even though they are not strictly vowels.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!enrW!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F594c5ad5-002b-4035-8a8e-3d325085fd49_1686x292.png)

So, अर्, अल् , आर् ,आल् are the higher grades for ऋ and ऌ.

Why are vowel grades important? This is because they play a very important grammatical role. Usually, as a word undergoes change in grammatical function (like a verb changing tense or a noun changing to plural), then the vowel grades can change.

> Eg. Look at the following words  
> कृत = done  
> करोति = does  
> कार्य = the act of doing

All these words share the same core meaning (to do) but they differ in their grammatical role. The first word कृत is a past passive participle (done), the second word करोति is a present tense verb for third person singular (does) and the third word कार्य is the verbal noun (the act of doing). What is happening is that as the same root undergoes multiple derivations to form multiple words of related meaning, apart from the change in endings, we also can have a change in the grade of the root vowel (in this case, ऋ↔अर्↔आर्. Look at the case endings of a noun.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TRYj!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F20e300da-6a50-463d-83be-d5479e42a8ca_950x810.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TRYj!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F20e300da-6a50-463d-83be-d5479e42a8ca_950x810.png)





Note the following words - मातरि (in the mother) and मातृषु (in the mothers). They both are singular and plural locative forms of the root मातृ that means ‘mother’. But in the first one, you see a medium grade अर् and in the second, a low grade ऋ. So, the vowel grades can change as one goes through different grammatical paradigms.

> If you find this strange, even English does this.  
> sing↔sang↔sung↔song   
> give↔gave

In English too, note that the vowels in words change as it performs different grammatical functions. In English however, this vowel change is irregular and unpredictable due to its complicated history as a language. But in Sanskrit, this is a normal process and the important difference is that each vowel to another grade of its own only - and not of others. Eg. इ can change only to its own higher grades - ए and ऐ . It cannot change to higher grades of other vowels (except in rare cases).

NOTE: Sanskrit grammarians in their root dictionaries, present the vowel in the lowest grade at which it occurs in all possible forms. Most of the roots have vowels in the low grade forms but for some roots, since the low grade form does not occur in any word, they are quoted in medium grade. Note that the high grade is rare and occurs only in special forms (causatives and associatives about which we will talk in future).

So, if you were to search the root behind the word करोति and you have figured that the ओति is the ending and कर् is the core root, you should search for the root **कृ** in the dictionary which is the vowel in the low grade.

# Sandhi Rules : External Sandhi



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!J9Cs!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6fde667a-f53b-44d8-ad1c-877f4a770a13_1696x876.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!J9Cs!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6fde667a-f53b-44d8-ad1c-877f4a770a13_1696x876.png)





In this section, we will examine what is called external sandhi. External sandhi happens between two different words. It concerns the final letter of the first word and the initial letter of the next word. Whereas internal sandhi happens between the final letter of a root and the initial letter of the suffix within a single word. Sandhi is broadly classified into two types depending on the nature of the sounds that meet. They are

1.  **Vowel Sandhi** स्वरसन्धिः (when both letters are vowels)

2.  **Consonant Sandhi** व्यञ्जनसन्धिः (when one of the letters is a
    consonant)

3.  **Visarga Sandhi** विसर्गसन्धिः (when the initial letter is the
    visarga)

Note : Sandhi does not happen when a vowel and a consonant meet which happens when a word ends in a vowel and the following word begins with a consonant.

> **Examples of no-sandhi :**   
> धर्मस्य ग्लानिः  
> सर्वेषु भक्तेषु  
> अकुर्वत सञ्जय

In this chapter, we shall deal only with external sandhi. Since many rules of internal sandhi are identical to that of external sandhi, we will study internal sandhi separately as and when we encounter verb roots.

## Vowel Sandhi



Let us now see what happens when two vowels meet at the boundary of Sanskrit words. Try saying the abbreviation “ECU” in English that is expected to be pronounced as ‘ईसीऊ’, fast and casually. Record it yourself and listen to it. Most probably, you would have pronounced as ईसीयू or ईसीवू .

Why did you insert a **y** or a **v**? The basic principle is that : Two vowels cannot be easily pronounced together in distinction.

So, you inserted a semi-vowel in between to facilitate that distinction. The semivowels corresponding to i and u respectively are y and v and hence you inserted them between the two successive vowel sounds. Sanskrit similarly follows two kinds of strategies to avoid two vowels in succession at any cost. They are:

- The two vowels merge to form a single vowel.  
  Example : महा + उदयः = महोदयः

- The initial vowel itself converts into a semi-vowel (unlike the
  example above where there is a semi-vowel inserted between)  
  Example : प्रति + उषा = प्रत्युषा

### अ vowel rises the grade : गुणसन्धि/वृद्धिसन्धि



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FtNA!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8da10c1a-8d23-438f-ad62-5c8d71b41454_1758x966.png)

When the initial vowel is अ/आ, there is no semi-vowel associated with them. So they cannot be semivocalized. But it simply cannot vanish and has to make its presence evident. What does it do? What happens is that the अ/आ vanishes but it modifies the vowel that follows it to makes its presence felt. But how can अ make its presence felt? We know that अ is all about opening one’s mouth fully open. Do you remember in the previous chapter on vowel gradation that increase in grade means that the mouth is getting open wider and wider? So yeah, **अ leaves but makes its effect felt by increasing the grade of the unlike vowel following it**. If it is low, it becomes medium grade. If it is medium, it becomes high grade. If it is already high grade, nothing happens and the अ simply drops off. Same for आ.

## Consonant Sandhi



Now, we will go to the case when atleast one of the sounds is a **hard consonant**. It is considered very difficult for students to remember the formulas for all hard consonant sandhis because it may be daunting when looked at, all at once. But the thing is there are just five golden rules at play and through that, one can derive most of the formulae for hard consonant sandhis. There are three things when it comes to compatibility of consonants - voice, aspiration and place of articulation and nasality. There is a golden rule for each of these 4 features and another one as to the overall nature of external sandhi. We have already seen about the distinction between voiced-vs-voiceless, aspirated-vs-unaspirated, velar-palatal-retroflex-dental-labial and nasal consonants in the previous chapter.

### The Five Golden Rules



**Golden Rule 1:** It is difficult to pronounce two aspirated consonants in succession.

**Golden Rule 2:** It is better to have two sounds in succession with the same voice which means that the voicing of the final sound in the preceding word should match with the voicing of the initial sound of the following word.

**Golden Rule 3:** It is difficult to keep the tongue straight and then suddenly bend it or curl it.

**Golden Rule 4:** It is difficult to suddenly turn your nose on.

**Golden Rule 5:** In external sandhi, (**excepting retroflexion loss),** it is the initial sound that adapts, for the most part\*.

\*External sandhi is a **one-way street**. It is the initial sound that undergoes all the adaptation and change to cohabitate with the following sound, **unless it is loss of retroflexion**. The following sound stays as it is, without changing anything (excepting retroflexion gain)

Keeping simply these rules in mind, let us work out some examples ourselves before we derive generic rules.

**Example 1:** जगत्+नाथः

Sounds that meet : त् + न्

Rule 1 does not apply as neither त् nor न् is aspirated.

Rule 2 does apply because while त् is voiceless, न् is indeed voiced (nose and throat vibrates)

Rule 3 does not apply as both त् and न् do not involve sudden curling or bending of the tongue

Rule 4 does apply because त् is not nasal while न् is nasal and so there is a sudden turning ON of the nose

Rule 5 says that it is the initial त् that undergoes any change to cohabitate with the following न्. Since none of the consonants are retroflex, no need to worry about the bracket in Rule 5.

So, we have rules 2 and 4. It is difficult to suddenly turn your nose ON. But the न् by rule 5, won’t change. So, it is the त् that has to become a nasal like न्. If त् becomes nasal, it is also voiced and hence it solves rule 3. But we know that when त् has to nasalize itself, the only nasal in the same place of articulation is न् only. So, we have त् → न्. This also takes care of rule 2 as both nasals are voiced.

So, we have जगत् + नाथः = जगन्नाथः

**Example 2:** जगत्+गुरुः

Sounds that meet : त् + ग्

Rule 1 does not apply as neither त् nor ग् is aspirated

Rule 2 does apply as त् is voiceless whereas ग् is voiced

Rules 3 and 4 do not apply as neither त् nor ग् are nasal or involve the tongue curling

Rule 5 says that it is the त् that adapts for the ग्. Now, ग् is voiced and won’t change (rule 5). Again, since none of the consonants are retroflex, no need to worry about the exception in Rule 5. So, for the sake of rule 2, त् has to be voiced. But what is the voiced consonant with the same place of articulation of त् ? It is द्. So, we have

जगत् + गुरुः = जगद्गुरुः

**Example 3:** वाक् + ईशः

Sounds that meet : क् + ई

Since one is a consonant and another is a vowel, most of the rules do not apply as they deal about successive consonants. Only rule 2 applies as क् is unvoiced whereas ई, being a vowel is always voiced (vowels always vibrate your Adam’s apple which is why you cannot sing any song without vowels). By rule 5, ई won’t adapt and hence क् has to become voiced for coexisting with ई. (again no need to worry for the bracket). But क् when voiced, becomes a ग्. Hence,

वाक् + ईशः = वागीशः

**Example 4:** आपद् + कालः

Sounds that meet : द् + क्

Rules 1,3,4 do not apply and no need to worry about bracket in rule 5.

द् is voiced whereas क् is voiced. By Rule 5, क् won’t change and hence द् has to become unvoiced. So, द् changes to त् and we have

आपद् + कालः = आपत्कालः

**Example 5:** बृहत् + ढमरुः

Sounds that meet : त् + ढ्

Here, rule 1 does not apply as we do not have two aspirated consonants but only one

Rule 2 does apply - त् is voiceless whereas ढ् is voiced. By rule 5, ढ् remains as it is and hence त् voices itself and becomes a द्. But this is not over - the answer is not ~~बृहद्ढमरुः~~

Because rule 3 applies here. द् involves keeping the tongue straight whereas ढ् is a retroflex consonant and involves immense tongue curling. But ढ् won’t change by rule 5 and hence you are forced to curl your tongue. When you are forced to run a marathon next month for which you do not have any stamina now, what do you do? You start practising before hand so that the eventual running in the marathon is not a sudden burden on you. So you prepare for the eventual retroflexion by retroflexing द् to ड्. Hence, we have

बृहत् + ढमरुः = बृहड्ढमरुः

Note that the initial sound is not losing retroflexion but gaining it. So, the bracket does not apply. So, this is the final answer.

But again च् won’t adapt and hence you are forced to curl eventually. So, you prepare for it. So, त् turns into a palatal consonant without voice and aspiration which is च् itself. So, we have

सत् + चित् = सच्चित्

If we blindly apply rule 5, the initial ष् should lose its retroflexion. So eventhough the final sound is adamant about every other feature, it does have a soft spot for its partner that precedes it. When it comes to its preceding partner losing retroflexion, it accepts to become retroflexed - the only time it agrees to change. So, we have

दृष् + तः = दृष्टः

## Visarga Sandhi



Now we come to the case when the initial sound is the visarga, denoted by : which we saw in the previous chapter. Many Sanskrit words (especially masculine nouns and adjectives) end in a visarga and hence it is important. It is nothing but the expulsion of air from the mouth. Indeed, the word visarga in Sanskrit means ‘release’. Naturally, we would not want to expel all the air out of the mouth in the middle of a sentence as we have to breathe fresh to start a new word again. So this visarga is pronounced as such only when it occurs in the end of a sentence and everywhere else, it changes depending on the sound that follows it in the next word. Visarga sandhi is considered very notorious and tough but we see that it is logical and once again has logical principles of phonetics behind it.

### Anticipatory Fricativisation



> Logic:   
> Visarga = complete release of air (**:**)  
> Fricative = partial hissy release of air (स ,श, ष्) (next better thing
> to have)

This happens only when the visarga is *followed by a voiceless stop or a voiceless fricative* (because Sanskrit has only voiceless fricatives स ,श, ष् and does not have voiced fricatives like **z**, french **j**). We see that voiceless sounds are hard and involve blockage of air completely and fricatives involve partial air flow. So, when the sound that follows the visarga is voiceless stop or a fricative, it prepares for that by fricativizing itself, if the corresponding fricative is available. If the fricative corresponding to the next sound is unavailable, then it stays as such.

**(:) \[+ voiceless consonant\] → fricative**

(of the same place as voiceless consonant)

What if the visarga if it meets voiced sounds like vowels or semi-vowels or voiced consonants? There are no voiced fricatives and how does the language get its way around it? Then, it depends on what is the vowel preceding the visarga. Whether it is अ, आ or any other voiced sound (including voiced consonants).

### Rounding the mouth for अः



When you have अः, then for अ you keep the mouth wide open and for the visarga (:) , you release the air. Now when you release the air, you will round your lips and they will sort of be like in the same position as उ. Now since you do not want to release air midway, you hold your air but still keep the tongue as though you pretend to release the air. But we know अ+उ=ओ. So, we have

**अः\[+ voiced sound\] → ओ**

Examples:

7.   नमः + नारायणाय = नमोनारायाणाय

8.   प्रियः + नरः = प्रियोनरः

**NOTE:** What if the following sound is a vowel? If the visarga अः becomes ओ, then we will have a vowel vowel collision. Does it mean that we apply vowel-vowel sandhi again? No. If the vowel following is अ, then that following vowel is elided (lost) and is denoted in writing as ऽ. Otherwise, the vowel-vowel hiatus persists. This is the only place where Sanskrit allows a vowel-vowel hiatus. Due to a अः visarga becoming ओ.

Examples:

9.   शिवः + अहं = शिवोऽहं

10.  सत्वः + आहारः = सत्वो आहारः

### Elimination for आः



What about आः? The thing is that already आ is a long vowel and it is time-consuming to do any gymnastics after it. So it simply vanishes. So आः is always converted to आ before any voiced sound. It is pronounced as such only at the end of a sentence. Even if the presence of आ leads to a vowel-vowel hiatus, it is tolerated.

**आः\[+ voiced sound\] → आ**

# Sandhi Rules : Internal Sandhi



In this small section, we will see some important sound changes that happen within the word to ease pronunciation. All of them have got to do with easing retroflexion.

## The RUKI Rule & Retroflexion of स् → ष् : ष्त्वम्



Now, let us study an important transformation that occurs in Sanskrit called the Ruki rule. It stays that

र् / ऋ / ॠ /उ / ओ / औ / क् / ख् / ग् / घ् / इ / ए / ऐ + **स् ==\> ष्**

The **R** in ruki stands for the sounds र् / ऋ / ॠ, the **U** in ruki stands for the sounds उ / ओ / औ , the **K** stands for the sounds क् / ख् / ग् / घ् and the **I** in ruki stands for the sounds इ / ए / ऐ . The rule states that when any RUKI sound following **स्** within a word triggers its retroflexion to **ष्.** Also since both स् and ष् are voiceless and that the preceding sound has to assimilate in voice, and no two aspirated consonants can come in succession, we have

क् / ख् / ग् / घ् +**स् =** क् + **ष् = क्ष्**

We will see this happening everywhere in internal sandhi.

Eg. उप (near) + नि (proximity) + सद् (to sit) = उपनि**ष**द्

## The RS Rule & Retroflexion of न् → ण् : णत्वम्



The retroflex consonants र् and ष् within a word trigger the retroflexion of the following न् sound to ण् sound. But unlike ruki rule, this triggering can happen even when there is a gap between र्/ष् and the न् sound. Which sounds can come in between in the gap and still enable this retroflexion? Generally, any sound that we can pronounce without moving our tongue very much can come in between. These are:

- all vowels

- य् / व्

- Velar consonants (back velum) क् ख् ग् घ् ङ्

- Bilabial consonants (lips) प् फ् ब् भ् म्

There are some exceptions and subtleties to this rule. For example :

- this retroflexion is blocked when the dental न् is at the end of a word
  (e.g. अकुर्वन्)

- this retroflexion is also blocked when the dental न् is immediately
  followed by another dental consonant (e.g. क्रान्तः)

For now, just remember that n can change to ṇ if it follows r or ṣ and there are only those consonants in between that can be pronounced without moving the tongue.

Eg. **** राम + आयन = रामायण

परि + नाम = परिणाम

There are many other subtleties to all the rules we have discussed so far but for now just remember these tendencies.

## Tendencies of Root Sounds



### Palatalisation of Velars



In this section, we will talk about another phenomenon that occurs in Sanskrit. We will discuss the concept of verb roots deeply in the next chapter and how every word can actually be derived from a verb root but for now I will give a superficial exposition to the kind of sound changes that you can expect to see when you look at derivations of words. One such tendency was already seen before - vowel gradation, which refers to the change in grades of the vowels in related words. Now we shall explore some changes in consonants as well. We will also get accustomed to internal sandhi in this process.

Consider the following related words:

व॒क्ति - he speaks

उ॒वाच॑ - he spoke

व॒क्ष्यति॑ - he will speak

वा॒चय॑ति - he causes to speak

विव॑क्षति - he wants to speak

वोच॑ति - he may speak

वच॑नम् - speech

One sees that all of these words are derived from the core idea of speaking. But some words seem to use वक् as the part that conveys the core idea to speak whereas other words seem to use वच् as the part. So there is a tendency of alternation between patalal sounds (like च्) and a velar sound like क्. When this happens, grammarians always like to quote the root in the palatal form as a convention. For example, in a Sanskrit root dictionary, you will find only the verb root वच् and it is assumed that you are aware that this palatal at the end can become velarized in word derivations. As a rule, before a consonant, one always finds only the velar form. Eg. वच्+ष्यति =\> वक् + ष्यति = व॒क्ष्यति॑ . So whenever you see a च् at the end of a root, be prepared to expect a क्ष् for sure in forms where a suffix beginning with a consonant (ष् is one such popular suffix) is attached. Same happens for roots ending in ज्/श्.

A root ending in a palatal ज् has a tendency to velarize to ग् (a velar) and to further undergo the change as ज्+ष् =\> ग्+ष् =\> क्+ष् =\> क्ष् before suffixes starting with ष्. It comes क् before other suffixes starting with voiceless consonants like त्. As an example, consider the following related words.

भज॑ति - he divides

भ॒ज्यते॑ - it is divided

भ॒क्तः - divided

भा॒गः - division (share)

See that the ज् in the root भज् palatalizes to ग् in a derivative like भा॒गः and further becomes a voiceless क् due to consonant sandhi with a voiceless suffix like भग्+तः = भक्तः

This is also seen in roots ending in a palatal fricative श् that has a tendency to convert to क्. Sometimes, before the suffixes starting with त्, the श् also has a tendency to trigger a retroflexion of itself to ष् and that of त् to ट्. As an example, consider the following related words.

दि॒शति॑ - he directs / points out

अदि॑क्षत् - he has directed / he has pointed out

दि॒ष्टः - pointed out / directed \[retroflexion: श्+त =\> ष्+ट\]

दिक्पालः - protector from all sides

दि॒शा - direction

### The velarization of ह् to घ्



ह् has a tendency to become घ् in derivatives. When there is an alternation between ह् /घ्, the root as a convention is always quoted with a ह् . Consider the following words:

हन्ति॑ - he strikes / he slays

घ्न॒न्ति - they kill / they slay

ह॒तः - slain

घा॒तय॑ति - he causes to slay

Notice how the ह् changes to घ् across various derivations of words related to slaying.

Now, comes an internal sandhi rule. When ह् comes at the end of a root and changes to घ्, it undergoes a sandhi before encountering suffixes that begin with a consonant. Consider the following internal sandhi

घ् (from root) + त् (from suffix)

Under normal external sandhi, the initial sound completely assimilates to the final sound in voice and aspiration and we will have ~~घ् + त् = क्त्.~~ But in the case of internal sandhi between a root and a suffix alone, **the root sound being important to the meaning of a word, is preserved even if it is the initial sound and it is the final sound that adapts.**

So, we first have घ् + त् = घ् + द् (the त् becoming द् to become voiced like घ्). So, we have घ्द्. But the aspiration in the initial consonant cannot simply disappear as it is difficult to pronounce and hence it leaves its mark by transferring itself to the final consonant. So we have घ् + द् =\> ग् + ध् = ग्ध्. As an example, consider the following related words.

दु॒हन्ति॑ - they milk out

दोग्धि॑ - he milks out (दोग्धि॑ = दोघ्+ति)

दु॒ग्धः - milk (दुघ्+तः)

As an example of another internal sandhi involving preservation of initial consonant and transfer of aspiration, we have

बोध॑ति - he wakes (root बुध्)

बु॒द्धः - awakened one (बुध्+तः)

But what happens when we have something like घ्+ष्/स् . The aspiration cannot be transferred to a fricative like ष्/स् . In these situations, it is not uncommon to see the aspiration transferred to the previous consonant in the root. As an example from the same root, we have the following derivation

अभु॑त्स्महि - we were awakened (अ+**बुध्**+स्+महि)

The ह् sometimes also alternates to ध् / ढ् for some roots.

रि॒हन्ति॑ - they lick (root रिह्)

रेढि॑ - he licks (रेढ्+ति)

Just keep these tendencies in mind that the pure aspirate ह् has a tendency to change itself to any one of the voiced aspirates which in most of the cases is घ् and also that aspirates of the final consonant of a root is transferred to the succeeding consonant of a suffix if possible or to the initial consonant of the root if the former is impossible.

Thus, we have covered almost all of the sandhi rules in Sanskrit and other tendencies for sound change in the language.

> व्यवसायो व्यवस्थानः संस्थानः स्थानदो ध्रुवः ।
>
> परर्द्धिः परमस्पष्टस्तुष्टः पुष्टः शुभेक्षणः ॥ ४२॥
>
> रामो विरामो विरजो मार्गो नेयो नयोऽनयः । (विरामो विरतो)
>
> वीरः शक्तिमतां श्रेष्ठो धर्मो धर्मविदुत्तमः ॥ ४३॥
>
> वैकुण्ठः पुरुषः प्राणः प्राणदः प्रणवः पृथुः ।
>
> हिरण्यगर्भः शत्रुघ्नो व्याप्तो वायुरधोक्षजः ॥ ४४॥
>
> ऋतुः सुदर्शनः कालः परमेष्ठी परिग्रहः ।
>
> उग्रः संवत्सरो दक्षो विश्रामो विश्वदक्षिणः ॥ ४५॥
>
> विस्तारः स्थावरस्थाणुः प्रमाणं बीजमव्ययम् ।
>
> अर्थोऽनर्थो महाकोशो महाभोगो महाधनः ॥ ४६॥
>
> अनिर्विण्णः स्थविष्ठोऽभूर्धर्मयूपो महामखः ।
>
> नक्षत्रनेमिर्नक्षत्री क्षमः क्षामः समीहनः ॥ ४७॥
>
> यज्ञ इज्यो महेज्यश्च क्रतुः सत्रं सतां गतिः ।
>
> सर्वदर्शी विमुक्तात्मा सर्वज्ञो ज्ञानमुत्तमम् ॥ ४८॥
>
> सुव्रतः सुमुखः सूक्ष्मः सुघोषः सुखदः सुहृत् ।
>
> मनोहरो जितक्रोधो वीरबाहुर्विदारणः ॥ ४९॥
>
> स्वापनः स्ववशो व्यापी नैकात्मा नैककर्मकृत् ।
>
> वत्सरो वत्सलो वत्सी रत्नगर्भो धनेश्वरः ॥ ५०॥
>
> धर्मगुब्धर्मकृद्धर्मी सदसत्क्षरमक्षरम् ।
>
> अविज्ञाता सहस्रांशुर्विधाता कृतलक्षणः ॥ ५१॥
>
> गभस्तिनेमिः सत्त्वस्थः सिंहो भूतमहेश्वरः ।
>
> आदिदेवो महादेवो देवेशो देवभृद्गुरुः ॥ ५२॥
>
> उत्तरो गोपतिर्गोप्ता ज्ञानगम्यः पुरातनः ।
>
> शरीरभूतभृद्भोक्ता कपीन्द्रो भूरिदक्षिणः ॥ ५३॥
>
> सोमपोऽमृतपः सोमः पुरुजित्पुरुसत्तमः ।
>
> विनयो जयः सत्यसन्धो दाशार्हः सात्वताम्पतिः ॥ ५४॥ विनियोज्यः
>
> जीवो विनयिता साक्षी मुकुन्दोऽमितविक्रमः ।
>
> अम्भोनिधिरनन्तात्मा महोदधिशयोऽन्तकः ॥ ५५॥
>
> अजो महार्हः स्वाभाव्यो जितामित्रः प्रमोदनः ।
>
> आनन्दो नन्दनो नन्दः सत्यधर्मा त्रिविक्रमः ॥ ५६॥
>
> महर्षिः कपिलाचार्यः कृतज्ञो मेदिनीपतिः ।
>
> त्रिपदस्त्रिदशाध्यक्षो महाश‍ृङ्गः कृतान्तकृत् ॥ ५७॥
>
> महावराहो गोविन्दः सुषेणः कनकाङ्गदी ।
>
> गुह्यो गभीरो गहनो गुप्तश्चक्रगदाधरः ॥ ५८॥
>
> वेधाः स्वाङ्गोऽजितः कृष्णो दृढः सङ्कर्षणोऽच्युतः ।
>
> वरुणो वारुणो वृक्षः पुष्कराक्षो महामनाः ॥ ५९॥
>
> भगवान् भगहाऽऽनन्दी वनमाली हलायुधः ।
>
> आदित्यो ज्योतिरादित्यः सहिष्णुर्गतिसत्तमः ॥ ६०॥
>
> सुधन्वा खण्डपरशुर्दारुणो द्रविणप्रदः ।
>
> दिवस्पृक् सर्वदृग्व्यासो वाचस्पतिरयोनिजः ॥ ६१॥ (दिविस्पृक्)
>
> त्रिसामा सामगः साम निर्वाणं भेषजं भिषक् ।
>
> संन्यासकृच्छमः शान्तो निष्ठा शान्तिः परायणम् ॥ ६२॥
>
> शुभाङ्गः शान्तिदः स्रष्टा कुमुदः कुवलेशयः ।
>
> गोहितो गोपतिर्गोप्ता वृषभाक्षो वृषप्रियः ॥ ६३॥
>
> अनिवर्ती निवृत्तात्मा सङ्क्षेप्ता क्षेमकृच्छिवः ।
>
> श्रीवत्सवक्षाः श्रीवासः श्रीपतिः श्रीमतांवरः ॥ ६४॥
>
> श्रीदः श्रीशः श्रीनिवासः श्रीनिधिः श्रीविभावनः ।
>
> श्रीधरः श्रीकरः श्रेयः श्रीमाँल्लोकत्रयाश्रयः ॥ ६५॥
>
> स्वक्षः स्वङ्गः शतानन्दो नन्दिर्ज्योतिर्गणेश्वरः ।
>
> विजितात्माऽविधेयात्मा सत्कीर्तिश्छिन्नसंशयः ॥ ६६॥
>
> उदीर्णः सर्वतश्चक्षुरनीशः शाश्वतस्थिरः ।
>
> भूशयो भूषणो भूतिर्विशोकः शोकनाशनः ॥ ६७॥
>
> अर्चिष्मानर्चितः कुम्भो विशुद्धात्मा विशोधनः ।
>
> अनिरुद्धोऽप्रतिरथः प्रद्युम्नोऽमितविक्रमः ॥ ६८॥
>
> कालनेमिनिहा वीरः शौरिः शूरजनेश्वरः ।
>
> त्रिलोकात्मा त्रिलोकेशः केशवः केशिहा हरिः ॥ ६९॥
>
> कामदेवः कामपालः कामी कान्तः कृतागमः ।
>
> अनिर्देश्यवपुर्विष्णुर्वीरोऽनन्तो धनञ्जयः ॥ ७०॥
>
> ब्रह्मण्यो ब्रह्मकृद् ब्रह्मा ब्रह्म ब्रह्मविवर्धनः ।
>
> ब्रह्मविद् ब्राह्मणो ब्रह्मी ब्रह्मज्ञो ब्राह्मणप्रियः ॥ ७१॥
>
> महाक्रमो महाकर्मा महातेजा महोरगः ।
>
> महाक्रतुर्महायज्वा महायज्ञो महाहविः ॥ ७२॥
>
> स्तव्यः स्तवप्रियः स्तोत्रं स्तुतिः स्तोता रणप्रियः ।
>
> पूर्णः पूरयिता पुण्यः पुण्यकीर्तिरनामयः ॥ ७३॥
>
> मनोजवस्तीर्थकरो वसुरेता वसुप्रदः ।
>
> वसुप्रदो वासुदेवो वसुर्वसुमना हविः ॥ ७४॥
>
> सद्गतिः सत्कृतिः सत्ता सद्भूतिः सत्परायणः ।
>
> शूरसेनो यदुश्रेष्ठः सन्निवासः सुयामुनः ॥ ७५॥
>
> भूतावासो वासुदेवः सर्वासुनिलयोऽनलः ।
>
> दर्पहा दर्पदो दृप्तो दुर्धरोऽथापराजितः ॥ ७६॥
>
> विश्वमूर्तिर्महामूर्तिर्दीप्तमूर्तिरमूर्तिमान् ।
>
> अनेकमूर्तिरव्यक्तः शतमूर्तिः शताननः ॥ ७७॥
>
> एको नैकः सवः कः किं यत् तत्पदमनुत्तमम् ।
>
> लोकबन्धुर्लोकनाथो माधवो भक्तवत्सलः ॥ ७८॥
>
> सुवर्णवर्णो हेमाङ्गो वराङ्गश्चन्दनाङ्गदी ।
>
> वीरहा विषमः शून्यो घृताशीरचलश्चलः ॥ ७९॥
>
> अमानी मानदो मान्यो लोकस्वामी त्रिलोकधृक् ।
>
> सुमेधा मेधजो धन्यः सत्यमेधा धराधरः ॥ ८०॥
>
> तेजोवृषो द्युतिधरः सर्वशस्त्रभृतां वरः ।
>
> प्रग्रहो निग्रहो व्यग्रो नैकश‍ृङ्गो गदाग्रजः ॥ ८१॥
>
> चतुर्मूर्तिश्चतुर्बाहुश्चतुर्व्यूहश्चतुर्गतिः ।
>
> चतुरात्मा चतुर्भावश्चतुर्वेदविदेकपात् ॥ ८२॥
>
> समावर्तोऽनिवृत्तात्मा दुर्जयो दुरतिक्रमः ।
>
> दुर्लभो दुर्गमो दुर्गो दुरावासो दुरारिहा ॥ ८३॥
>
> शुभाङ्गो लोकसारङ्गः सुतन्तुस्तन्तुवर्धनः ।
>
> इन्द्रकर्मा महाकर्मा कृतकर्मा कृतागमः ॥ ८४॥
>
> उद्भवः सुन्दरः सुन्दो रत्ननाभः सुलोचनः ।
>
> अर्को वाजसनः श‍ृङ्गी जयन्तः सर्वविज्जयी ॥ ८५॥
>
> सुवर्णबिन्दुरक्षोभ्यः सर्ववागीश्वरेश्वरः ।
>
> महाह्रदो महागर्तो महाभूतो महानिधिः ॥ ८६॥
>
> कुमुदः कुन्दरः कुन्दः पर्जन्यः पावनोऽनिलः ।
>
> अमृताशोऽमृतवपुः सर्वज्ञः सर्वतोमुखः ॥ ८७॥
>
> सुलभः सुव्रतः सिद्धः शत्रुजिच्छत्रुतापनः ।
>
> न्यग्रोधोऽदुम्बरोऽश्वत्थश्चाणूरान्ध्रनिषूदनः ॥ ८८॥
>
> सहस्रार्चिः सप्तजिह्वः सप्तैधाः सप्तवाहनः ।
>
> अमूर्तिरनघोऽचिन्त्यो भयकृद्भयनाशनः ॥ ८९॥
>
> अणुर्बृहत्कृशः स्थूलो गुणभृन्निर्गुणो महान् ।
>
> अधृतः स्वधृतः स्वास्यः प्राग्वंशो वंशवर्धनः ॥ ९०॥
>
> भारभृत् कथितो योगी योगीशः सर्वकामदः ।
>
> आश्रमः श्रमणः क्षामः सुपर्णो वायुवाहनः ॥ ९१॥
>
> धनुर्धरो धनुर्वेदो दण्डो दमयिता दमः ।
>
> अपराजितः सर्वसहो नियन्ताऽनियमोऽयमः ॥ ९२॥
>
> सत्त्ववान् सात्त्विकः सत्यः सत्यधर्मपरायणः ।
>
> अभिप्रायः प्रियार्होऽर्हः प्रियकृत् प्रीतिवर्धनः ॥ ९३॥
>
> विहायसगतिर्ज्योतिः सुरुचिर्हुतभुग्विभुः ।
>
> रविर्विरोचनः सूर्यः सविता रविलोचनः ॥ ९४॥
>
> अनन्तो हुतभुग्भोक्ता सुखदो नैकजोऽग्रजः ।
>
> अनिर्विण्णः सदामर्षी लोकाधिष्ठानमद्भुतः ॥ ९५॥
>
> सनात्सनातनतमः कपिलः कपिरव्ययः ।
>
> स्वस्तिदः स्वस्तिकृत्स्वस्ति स्वस्तिभुक्स्वस्तिदक्षिणः ॥ ९६॥
>
> अरौद्रः कुण्डली चक्री विक्रम्यूर्जितशासनः ।
>
> शब्दातिगः शब्दसहः शिशिरः शर्वरीकरः ॥ ९७॥
>
> अक्रूरः पेशलो दक्षो दक्षिणः क्षमिणांवरः ।
>
> विद्वत्तमो वीतभयः पुण्यश्रवणकीर्तनः ॥ ९८॥
>
> उत्तारणो दुष्कृतिहा पुण्यो दुःस्वप्ननाशनः ।
>
> वीरहा रक्षणः सन्तो जीवनः पर्यवस्थितः ॥ ९९॥
>
> अनन्तरूपोऽनन्तश्रीर्जितमन्युर्भयापहः ।
>
> चतुरश्रो गभीरात्मा विदिशो व्यादिशो दिशः ॥ १००॥
>
> अनादिर्भूर्भुवो लक्ष्मीः सुवीरो रुचिराङ्गदः ।
>
> जननो जनजन्मादिर्भीमो भीमपराक्रमः ॥ १०१॥
>
> आधारनिलयोऽधाता पुष्पहासः प्रजागरः ।
>
> ऊर्ध्वगः सत्पथाचारः प्राणदः प्रणवः पणः ॥ १०२॥
>
> प्रमाणं प्राणनिलयः प्राणभृत्प्राणजीवनः ।
>
> तत्त्वं तत्त्वविदेकात्मा जन्ममृत्युजरातिगः ॥ १०३॥
>
> भूर्भुवःस्वस्तरुस्तारः सविता प्रपितामहः ।
>
> यज्ञो यज्ञपतिर्यज्वा यज्ञाङ्गो यज्ञवाहनः ॥ १०४॥
>
> यज्ञभृद् यज्ञकृद् यज्ञी यज्ञभुग् यज्ञसाधनः ।
>
> यज्ञान्तकृद् यज्ञगुह्यमन्नमन्नाद एव च ॥ १०५॥
>
> आत्मयोनिः स्वयञ्जातो वैखानः सामगायनः ।
>
> देवकीनन्दनः स्रष्टा क्षितीशः पापनाशनः ॥ १०६॥
>
> शङ्खभृन्नन्दकी चक्री शार्ङ्गधन्वा गदाधरः ।
>
> रथाङ्गपाणिरक्षोभ्यः सर्वप्रहरणायुधः ॥ १०७॥
>
> सर्वप्रहरणायुध ॐ नम इति ।
>
> वनमाली गदी शार्ङ्गी शङ्खी चक्री च नन्दकी ।
>
> श्रीमान् नारायणो विष्णुर्वासुदेवोऽभिरक्षतु ॥ १०८॥
>
> श्रीवासुदेवोऽभिरक्षतु ॐ नम इति ।
>

>
> भीष्म उवाच ---
>

>
> नाम्नां सहस्रं दिव्यानामशेषेण प्रकीर्तितम् ॥ १॥
>
> य इदं श‍ृणुयान्नित्यं यश्चापि परिकीर्तयेत् ।
>
> नाशुभं प्राप्नुयात्किञ्चित्सोऽमुत्रेह च मानवः ॥ २॥
>
> वेदान्तगो ब्राह्मणः स्यात्क्षत्रियो विजयी भवेत् ।
>
> वैश्यो धनसमृद्धः स्याच्छूद्रः सुखमवाप्नुयात् ॥ ३॥
>
> धर्मार्थी प्राप्नुयाद्धर्ममर्थार्थी चार्थमाप्नुयात् ।
>
> कामानवाप्नुयात्कामी प्रजार्थी प्राप्नुयात्प्रजाम् ॥ ४॥
>
> भक्तिमान् यः सदोत्थाय शुचिस्तद्गतमानसः ।
>
> सहस्रं वासुदेवस्य नाम्नामेतत्प्रकीर्तयेत् ॥ ५॥
>
> यशः प्राप्नोति विपुलं ज्ञातिप्राधान्यमेव च ।
>
> अचलां श्रियमाप्नोति श्रेयः प्राप्नोत्यनुत्तमम् ॥ ६॥
>
> न भयं क्वचिदाप्नोति वीर्यं तेजश्च विन्दति ।
>
> भवत्यरोगो द्युतिमान्बलरूपगुणान्वितः ॥ ७॥
>
> रोगार्तो मुच्यते रोगाद्बद्धो मुच्येत बन्धनात् ।
>
> भयान्मुच्येत भीतस्तु मुच्येतापन्न आपदः ॥ ८॥
>
> दुर्गाण्यतितरत्याशु पुरुषः पुरुषोत्तमम् ।
>
> स्तुवन्नामसहस्रेण नित्यं भक्तिसमन्वितः ॥ ९॥
>
> वासुदेवाश्रयो मर्त्यो वासुदेवपरायणः ।
>
> सर्वपापविशुद्धात्मा याति ब्रह्म सनातनम् ॥ १०॥
>
> न वासुदेवभक्तानामशुभं विद्यते क्वचित् ।
>
> जन्ममृत्युजराव्याधिभयं नैवोपजायते ॥ ११॥
>
> इमं स्तवमधीयानः श्रद्धाभक्तिसमन्वितः ।
>
> युज्येतात्मसुखक्षान्तिश्रीधृतिस्मृतिकीर्तिभिः ॥ १२॥
>
> न क्रोधो न च मात्सर्यं न लोभो नाशुभा मतिः ।
>
> भवन्ति कृतपुण्यानां भक्तानां पुरुषोत्तमे ॥ १३॥
>
> द्यौः सचन्द्रार्कनक्षत्रा खं दिशो भूर्महोदधिः ।
>
> वासुदेवस्य वीर्येण विधृतानि महात्मनः ॥ १४॥
>
> ससुरासुरगन्धर्वं सयक्षोरगराक्षसम् ।
>
> जगद्वशे वर्ततेदं कृष्णस्य सचराचरम् ॥ १५॥
>
> इन्द्रियाणि मनो बुद्धिः सत्त्वं तेजो बलं धृतिः ।
>
> वासुदेवात्मकान्याहुः क्षेत्रं क्षेत्रज्ञ एव च ॥ १६॥
>
> सर्वागमानामाचारः प्रथमं परिकल्प्यते । (परिकल्पते, परिकल्पितः)
>
> आचारप्रभवो धर्मो धर्मस्य प्रभुरच्युतः ॥ १७॥
>
> ऋषयः पितरो देवा महाभूतानि धातवः ।
>
> जङ्गमाजङ्गमं चेदं जगन्नारायणोद्भवम् ॥ १८॥
>
> योगो ज्ञानं तथा साङ्ख्यं विद्याः शिल्पादिकर्म च ।
>
> वेदाः शास्त्राणि विज्ञानमेतत्सर्वं जनार्दनात् ॥ १९॥
>
> एको विष्णुर्महद्भूतं पृथग्भूतान्यनेकशः ।
>
> त्रींल्लोकान्व्याप्य भूतात्मा भुङ्क्ते विश्वभुगव्ययः ॥ २०॥
>
> इमं स्तवं भगवतो विष्णोर्व्यासेन कीर्तितम् ।
>
> पठेद्य इच्छेत्पुरुषः श्रेयः प्राप्तुं सुखानि च ॥ २१॥
>
> विश्वेश्वरमजं देवं जगतः प्रभुमव्ययम् ।
>
> भजन्ति ये पुष्कराक्षं न ते यान्ति पराभवम् ॥ २२॥
>
> न ते यान्ति पराभवम् ॐ नम इति ।
>
> अर्जुन उवाच ---
>
> पद्मपत्रविशालाक्ष पद्मनाभ सुरोत्तम ।
>
> भक्तानामनुरक्तानां त्राता भव जनार्दन ॥ २३॥
>
> श्रीभगवानुवाच ---
>
> यो मां नामसहस्रेण स्तोतुमिच्छति पाण्डव ।
>
> सोहऽमेकेन श्लोकेन स्तुत एव न संशयः ॥ २४॥
>
> स्तुत एव न संशय ॐ नम इति ।
>
> व्यास उवाच ---
>
> वासनाद्वासुदेवस्य वासितं भुवनत्रयम् ।
>
> सर्वभूतनिवासोऽसि वासुदेव नमोऽस्तु ते ॥ २५॥
>
> श्रीवासुदेव नमोऽस्तु त ॐ नम इति ।
>
> पार्वत्युवाच ---
>
> केनोपायेन लघुना विष्णोर्नामसहस्रकम् ।
>
> पठ्यते पण्डितैर्नित्यं श्रोतुमिच्छाम्यहं प्रभो ॥ २६॥
>
> ईश्वर उवाच ---
>
> श्रीरामरामरामेति रमे रामे मनोरमे । (रामरामेति रामेति)
>
> सहस्रनामतत्तुल्यं रामनाम वरानने ॥ २७॥
>
> श्रीरामनाम वरानन ॐ नम इति ।
>
> ब्रह्मोवाच ---
>
> नमोऽस्त्वनन्ताय सहस्रमूर्तये
>
> सहस्रपादाक्षिशिरोरुबाहवे ।
>
> सहस्रनाम्ने पुरुषाय शाश्वते
>
> सहस्रकोटियुगधारिणे नमः ॥ २८॥
>
> सहस्रकोटियुगधारिणे नम ॐ नम इति ।
>
> ॐ तत्सदिति श्रीमहाभारते शतसाहस्र्यां संहितायां वैयासिक्यामानुशासनिके
>
> पर्वणि भीष्मयुधिष्ठिरसंवादे श्रीविष्णोर्दिव्यसहस्रनामस्तोत्रम् ॥
>
> सञ्जय उवाच ---
>
> यत्र योगेश्वरः कृष्णो यत्र पार्थो धनुर्धरः ।
>
> तत्र श्रीर्विजयो भूतिर्ध्रुवा नीतिर्मतिर्मम ॥ २९॥
>
> श्रीभगवानुवाच ---
>
> अनन्याश्चिन्तयन्तो मां ये जनाः पर्युपासते ।
>
> तेषां नित्याभियुक्तानां योगक्षेमं वहाम्यहम् ॥ ३०॥
>
> परित्राणाय साधूनां विनाशाय च दुष्कृताम् ।
>
> धर्मसंस्थापनार्थाय सम्भवामि युगे युगे ॥ ३१॥
>
> आर्ता विषण्णाः शिथिलाश्च भीता घोरेषु च व्याधिषु वर्तमानाः ।
>
> सङ्कीर्त्य नारायणशब्दमात्रं विमुक्तदुःखाः सुखिनो भवन्ति ॥ ३२॥ (भवन्तु)
>
> कायेन वाचा मनसेन्द्रियैर्वा बुद्ध्यात्मना वा प्रकृतेः स्वभावात् । (प्रकृतिस्वभावात् ।)
>
> करोमि यद्यत् सकलं परस्मै नारायणायेति समर्पयामि ॥ ३३॥
