# Arabia on the Eve of Islam



To understand the rise of Islam, it is necessary to possess an understanding of the state of the wider Middle East and in particular the Arabian peninsula in late antiquity (the period from the fourth to the seventh century) that would enable or at least render possible, the formation and spread of Islam.

## Geopolitical Landscape



Here, we have to divide the region into some types depending on the geography and the types of polity enabled by it.

#### Two Grand Empires



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qzUm!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc0b6c292-ca07-49c2-a11b-e9f87d074e9b_1368x674.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qzUm!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc0b6c292-ca07-49c2-a11b-e9f87d074e9b_1368x674.png)





To the north of the Arabian peninsula, two great and vast empires dominated the rest of the Middle East. These were the **Byzantine** (Eastern Roman) Empire to the West and the **Sassanid** (Persian) empire to the east, which were large states with a world of teeming cities and a taxed peasantry under the control of a powerful imperial apparatus. This two-empire configuration that remained stable for centuries despite frequent skirmishes, came under a serious tension and eventual exhaustion in the beginning of the seventh century (602 - 628 CE) because of an exceptionally long series of drawn out wars that strained both the empires to a dangerous level. This was the ripe time for any other emerging third power to rise up in the region and expand easily by defeating these two tired empires.

#### Northern Nomads of the Steppes



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CxpF!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2a71f790-2755-45ba-bcc0-3d8c8e203566_2458x1622.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CxpF!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2a71f790-2755-45ba-bcc0-3d8c8e203566_2458x1622.png)





Farther north, lived the nomadic people of the Steppe grasslands. These nomadic tribes had a good and long track record of threatening sedentary empires throughout Asia and Europe (remember the **Scythians**, **Xiongnu**, **Huns**, and **Avars**?), and would also threaten future empires (remember the **Magyars**, **Turks**, **Mongols**, and **Timurids \[**also known as **Mughals\]**?). One would then expect intuitively that, if at all a third power that threatened the Byzantine and Sassanid empires would rise up in the seventh century, it would be some nomads from the Steppes (based on what happened before and what would happen after). Nobody (except historians who have the gift of hindsight) could have predicted that the Arabs would come to have the honour of diminishing these two empires to establish a world empire of their own, something that they never did before, and something that they would never do anytime after.

#### Arabian Interior



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mHpT!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe0fa5861-9003-45a6-9ff0-65392fbd057f_2000x2000.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mHpT!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe0fa5861-9003-45a6-9ff0-65392fbd057f_2000x2000.jpeg)





In much of the interior of the Arabian peninsula and also its western side, known as the **Ḥijāz** region (where Islam would originate and where the two most sacred sites of Islam are), by the time of the sixth century, we see a stateless, heavily tribal society. This will be evident when we later discuss in detail about the nature of identity, culture, and the style of warfare between the tribes. These tribes could be further subdivided into two groups based on their lifestyle, which in turn was shaped by geography. The first group were the nomadic pastoralists, called in Arabic as **Baddū** (Anglicised as **Bedouin**) who roamed around in the deserts and grazed camel and sheep. The second group were the **sedentary** peoples, called in Arabic as the **Mustaqraʾ**, who lived in fixed settlements as farmers around the oases (like at Medina), or as traders in the coastal areas with trade hubs (like at Mecca), or sometimes engaged in other economic activities. Arab nomads and sedentary peoples did not live in isolation from each other. The nomads reinforced tribal identity while the sedentary dwellers instilled a sense of a common Arab identity. Both of them maintained continuous interaction (extensive trade) with each other, which led to this common culture despite the heavy tribalism. This contrasts with many other parts of the world, where nomads roamed around in almost self-sufficient groups, only sparsely interacting with settled societies, if not through conquest. This interaction is also one of the reasons why the history of the Middle East looks different from that of contemporary Europe, which lacked this extensive nomad–settler interaction.

One of the most conspicuous things evident in any survey of pre-Islamic interior Arabia is the absence of any state. We do hear of some kings who emerged and ruled parts of the region sporadically but they appear very insignificant and weak by even the contemporary standards of the outside world. For example, we hear about a tradition of kings in the tribe of **Sulaym** in the Ḥijāz region. One of those about-to-be kings was slapped by his cousin in the face when he was about to assume the title, following which he left the tribe in humiliation. Another one of those kings, we hear, got deposed after losing popular support, because he acted against the wishes of the tribesmen in some matter. In **Yathrib** (which was to later become Medina), we hear of a tribal leader who unsuccessfully aspired to become king and also hear of another king who went on a strike by sitting in the sun and not entering his house till a score he had with another person was settled. These stories tell us how weak the kings were in the interior of the Arabian peninsula when we contrast them with the tales of grandeur and power about kings of other nations. The tribes of Arabia not only lacked kings but were resentful of them. A tribe would set a high value on being **laqāḥ**, which in Arabic, means that it had never given any allegiance or tribute to a king. Another story goes that when a member of the tribe of **Quraysh** (to which Muhammad belonged) sought to become king in Mecca, his fellow tribesmen prevented it because the tribe was **laqāḥ**, and hence neither wields royal power nor is subjected to it. There is another story we hear about a pre-Islamic poet who had killed a king of Ḥīra when he asked a favour to the poet’s mother to just pass a plate to his own mother. A yet another story recounts a tribe responding to the arrival of an aspiring tax collector sent by the king of Ḥīra by throwing him into a well.

This absence of states is not hard to understand given the geography of the region. In pre-modern times, a state was an entity that collected taxes from its subjects in exchange of providing them security which encouraged economic activity, that would generate more wealth, a part of which would fund these taxes. Such a project is possible only in a region where a decent amount of tax can be collected without difficulty. Egypt is an example of a tax-collector’s paradise where a rich agricultural land was cultivated by peasants along the strip surrounding a river (Nile) which also provided the means of transport for the agents of the state to access the subjects for taxation. On the contrary, the riverless desert of the Arabian interior was a tax collector’s hell because it was a harsh wilderness where agricultural land was scarce and widely dispersed among the oases while the rest of the landscape contained nomads in the desert, who kept moving from place to place, making them difficult to access for taxation by any agents of a large state. The trade and agriculture that did happen in the interior of pre-Islamic Arabia did not generate sufficient wealth for state-building.

#### Kingdoms in Northern and Southern Arabia



It is to be noted that parts of southern and northern Arabia did have a number of large states by the 1^(st) century CE and boasted sophisticated civilisations of ancient antiquity. We hear of some famous ones like the **Nabāṭū** (**Nabataean) Kingdom** in the north (annexed by the Romans in 106 CE), and the South Arabian kingdoms like **Sabaʾ (**the kingdom of **Sheba** from the Bible ; remember that a queen of Sheba visited King Solomon), **Qatabān**, **Ḥaḍramaut**, and the **Ḥimyarite** Kingdom.

The Nabataean kingdom of Arabia based on the North at its greatest extent

How were these states sustained then? The first main ingredient that sustained these states was the trade of **frankincense** and **myrrh** (used to make something like the *agarbatti*). These were aromatics that were grown and harvested in Southern Arabia that were highly prized and coveted in the Roman, Mediterranean, and Near Eastern worlds for use in religious rituals, festivals, funerals, and medicines. The next thing that sustained these kingdoms was due to the fact that coastal Arabia lay at the transition of the ancient trade route by the sea that linked the Mediterranean world with India which exported its spices and other luxury goods to the Roman empire. These Arabian kingdoms managed to monopolise this **transit spice trade**, receiving goods via ships from India arriving in the southern part of Arabia, and transferring them to the Levant by carrying them north through overland caravans. The Nabataean Kingdom managed this treacherous land-based caravan routes. They provided security, camels, water supply, and other infrastructure for the transfer of Indian goods and the southern Arabian frankincense goods, moving them from the south to the north, after which they would be carried up to the Roman ports in the Levant.

But in late antiquity, two key changes happened that would collapse both of these trades that sustained these kingdoms. One big change that happened very early on in the closing of the BCE period itself was that, Arabia lost its monopoly as a transit for the spice trade between India and the Mediterranean. In the late Hellenistic and early Roman periods, navigators learnt to use the seasonal monsoon winds to sail directly from the Arabian sea to the Red Sea in Egypt which was part of the Greek and Roman Empires. Instead of buying spices from Arab merchants who transported them slowly over a thousand miles of desert, Roman merchants sailed directly to India from the Red Sea, bypassing the Arabian Peninsula entirely, and thus eliminating the Arab kingdoms’ middleman profits. The second catastrophic change that happened in late antiquity was that, the demand for incense collapsed as the Mediterranean world ruled by the Roman Empire transitioned from paganism to Christianity. The Church in late antiquity associated the strongly sensual aroma of the incense with paganism as they were widely used right from household rituals to the large state sponsored pagan festivities and sacrifices. In the end of the 4th century, the Roman Emperor Theodosius I, who declared Christianity to be the only tolerated religion of the empire, issued decrees that closed all pagan temples, outlawed public sacrifices, and explicitly **banned the burning of incense** at all occasions. Hence, the single largest institutional consumer of South Arabian frankincense (the Roman empire with its thousands of pagan temples and millions of pagan households across the Mediterranean) was eliminated in late antiquity.

Note that the Catholic and the Orthodox Churches did eventually reintroduce incense (and other pagan practices like dancing and processions) into their liturgy and festivals, long after their pagan associations were forgotten and paganism became a distant memory. But, this was not until centuries later, and was only at a fraction of the quantity used by the pagan Romans.

When the Roman market for Arabian incense and transport of Indian goods vanished, these Arabian kingdoms lost their primary source of external revenue which sustained them. Bereft of this capital, they could no longer afford to maintain the complex and expensive infrastructure that kept their societies alive. Most notably, in the south, the great **Ma’rib Dam** (an engineering marvel of its time in the kingdom of **Sheba** which was first built in 8th century BCE), which required constant, costly maintenance to redirect flash floods into agricultural water networks, fell into disrepair. The lack of funds directly contributed to the dam’s final, catastrophic breach around 570 CE (around the time of Muhammad’s birth), triggering mass famine and hence resulting in migration of a lot of sedentary peoples and transitioned them to become Bedouin nomads. The Nabataeans in the north too, with their profits (from transporting incense and Indian goods from the south gone), were forced to transition to subsistence farming and their political autonomy was eroded, and their legendary cities were eventually abandoned to the desert sands.

Also, by the end of the 6th century, the relentless and nearly apocalyptic wars between the Eastern Roman (**Byzantine**) and **Sassanid** (Persian) Empires turned the traditional northern borders of Arabia into a war-zone. The northern caravan routes not only became less profitable but also far too dangerous for merchants. Thus, any remaining trade lines were fractured, and the global commerce now shifted entirely to maritime routes controlled by either the Byzantines in the Red Sea or the Sassanians in the Persian Gulf. Hence, between the decline of the Arabian frankincense trade and transit Indian spice trade in the fourth century and the rise of the Yemeni coffee trade in the seventeenth century, no part of Arabia could grow a cash crop valuable enough to be of serious interest to the outside world.

#### Two Client Kingdoms



There were two other Arab client kingdoms in the north which acted as buffer states between the Arabians and the two dominating empires in late antiquity, which were

1.  the kingdom of **Lakhmids** (also known as **al-Manādhirah**) which
    acted as a client state for the Sassanid Persian Empire and

2.  the Arab Christian kingdom of **Ghassānids** (also known as
    **Jafnids**) which acted as a client state for the Byzantine Empire

The kingdoms of Arabia around 600 CE

Two religious tensions however upset the status of these kingdoms and their relationship to their two respective patron empires. An earlier heretical form of Christianity called **Nestorian Christianity** was gradually spreading and becoming popular among the populace of the Lakhmids (interesting fact : all the brands of Christianity which were declared heretical by the Roman state diffused outside its borders and evangelised to the non-Roman tribes and kingdoms). The Lakhmid kingdom was home to a distinct class of literate, Arabic-speaking Christians known as the *‘***Ibād** (meaning : devotees in Arabic), who built numerous churches and monasteries. But, the Lakhmid rulers hesitated to convert to Christianity despite ruling over a largely Nestorian Christian population, because it would upset their patron Sassanid empire, that viewed Christianity as the religion of its bitter enemies (the Romans). There is a small nuance to note here. The Sassanid monarchs hated Christianity initially and persecuted all Christians intensely within their Persian empire, because they were following a religion of their bitter enemies (the Romans / Byzantines). They hence suspected Christians within their borders to act as spies or agents for Rome. But, the Persian empire eventually came to tolerate the Nestorian Christians as subjects under their control. This was because, as the Byzantines persecuted Nestorians as heretics, the Sassanids came to realise that Nestorian Christians inside Persia would not act as a Byzantine fifth column within their Persian empire, and would be fully loyal to the Shahenshah of Persia. Nestorian Christians inside the Sassanid Empire organised themselves independently of the Roman Byzantine Empire, under the Church of the East. The Sassanid **Shahanshah** (King of Kings) also heavily regulated the Nestorian Church. The King held the power to veto the election of the Nestorian Patriarch (the head of the Church, equivalent to their Pope). Because they were deeply embedded inside Persian territory and had no military power of their own, the Nestorian Christians posed no geopolitical threat as long as they were subjects strictly under their control. Hence, while the Persian empire tolerated the Nestorian Christians as subjects under their tight control, still they could not tolerate them becoming politically powerful and establishing a new independent Christian kingdom in the neighbourhood. But, following a temporary truce between the Byzantines and the Sassanids in 591 CE, the ruler of the Arab Lakhmid kingdom, wrongly got emboldened to convert to Nestorian Christianity. Predictably, this severely enraged the then Sassanid king **Khusrow II**, because he saw this conversion of the Arab Lakhmid king to Nestorian Christianity as setting a dangerous precedent for an eventual possibility of a pan-Arab Christian alliance, especially with the more immediate possibility of a reconciliation with the rival Arab Christian kingdom of Ghassānids (next paragraph) who were under Byzantine control. The Persian king then conquered the Arab kingdom, executed the ruler, and ended the Lakhmid kingdom by assuming direct control of it in 602 CE. Khusrow II thus destroyed the vital Arab buffer state that had protected Persia’s southwestern frontier from raiding nomadic tribes from the interior of the Arabia, thus leaving his empire exposed to future raids from the Bedouins.

Next, the **Ghassānids** were practicing a Miaphysite form of Christianity which was also considered heretical by the Byzantine state. The Ghassānid king, **al-Hārith ibn Jabalah**, used his immense political leverage with the Byzantine Emperor **Justinian I** to secure the ordination of **Jacob Baradaeus** as a bishop. But, Jacob went on to clandestinely organise a completely independent Miaphysite church hierarchy across the Middle East (which became the **Syriac Orthodox Church**, the parent of the **Jacobite Christian Church** of Kerala today, named after this Jacob Baradaeus). The Byzantines viewed this as an underground heretical movement that was also subverting the empire’s authority. The Byzantine government hence periodically launched brutal crackdowns and persecutions against the Miaphysites in Syria and Egypt. Because the Ghassānids saw themselves as the protectors of all Miaphysite Christians in the region, Byzantine religious policy deeply angered and alienated this Arab kingdom which was eventually dissolved by the Byzantines into a series of 15 smaller, localised tribal districts, each led by its own minor chieftain. Stripped of centralised command and funding from Constantinople, these fragmented tribes could no longer effectively police the desert, thus making their frontier wide open to Bedouin raids. During the devastating Byzantine-Sasanian wars of 602–628 CE, the Sasanian Persians overran the Levant. Facing annihilation, the Byzantines under the Emperor **Heraclius** scrambled to restore the Ghassānid monarchy under a final king, **Jabalah ibn al-Ayham**, hoping to resurrect their formidable Arab buffer force. But, this newly resurrected Ghassānid kingdom would be a very weak and vulnerable one as we will see and would easily collapse to the Islamic conquests.

Hence, we see that all the states of northern and southern Arabia were either fallen to the large empires, or were much weaker and more vulnerable than ever. The **Aksumite** kingdom (based in Ethiopia), the **Sassanid** Empire of Persia, and the **Byzantine** Empire - all three were ravaging whatever was left of these kingdoms, before the latter two themselves went into an intense and prolonged war between themselves. With the traditional northern and southern states at the outer rims of Arabia more weakened than ever, the political and economic centre of gravity of the Arabian peninsula toward the independent, untouched tribal trade hubs of the Ḥijāz region (most notably **Mecca**), thus creating a huge power vacuum, and setting the stage for the rise of Islam.

#### Mecca in the time of Muhammad



The collapse of the kingdoms in the south, alongside the dismantling of the **Ghassānid** and **Lakhmid** buffer states in the north, created a massive geopolitical vacuum. Mecca’s leading tribe, the **Quraysh**, used a brilliant mix of religious positioning, trade diplomacy, and logistical innovation to exploit this situation to develop the city into a decent regional trading hub based in the Ḥijāz.

Previously, large kingdoms protected trade caravans moving through their territories. Once kingdoms collapsed or got weakened, the desert became a lawless zone controlled by fragmented, warring nomadic tribes. Mecca’s leadership stepped in and engineered the **‘ilāf (security treaties)**. The older kingdoms relied on physical control of trade routes, military fortifications, and geographic monopolies (like the growing regions of frankincense). Mecca, by contrast, had no natural resources, no agricultural land, and no local luxury crops. Its economy was built entirely around the **Kaʿbah**, which served as a pan-Arab polytheistic sanctuary. The ruling tribe of Mecca, the **Quraysh**, established a sacred zone, called the **ḥarām** (which means forbidden in Arabic), around the city where tribal warfare was strictly forbidden. Mecca functioned as a regional “free trade zone” where blood feuds were paused, allowing nomadic tribes to safely come together to trade, settle debts, and worship. Tribes came there not just to worship the pagan idols housed at the **kaʿbah**, but to resolve blood feuds and trade goods while Mecca took a lucrative tax cut from it all. By the dawn of the 7th century, the declining regional kingdoms had left Meccan trade as the primary binding force in Arabian economy and religious life. The wealth, financial networks, and tribal alliances forged during this economic boom which was also directly to pagan religious pilgrimage served as the matrix into which Islam would evolve and expand.

## Arabian Tribal Culture



Now, let us talk in more detail about the tribal culture of pre-Islamic Arabia. Instead of giving a bunch of raw facts, let me illustrate them with the help of three interesting stories. In the later part of the sixth century (around the time Muhammad was born), the Ḥijāz region was the scene of four wars concentrated around the city of Mecca, known in later times, as the **Fijār wars** (the word means depravity in Arabic). The reason for such a name is that they took place during the four sacred months (**muḥarram**) in the Arabian calendar when the Arab tribes were forbidden to fight. A ninth century Arab scholar **Ibn Ḥabīb** (died 860 CE) is one of the main sources as to what happened during these wars and although he was writing more than two centuries after the incident in Baghdad, he gives a chain of transmission from a resident of Medina who died in 768 CE and belonged to the same clan of the Quraysh which was involved in the conflicts. Even if the episodes narrated are not reliable, this is still a good overall picture of what type of tribal society was prevalent in Arabia during the times of Muhammad. We will concentrate on the third Fijār war as an example.

An Arab woman from a nomadic tribe came to **ʿUkāẓ** (around Mecca) for shopping in an annual fair. The woman is not named but described to have belonged to the clan of **Banū ʿĀmir ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿa** (meaning : sons of ʿĀmir who was the son of Ṣaʿṣaʿa), who in turn were a part of a larger tribal group descended from **ʿIkrima ibn Khaṣafa ibn Qays** (hereafter simply called the tribe of **Qays**). The woman was wearing a veil that covered all parts of her body except her eyes. We hear that some young men suggested her to remove the veil which she ignored. When the woman sat down to negotiate, one of these young men pulled a prank on her by pinning the end of her dress to her back. This young man is named and described as as **Abū ʾl-Ghashim ibn ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā ibn ʿĀmir ibn al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥāritha ibn Saʿd ibn Taym ibn Murra** (in Arabic, “**ibn**” means “*son of*”, and hence this list is basically listing the name **Abū ʾl-Ghashim** and his male ancestors in succession) who were a part of the larger tribe of **Quraysh**. You do not need to remember any of the long names except that the woman was from the tribe of **Qays** and the young man was from the tribe of **Quraysh**. This prank was successful because as the woman got up, her lower body was stripped naked and exposed to everyone around her. The young men then taunted her by saying that she hesitated to show her face to them but now everyone could see her buttocks. She then unveiled her face (which symbolises an appeal for help) and then shouted “*Help, O **Qays**! Look at what they have done to me*!”. Members of her tribe of Qays gathered around her and so did the members of the tribe of the young man which was Quraysh. Ibn Habīb tells us that there was a fight between the two sides but it was not serious and the opponents soon separated. Other sources describe that this fight did lead to injuries following which a prominent member of the Quraysh took responsibility to pay the requisite blood money and settling the matter. We can argue on whether this story is historical or not but that is not the concern here. We are more interested in this story for what it tells us about the social and cultural milieu of pre-Islamic Arabia.

The long list of male ancestors mentioned with the “ibn” chains clearly tells us that a form of tribalism based on patrilineal descent was prevalent on the Arabian population. Each of the two concerned parties in the fight (both the young man and the woman) is given an identity by listing a string of their male ancestors. In the case of the woman specifically, her own name is not even mentioned and only her tribal ancestry is given. Why give a string of male ancestors instead of simply mentioning that the woman was from the tribe of Qays and the man was from the tribe of Quraysh? In this particular case, it does not matter as the parties involved in the conflict (the woman and the young man) belong to different tribes. But if the woman had gotten sexually harassed and humiliated by another man from her own tribe of Qays, she would not issue a general call to the Qays as a whole. She would then call out the name of an intermediate ancestor that was different between her and the man who wronged her. Indeed, in a variant version of this story in another source, we find that the woman calls out the name of an intermediate ancestor **Hawāzin** who was a grandson of ʿIkrima and great-grandfather of Ṣaʿṣaʿa. In another conflict, both the Qays and the Quraysh tribe might find themselves pitted on the same side (as both Qays and the Quraysh share a more distant ancestor named **Muḍar**) against another tribe that did not share this ancestor. So, depending on the identity of the parties involved in the conflict, the operative pool of people available to help on both sides may be large or small.

Another important relationship between the tribes apart from patrilineal descent was the formal agreement of protection which fell into three broad categories. One such relationship was wherein one tribe or clan would become an **ally** (**ḥalīf**) of another, which involved mutual defence and support, while retaining a degree of autonomy. Another relationship was when an individual became a **client** (**mawlā**, pl. **mawālī**) of a tribe. This was more of a patron-client relationship adopted by outsiders or outcastes of other tribes or freed slaves or anyone else who fell outside the tribal lineage or did not know their tribal lineage. Such people would enter into a legal pact of protection (**mawlā al-‘aqd**) as a subservient client with an Arab tribe because Arab tribal society recognised no legal standing of an individual who did not possess a tribal identity based on blood lineage. In this case, the individual who was a client had to keep up with the rules of his tribe (who would take responsibility for his actions) and would risk banishment from the tribe if he violated the conditions. The last kind of formal relationship was that of **protected neighbour** or for temporary protection (**jār**) where an individual or a group of people from another tribe who entered within the premises of the tribe’s camp or territory were granted physical security and protection under tribal customary law as long as they abided by their end of the deal.

Many elements of the Arabic tribal structure persisted even long after the Arabs became Muslim. During the first (and relatively short-lived) **Rāshidun Caliphate** and the second **Umayyad** **Caliphate,** Islamic rule rapidly expanded to encompass the entirety of Middle East and North Africa. One important example of the persistence of tribalism in these two caliphates is the persistence of the institution of clientage. It was explained in the previous paragraph that clientage was the mechanism by which banished individuals and those people outside the Arab tribal identity sought protection by becoming clients (**mawālī**) under an Arab tribe.

During the first two caliphates of Islam, non-Arab converts to Islam had to become clients (**mawālī**) under the protection of an Arab tribe, which was introduced above. That is, in order to be a Muslim, one had to either be an Arab, or a client subject under an Arab tribe, with hence the Arab always being in the higher position of the hierarchy in Islam. The early administrative apparatus of the Umayyad caliphate mapped state hand-outs and legal identities directly onto tribal registers, which was essentially the same Arabian system in pre-Islamic times for booty distribution.

Many times, non-Arab converts in these caliphates were also demanded to continue paying the **jizyah** tax (technically to be levied only for non-Muslims). Non-Arab converts to Islam could neither enter the political bureaucracy nor the religious bureaucracy of the state. Also, non-Arab converts either prayed in their own local mosques or could be led by an Arab in prayer, but a non-Arab could not lead the prayer in any congregation with Arabs at mosques.

All of these enraged the non-Arab converts (especially Persians), eventually leading to the overthrow of the Umayyads by the **‘Abbāsids** who established the next caliphate (also Arab, but a more non-Arab friendly and a more egalitarian caliphate). As Abbāsid political power declined, this eventually led to the Persianisation of the entire Islamic world and the religion itself. Thus, the absorption of Persian administrative and cultural traditions was the one that transformed Islam from an Arab tribal empire into a multi-ethnic global polity and religion. To know more in detail about this Persianisation of Islam, read this article below.

A description of this tribal kinship can be found mentioned in **Herodotus** (Greek historian in 5th century BCE!) who writes in his histories about the Arabs as :

No nation regards the sanctity of a pledge more seriously than the Arabs. When two men wish to make a solemn compact, they get the service of a third, who stands between them and with a sharp stone cuts the palms of their hands near the base of the thumb; then he takes a little tuft of wool from their clothes, dips it in the blood and smears the blood on seven stones which lie between them, invoking, as he does so, the names of Dionysus and Urania; then the person who is giving the pledge commends the stranger—or fellow-citizen, as the case may be—to his friends, who in their turn consider themselves equally bound to honour it.

By the time period of our interest though, this ritual seems to have been forgotten and we hear of other rituals for taking oath but the basic social building block described by Herodotus serves correctly still. In the absence of a strong state, it is this tribal institutions of kinship and customary oath that enables a tribal society to function. Contrast this with the situation in modern states. Today, if a woman is sexually harassed, she might call upon her immediate family members and friends, but they are not numerous, and they would not be of much help if they are not situated immediately around her. A today’s woman has much narrower options to call for help in terms of her kinship alone, as no one today maintains relations with relatives more distant than cousins, let alone with people sharing more distant ancestors. But, in terms of institutions outside kinship, the options available for a woman who is sexually harassed today are much more - the police, the court, campus authorities, sexual harassment helplines in her workplace or study place, and so on, which are possible only due to the existence of a state. So, we clearly see how the tribal structure substitutes for the state to widen the pool of help available for an individual in the absence of states.

How did justice work in this tribal society? Let us illustrate this with another story. We hear of this incident mentioned by **Balādhurī** (died 892 CE) in his work ***Ansāb al-ashrāf*** (Genealogies of the Nobles).

**Murattiʿ** … married a woman of **Ḥaḍramawt**; her father imposed on him the condition that he take no further wife in addition to her and that she give birth only in the abode of her own people. But Murattiʿ did not observe the condition. So they made **Afʿā ibn al-Ḥuṣayn** the **Jurhumite** (as) their judge … the Arabs being in the habit of having recourse to him in their disputes. At the hearing, the fact that the condition had been imposed, was established, and Af ʿā gave the verdict “The condition is binding.” He was the first to use this maxim. So the Ḥaḍramīs took the woman and the son she had had by Murattiʿ..

Here, we see a sort of judicial system but the judge is not appointed by a ruler or a state or any institution that gives him the authority to try cases under a certain jurisdiction. Instead, the judge is chosen by the mutual consent of the concerned parties and the authority of the judge is limited to this particular case. In the above story, **Afʿā** looks like he has a recurring role to play as judge only because the Arabs in his area made a practice of submitting cases to him, because he had established a wide reputation for himself as a fair judge. If the parties could not agree on a common judge or could not agree on even taking their dispute to a judge, it was likely to be resolved only by violence. What was the nature of tribal warfare in Arab society and how were battles resolved? This is again illustrated best through another story, this time of the fourth Fijār where a murder indeed happened.

The King of Ḥīra wanted someone to secure his caravan and had two candidates in mind for the job. One was a man named **Barrāḍ**, who was disowned by his own tribe for bad behaviour, following which he moved to Mecca and obtained the protection of a leading member of the Quraysh tribe by becoming his client. Because he still continued to be a menace, his Qurayshi patron wanted to end the relationship and disown him, but out of compassion for the poor guy, decided to not end it as long as he promised to leave Mecca, and not trouble the Meccans. So, he went to Ḥīra to serve as an escort for the king’s caravan. But instead, the king (having concerns about the guy’s bad reputation) gave the job to another member named **ʿUrwa**, who was from the tribe of Qays in the same clan that the woman who has harassed in the third Fijār belonged to. Barrāḍ then followed the caravan and killed his rival ʿUrwa and went off with the caravan goods. He did however have the courtesy to inform his Qurayshi patron of this act. The problem was that since this guy was under the clientage of the Quraysh tribe, the members of the Qays tribe would want revenge for the murder of their own tribesman. A simple act of revenge killing would be that the Qays kill Barrāḍ, the murderer of their ʿUrwa. and settle the matter. But, this was not enough because Barrāḍ was not a full member of the Quraysh but was just a client. They could not kill a member of the tribe from which Barrāḍ came from because they had disowned him and hence bore no responsibility for what he did under the tribal customs. Hence, they had to take it out on the a member of the Quraysh who patroned this murderer. This led to a series of one-day battles at exact intervals of a year at the days in which the murder took place. This sounds weird to us but such anniversary battles find standard mention in pre-Islamic Arabian warfare. It was followed by a series of revenge killings on both sides. Now, in a justice system based on revenge killing (where a person kills their relative’s murderer) has to stop at some point, without which killings will continue ad infinitum. Normally, these killings stopped after a certain point, when both parties find it exhausting or infeasible to continue the cycle. After the killings stop, as a settlement, the side that suffered the more number of killings would receive a monetary compensation from the party that suffered the lesser number of killings to make up for the difference. This type of settlement is called **blood money** (Arabic : **diyah**), and is a recurrent occurrence in Arab sources, even after the advent of Islam (with some constraints though). In this particular incident, we are told that the clan of the murdered man (a clan of Qays) had lost twenty more men than the side of the murdering man (a clan of Quraysh) and hence the patron of Barrāḍ from Quraysh paid blood money to the clan of Qays, thus ending the cycle of killings.

The level of violence should not be surprising because in the tribal Arab society, there was no distinction between civilians and combatants and all adult males bore arms and were trained to fight. We have pre-Islamic poetry boasting stuff like “*Not one chief of ours ever died a natural death, nor was any slain man of ours ever left where he lay unavenged*” (Source : A.J. Arberry, Arabic poetry, Cambridge 1965, 30.10). This then, was a society with an excess of military energy that was dissipated in inter-tribal hostilities. Warfare was recurring and was regulated according to long established customary rules and could be tied to what we might associate more readily with sports in today’s scenario.

### Paganism and its discontents



The overwhelming majority religion in the Arabian peninsula was Arabian paganism which actually boasted a long antiquity. As early as the seventh century BCE, we find Assyrian sources mentioning that the ruler **Sennacherib** (r. 704 - 681 BCE) raiding an oasis in northern Arabia and taking its idols as booty. Soon, after his son Emperor **Esarhaddon** ascended the throne, an Arab king is said to have come to Assyria and kissed the emperor’s feet and begged him to return the idols. Esarhaddon is then said that out of mercy, he repaired and handed over the six idols to the Arab king after however inscribing a message on them that proclaimed the power of the god **Ashur** (patron god of the Assyrian empire) along with signing his own name. The name of one Arabic deity whose idols are carried back is mentioned in these Assyrian sources as **Ruladaiu**, which mostly looks like the god **Ruḍā** remembered in early Islamic sources much later (Arabic names like **ʿAbd Ruḍā** - meaning : servant of Ruḍā - are attested in early Islamic sources for pre-Islamic pagan names). A couple of centuries later in the fifth century BCE, the Greek historian Herodotus describes the Arabs in his histories by mentioning that they swear oaths on the gods Dionysus and Urania (which we saw in the previous section). But, Herodotus mentions that these are the Greek counterparts of the Arabian pagan gods and that the Arabs themselves call the deities by the names that go like **Orotalt** and **Alilat**. Clearly, the name Orotalt appears to resemble the Assyrian rendition Ruladaiu, which eventually came to be called by the name **Ruḍā**, which we saw just now. And Alilat is more easily identified with the goddess **Al-Lāt** who was mentioned to be widely worshipped by the Arabian pagans in Muhammad’s day (Al-Lāt is also one of the three goddesses behind the Satanic verses of the Quran, more on this later).

We see from early Islamic sources that every clan had a patron deity and above them, every tribe on the whole, had a patron deity as well, apart from a pantheon of gods worshipped by all Arabs. We read that each home in Arabia had idols and surrounding the kaʿbah, there were 360 idols worshipped across tribes, one for each day of the year. The idol of the deity Hubal had the privilege of being placed inside the kaʿbah which was important to the Quraysh, and it is said that a Meccan had brought that deity from Syria many centuries before the emergence of Islam. The legend is described in the work “***The Life of the Prophet***” (**Al-Sirā al-nabawiyyah**) by **Ibn Hishām** (died 833 CE).

ʿAmr ibn Luḥayy left Mecca for Syria on business. When he got to Moab in the Balqāʾ, then inhabited by the Amalekites, … he saw them worshipping idols, and said to them, “What are these idols I see you worshipping?” They said, “These are idols that we worship. We ask them for rain, and they make it rain; we ask them for victory, and they give us victory.” He said to them, “Do you think you could spare me one to take back to the land of the Arabs for them to worship?” So they gave him an idol called Hubal. He took it back to Mecca and set it up there, telling people to worship and venerate it.

Whatever be the historicity of this story, this answers two things : why pagan Arabs worshipped idols, and if pagan Arabs were hostile to foreign gods (the answer is a no). Whatever little survives from pre-Islamic times in Arabia, we find there that these pagan gods did not play any role when the Arabs pondered the deeper questions of human existence which were instead relegated to be discussed in their heroic poetry.

As the British scholar of Islam Michael Cook puts it, ***paganism is basically a loose term for forms of religion that are historically the human default***. They involve the worship of many gods and performance of elaborate rituals to appease them. In the Mediterranean world, the Middle East, Egypt, and North Africa, paganism was on its way out by the end of the time period of late antiquity. By contrast, in eastern and central parts of Asia (especially China and India) it never suffered any death, particularly in India, despite centuries of Islamic and later European Christian rule. So, unlike what monotheists tend to say, it is not the intrinsic nature of paganism to eventually become outdated and be replaced by a monotheism. Yet we do see that at a certain point in history, paganism in its ancient form, did find itself to be inadequate to its practitioners, at least among the cultural elite in all parts of the world. But, the normal reaction to this discontent in the East and even in the West (before the advent of Christianity) was not to reject it but to rise above it. In Persia, this happened through Zoroastrianism. The Zoroastrians of Persia did not hesitate to speak of and honour their traditional gods in the plural, but what mattered ultimately was the grand cosmic struggle between a single good god **Ahura Mazda** and his evil adversary **Ahriman**. In India, the reaction to this discontent happened through the emergence of the philosophical and soteriological ideas in the **Upaniṣads** and later, heterodox schools like Buddhism, both of which still performed traditional Vedic rituals (in Hinduism) or an adaptation of them (in Buddhism) to varying degrees and even worshipped many gods, but what became a matter of ultimate importance to these philosophically grounded religions, was the realisation of the **brahman** by the **ātman** (in Hinduism) or the realisation of the **impermanence and transitory nature of all of reality** (in Buddhism), both of which lead to individual liberation, called as **mokṣa** or **nirvāṇa**. In China, the collapse of social order during the Warring States period prompted a massive discontent to the traditional religion of Chinese society and saw the rise of **Confucianism** and **Daoism** which provided an overarching ethics and soteriology while still being rooted in the older traditional religion of the civilisation. In Greece too, this process began with the rise of philosophy which while not always outright rejecting the gods, myths, and rituals of paganism, sought to rise above them, until it was arrested and appropriated by the rise of Christianity. Monotheism, on the other hand, does not see paganism as just inadequate but as the worst form of abomination possible. In the previous articles in this series, we saw how this ball was set in motion by the ancient Egyptian king Akhenaten and by the ancient Israelites. While Akhenatenism failed and Judaism remained confined ethnically while spreading sparsely beyond Israel, Christianity was the first successful monotheism to convert people across the world beyond its ethnic origins.

### Judaism & Alternative Monotheisms in Arabia



Despite the success of Christianity (as we will see in the next section), it was not the only form of monotheism at play in Arabia. We also hear of a substantial Jewish population. Yemen boasted a significant Jewish population that is attested by inscriptions in the **Ḥimyarite** kingdom beginning in the fourth century and we find no polytheistic inscriptions there from the fifth century onwards. We cannot exactly tell if these people converted to Judaism, or adopted a monotheism as gentiles with strong Jewish influences, but the fact remains that there was a clear religious transformation. We find reports of persecution of Christians by the Jewish Ḥimyarite kingdom in the early sixth century.

Judaism also spread in the tribal parts of the Arabian peninsula (without any states) through a gradual cultural diffusion. We read of the tribe of **Kindah** in Arabia converting to Judaism under the influence of the Ḥimyarites. We also read that three of the five tribes that lived on the oases town of **Yathrib** (later Medina) were Jewish and there were other Jewish inhabitants around the oases in the Ḥijāz region. There were also smaller groups of Jewish clans who were the clients of Arab clans.

Apart from Judaism, we hear from early Islamic sources about “generic monotheists” mentioned 10 times in the Qur’an as **Ḥanīfs (**meaning in Arabic: **upright people).** The Qur’an views these people favourably for shunning the ignorance and error of Arabian polytheism and instead choosing to solely worship the God of Abraham, thus setting themselves apart from pagans. However, they were not associated with Judaism or Christianity; instead proclaimed what they practiced as the unaltered beliefs and morals of Abraham (this would be important to Islam ; as would be evident in the section on Muhammad as an Abrahamic revivalist). The Islamic faith views **Ḥanīfism** as a precursor to Islam and paving the way for it and we can say with a reasonable confidence that they had a presence in Mecca where the Quraysh lived, given their mention in the Qur’ãn. We unfortunately do not have any of their own surviving texts and hence we never know what they exactly believed and practised; but they seem to have been an insignificant bunch of scattered individuals but who definitely existed as supported by an epigraphical record found in the neighbourhood of **Ṭāʾif** in this period.

Apart from this, there is another religious group mentioned three times the Quran as **Ṣābiʾūn** who are referred to as monotheists and hence people of the book. Their original identity, which seems to have been forgotten at an early date in Islamic history, has been called an “*unsolved Quranic problem*”. We will not dwell into the theories of speculation as to who these people were.

Since their exact identity was forgotten and debated even by the early Islamic commentators to the Qur’ãn, numerous groups throughout history have claimed themselves as the Ṣābiʾūn, or have been called as Ṣābiʾūn by the Muslims, to draft them as the people of the book and hence escape forced conversion to Islam and reside as dhimmis in Islamic empires. This also included Hinduism and Buddhism in the Indian subcontinent. For example, **Al-Biruni** identifies the brahmins with the Ṣābiʾūn as they possessed a high philosophy that recognised one supreme being, while distinguishing them from the masses who practised devotion in the form of idolatry. In medieval Islamic academic literature, Ṣābiʾūn hence effectively evolved into a catch-all term for *any ancient, highly civilised, non-Abrahamic people who possessed sacred wisdom, philosophy, and sciences*.

### Christianity in Arabia



Without doubt, the only form of monotheism that was on continuous ascent in Arabia was Christianity. Elsewhere, the spread and triumph of Christianity was made possible only because of the conversion of emperors (both in the Roman Empire and later and beyond). These converted kings used their state apparatus to persecute paganism and convert their subjects to Christianity, wherein the might of a state is used to overcome the cultural inertia offered by the antiquity of paganism to convert people to the new monotheistic religion of the emperor. In late antiquity, it was clear that Christianity was triumphing its way out in the world of Europe, the Middle East, North Africa, and even Arabia. While the Sassanid Persians were encircling the Arabian peninsula politically, when it came to religion, it was Christianity (and not Zoroastrianism) that was encircling Arabia from the outside. Even other ethnic groups that lived in the neighbourhood of Persia like the Armenians, Georgians, and the Albanians, became Christian. We saw that there were two Arab kingdoms in the north of Arabia with substantial population of Miaphysite and Nestorian Christians. Persian rule might have slowed its spread but could not stop Christianity which was now making new inroads into the southern region of Yemen in the sixth century and also into the Mesopotamian provinces of the Persian Empire. But, definitely Christianity was finding it tough to make inroads into the interior of the Arabian peninsula and in the Ḥijāz region, even though it was in touch with the Christian world around it. Early Islamic sources mention the dependence of the Quraysh on Syria for trade and Muhammad himself had been involved in it before his commission as a prophet of Islam. The absence of a strong state was the main obstacle in the easy spread of Christianity into the Arabian interior. While the Arabian interior was still the largest outpost of paganism in late antiquity, for anyone with even a slight awareness of the wider world, paganism appeared as the odd ball out among the prevailing religious ideas at large. But, the conversion of Arabs to monotheism was expected to be sure despite being slowed by the absence of large states. The obvious alternatives for monotheism were the local Judaism and the Christianity of the outside world. But Judaism had been present in the oases of the Ḥijāz for centuries without provoking the conversion of its inhabitants at large; it was the Christian encirclement of Arabia that was new. Why wouldn’t the Arabs follow the example of so many other peoples of the time and convert to Christianity?

### Islam as a Black Swan Event in World History



In the immediate aftermath of the long drawn out war between the Byzantine and Persian empires in the beginning of the seventh century, a well-informed observer would not have been faulted to think that soon things would return to normal in the Middle East. With the Persian Empire under new management and the Byzantine empire stabilised, it looked as if the long-lasting balance of power between the two empires was again being restored after this prolonged period of war which ended in 628 CE. If anything were to upset this delicate balance, it would surely be the sudden emergence of some fearsome new tribe among the Steppe nomads. Meanwhile the tribes of Arabia could be expected to continue to keep fighting among themselves. They might well pose local threats to the borderlands of these empires at a time when both were exhausted. But the Arabs definitely were not in the same league as those sophisticated Steppe nomads. Sooner or later, the Arabs would surely follow the path taken by all other peoples around them and convert to Christianity, as a large number of them had already done in the borders of the Arabian peninsula.

If there was an open question about the future of religion in the Middle East, it was whether in the long run, the Persian Empire could continue to stand out against the Christianisation of the Middle East. Christianity was making inroads right into the Persian royal family where the Sassanid king **Khusrow II’**s favourite wife **Shirin** was a Christian and began to influence the emperor to make her own Christian son (or at least Christian sympathetic son) as his successor, rather than his eldest son from another wife ! This enraged the Zoroastrian priesthood and nobility who led a coup and overthrew the emperor Khusrow II in 628 CE after the war with Byzantium ended and he was weakened with the economy of his empire in ruins. Khusrow’s eldest and staunchly Zoroastrian son **Kavad II** was placed on the throne with the support of the Zoroastrian priesthood and nobility. Thus, Christianity seemed to have been gaining power all across the known world of the Middle East and in Europe, in late antiquity. Anything that happened in Arabia would just be the usual inter-tribal warfare. All these guesses in this paragraph would sound very reasonable by a well-aware contemporary, and indeed it would have been the case. But, we all know that it is not what happened.

The rise and spread of Islam constitutes a **Black Swan Event** in world history, the concept of which is elaborated by the famous financier and mathematician **Nassim Nicholas Talib** in his book “**The Black Swan**”. A Black Swan Event in a system possess these three features :

- **Unpredictability** : The event is an outlier so far outside normal
  expectations that nobody then could have seen it coming and even the
  possibility of it happening is unknown when it happens.

- **Extreme Impact :** It unexpectedly changes the course of events
  drastically for all time yet to come and carries massive, widespread
  consequences or disruptions on the system (whether the system is a
  financial market, world history, or the human body) .

# Muhammad’s Career



Two things were critical to Muhammad’s career in early seventh century Arabia and were unprecedented :

1.  a new monotheistic religion (Islam)

2.  a new state uniting a majority of Arabian peninsula

Both were novel and unprecedented to the region. And after Muhammad’s death, his successors not only ensured the survival of both his state and his religion but also

3.  conquer and establish for themselves, one of the largest empires in
    history

There are several questions we need to ask regarding the origins of Islam. Why did Muhammad not adopt a monotheistic religion in the form of Judaism (familiar to him) or Christianity (on ascent in Arabia and the world around him) and instead provide the Arabs with a monotheism of their own? The other equally revolutionary feature of his career was that he founded a state in the region of Arabia whose geography and tribal polity seemed antithetical to state formation. Both of the first two events are important and each linked to each other while forming the base for the third development. What this exactly means is that Muhammad’s new religion could not have survived if he had not founded a state to go with it and Muhammad’s state would not have been possible or even necessary to establish without the offensiveness of his new religion. Also, the combination of the state and religion he established were both key in the quick and vast expansion of both of them after his death.

To answer these questions in short, the offensiveness of his monotheistic message necessitated him to unleash a chain of events that led him to establish a state in Arabia and conversely the state that he founded would enable him to spread his monotheistic religion throughout Arabia and after his death, the entire Middle East and North Africa. None of these events were easy or obvious even if required. The key documents that we will be using to study this process are :

- a document referred to by scholars as the **Constitution of Medina**.

The basic outlines of Muhammad’s life is widely known to everyone but this paragraph gives a recap in case it is rusty. He was born in Mecca around the year 570 CE in the tribe of the **Quraysh**, specifically the clan of the **Banū Hāshim**. As we saw, this tribe made a living from business and Muhammad himself was involved as a merchant in his youth, and had even travelled to Syria. At about 610 CE, it is said that he received the first of the revelations from the angel **Jibrāʾīl** (Biblical name : **Gabriel** ; the same Gabriel in Judaism who appears to the prophet Daniel to explain his visions, and the same Gabriel in Christianity who appears to the Virgin Mary to tell that she will get pregnant by the Holy Spirit and bear the Son of God). But, Christianity also accepts the entire Jewish Bible as scripture, claims that it is not a deviation from Judaism but a fulfilment of it, and that the Christian Church is the eschatological conclusion of the mission of Judaism. This tension is felt when we compare some verses in the New Testament. On the one hand, we have Jesus in the gospel of Matthew (5:17) saying : “*Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfil them*”. Here, the Law refers to the Jewish law codes and the Prophets refers to the prophetic mission of the Hebrew prophets of Israel. On the other hand, we have Paul saying in his letter to the Colossians (2:14) : “*having canceled the charge of our legal indebtedness* (Jewish law codes), *which stood against us and condemned us; he (Jesus) has taken it away, nailing it to the cross*”, and the unknown author of the letter to the Hebrews (7:18-19) saying : “*The former law is set aside because it was weak and useless, for the law made nothing perfect, and a better hope is introduced, by which we draw near to God*”. The way Christianity reconciles its Jewish origins and its claim to Jewish antiquity while also deviating from it is a complex theological argument. We discussed this in the last article in the series and we will recollect it now. Paul puts this nicely in his letter to the Romans as usual.

Paul argued that because of the crucifixion of Jesus, the ritualistic part of the Jewish law (including circumcision, kosher diet regulations, keeping the Sabbath, animal sacrifice, and all other rituals that marked Jews as a distinct people from gentiles) in the Hebrew scriptures had been **transcended and fulfilled once and for all**, and has been **substituted for through faith in Jesus.** The brilliancy in the rhetoric of Paul can be seen in his letter to the Romans (in the New Testament) where he argues using the Jewish scriptures themselves that the Jewish law (in those very same scriptures) was not that important to Judaism! The central crux of Paul’s argument is that God chose and blessed Abraham’s descendants as his own people after observing how steadfast Abraham was in his faith. Only after that, he commanded circumcision of himself and his male descendants. Paul understands this to imply that it was faith that first made Abraham upright in the eyes of God ; the commandment of circumcision came later. For Paul, the fundamental essence of Judaism is that of the relationship between Abraham and God through his faith. All the other things - circumcision and other laws revealed at Mt Sinai through Moses - all these came later. So, Paul justified that when he was grafting gentiles into the chosen people of God and proclaiming that faith in Jesus was enough for gentiles to be chosen and partake in the blessings of God, he was not violating or blaspheming against Judaism (as most other Jews thought) but was returning Judaism to its primordial original Abrahamic essence. He also interprets the verse in Genesis where God blesses Abraham saying “*all peoples on earth will be blessed through you*”, this actually was fulfilled when the gentiles (non-Jews) from other nations joined the Christian movement with just their faith in Jesus. So, according to Paul, the Christian Church was not a heresy of Judaism but the fulfilment of Judaism and a return to its original essence.

Judaism, with its law and its prophetic mission of the Hebrew prophets, are all important in Christianity; but only instrumentally so. Judaism had its place in instructing the Jews and to point to the incarnation, but Jesus Christ fulfilled the entire law once and for all by paying for our sins on the cross. The sayings of the Hebrew prophets in the Jewish Bible were re-interpreted as predicting or pre-figuring the incarnate Christ. For Christians, Judaism (with its laws and prophets) is divine in origin and is important, but only instrumentally, to lead to Christ, who is the fulcrum of the Jewish revelation and mission. In Christian thought, Judaism represents the **Old Covenant** (Latin : **Old Testament**) that God made with the Jewish people, and is neither evil nor false ; Judaism is indeed holy and divinely inspired. But, it is provisional, and was designed by God to prepare the world for the **New Covenant** (Latin : **New Testament**) that God made through Christ with all of humanity, which is proclaimed by Christianity. Thus, Judaism is indeed ancient and divine in origin, but the entire point of Judaism is to lead the Jews to Christianity, which is its fulfilment, when non-Jews and Jews alike would get justified with God revealed in fullness through Christ. Thus, through this re-interpretation, Christianity anchors itself to the antiquity of Judaism and sees itself as part of the same tradition as Judaism, while still innovating from it, by re-interpreting the older religion.

Now, what about the attitude of Islam to Judaism and Christianity? Does Islam reinterpret Christianity and Judaism, in some way to anchor itself to their Abrahamic origins while re-interpreting them (like Christianity did with the Jewish scriptures and Judaism)? The short answer is a “No”. This should be evident from the basic fact that unlike Christianity whose canon of sacred scripture includes the Jewish Bible as a part of it (calling it the Old Testament), the sacred scripture of Islam does not include the Bible (neither the Old Testament nor the New Testament) and contains only the revelations to the prophet Muhammad contained in the Qur’an. Thus, while Christianity physically bound its new writings to the existing Jewish canon to form a two-part Bible, Islam did not incorporate the Bible into its canon; instead, the Quran stands alone in Islam as the final, unmediated, direct speech of God.

This itself must tell us that Islam does not think of the Jewish Old Testament or the Christian New Testament as a part of the divine plan and inspired from God. Then, how does Islam anchor itself to the antiquity of the Abrahamic traditions of the Jewish and Christian prophets while still calling itself the religion followed by Abraham? The answer provided in the Islamic tradition is much more simpler. Islam believes that God did give revelations to the Jews and Christians, which are mentioned in the Qur’an like the **Tawrāh** (Torah) given to Moses, the **Zabūr** (the Psalms) given to David, and the **ʾInjīl** (Gospel) given to Jesus. But, it believes that the Bible, as possessed by the Jews and Christians, is not the true record of the revelation that Allah gave to these earlier prophets. Islamic tradition believes that the original scriptures given to these earlier prophets were corrupted by humans who eventually wrote down, not the original revelations given to these prophets, but their own re-interpretation of the revelation. Islam holds that all the original revelations given to all of these earlier prophets are all in harmony with each other and the Islamic message in the the Qur’ān, and they all confirm what came before them and what will come after them, including the final revelation of Islam. For example, we have Jesus in the Qur’ān (61:6) saying : “*O children of Israel! I am truly Allah’s messenger to you, confirming the Torah which came before me, and giving good news of a messenger after me whose name will be Aḥmad* (another name for Muhammad)”.

This puts Islam in the same mainstream monotheistic tradition of the Abrahamic faiths of Judaism and Christianity. But, Islamic theology also says that, all of these previous revelations to these earlier prophets, were corrupted by the time they were written down by other humans, and this is the reason Islam differs from both Christianity and Judaism. According to Islam, whatever is there in these scriptures of Judaism and Christianity, that is in harmony with the Qur’an, are signs of Islam’s continuity with the older Abrahamic prophetic tradition (starting from Abraham to Jesus). But, whenever anything in these older scripture disagrees with Islam, it is because those parts in the older scriptures are corruptions (Arabic : doctrine of **taḥrīf**) of the original revelation from God to the earlier prophets. Islam holds that the Qur’an is the uncorrupted revelation directly from God and whatever is written in the Qur’an are the exact words that God sent to mankind and the contents of this Qur’anic revelation is in harmony with all the earlier revelations he sent to the older prophets.

In summary, while both Christianity and Islam claim continuity with the ancient Abrahamic heritage, they employ fundamentally different theological mechanisms to do so. Christianity physically and theologically incorporates the Jewish religion and even the Jewish scripture as its Old Testament, framing the Mosaic Law and the Hebraic prophets as a divinely inspired, provisional preparation that finds its ultimate fulfilment in Jesus Christ. Islam, by contrast, bypasses the integration of Judaism and Christianity entirely, asserting instead that while God sent original and true revelations to the Jewish prophets like Moses, David, and Jesus, the Jewish and Christian sacred canon of texts are not a faithful record of these true revelations but were written after being subjected to human corruption over time (*taḥrīf*). Consequently, where Christianity views its scripture as an expansion that permanently binds itself to a canonised Jewish past, Islam views the Qur’an as the final, uncorrupted restorer of a primordial monotheism that transcends and corrects the historical aberrations of both Judaism and Christianity.

## Muhammad : The Politician



In this section, we will focus on the political institutions and precedents established by Muhammad in Arabia.

### Monotheism and State Formation in the Bible



If we look at Judaism or even Christianity, we do not find a correlation between the establishment of a new monotheistic religion and the establishment of a state. In the Bible, Abraham is not seen founding any kind of state in the Bible. Moses too only assumes a leadership role for freeing the Israelites stuck in Egypt and guiding them through the desert. For a long time, the Israelites remain a tribal confederacy with occasional charismatic leaders called “Judges'“ taking leadership roles while fighting their common enemies in their land. Right from the time of Abraham’s calling till the time of the prophet Samuel (and even after the Babylonian exile to the return under Persian rule), for a long time, **the Israelites seem to be doing fine as monotheists without having a state of their own**. Even eventually, when the Israelites want to establish a state of their own in the land of Canaan, and want a king who would fight for them like in all other nations, God makes it very clear that he does not welcome the creation of the monarchy. In fact, in the Bible, we read that God is actually disappointed that the Israelites want to have a king, because God was supposed to be their king. For example, we read in I Samuel 8:4-21 :

So all the elders of Israel gathered together and came to Samuel at Ramah. They said to him, “You are old, and your sons do not follow your ways; now **appoint a king to lead us, such as all the other nations have**.” But when they said, “Give us a king to lead us,” this displeased Samuel; so he prayed to the Lord. And the Lord told him: “Listen to all that the people are saying to you; it is not you they have rejected, but **they have rejected me as their king**. As they have done from the day I brought them up out of Egypt until this day, forsaking me and serving other gods, so they are doing to you. **** Now listen to them; but **warn them solemnly and let them know what the king who will reign over them will claim as his rights**”.

Samuel told all the words of the Lord to the people who were asking him for a king. **** He said, “**This is what the king who will reign over you will claim as his rights**: He will take your sons and make them serve with his chariots and horses, and they will run in front of his chariots. Some he will assign to be commanders of thousands and commanders of fifties, and others to plow his ground and reap his harvest, and still others to make weapons of war and equipment for his chariots. He will take your daughters to be perfumers and cooks and bakers. He will take the best of your fields and vineyards and olive groves and give them to his attendants. He will take a tenth of your grain and of your vintage and give it to his officials and attendants. Your male and female servants and the best of your cattle and donkeys he will take for his own use. He will take a tenth of your flocks, and you yourselves will become his slaves. **When that day comes, you will cry out for relief from the king you have chosen, but the Lord will not answer you in that day**”.

But the people refused to listen to Samuel. “**No**!” they said. “**We want a king over us. Then we will be like all the other nations, with a king to lead us and to go out before us and fight our battles**”.

When Samuel heard all that the people said, he repeated it before the Lord. **** The Lord answered, “**Listen to them and give them a king.**”

So, we see that God in the Bible is anti-monarchical and only reluctantly consents for the Israelites to have a state of their own because other people have one and the Israelites are facing pressure from their enemies. In fact, God even warns them that having a king will make them more miserable as he will take stuff or labour from them. Although eventually, he does make a covenant with King David and promises him that his descendants will rule Israel forever, it is broken soon when the united monarchy of Israel is divided into two after the death of his son Solomon, and both the kingdoms fall to the Assyrian and Babylonian conquests. The Bible portrays kings like David and Solomon as not ideal role-models but as individuals who also have deeply flawed character. In fact, when one reads the Biblical books from I Samuel to II Kings that narrate the history of the Israelite kingdoms, one thinks that it is a lesson to illustrate the dangers of having a monarchy, rather than it being the ideal form of government. For example, although King David is regarded in Jewish and Christian history as the greatest king Israel has ever had, the Bible also says that he committed adultery with a woman named Bathsheba and then arranged for her husband to be killed after finding out that she is pregnant. The prophet Nathan then rebukes David for doing this and God gets angry and punishes him. As another example, even though King Solomon is renowned for his wisdom, he is also seen falling into temptation and worship foreign gods through diplomatic pressure and also the influence from his foreign wives and concubines. Although the Bible strongly encourages the people of Israel to form a state of their own in the land of their own, and even though political emancipation from foreign powers is an important and recurring theme, the Bible also makes it clear that the united monarchy of Israel is divided and the two kingdoms in Israel fall to conquests of the Assyrians and Babylonians, because the Israelites have sinned. It makes it clear that monarchy is far from being the best institution for the practice and upholding of religion. **Rather than being a divinely sanctioned ideal institution, earthly kingship in the Hebrew Bible is depicted as imperfect, and also as a potential source of corruption or even a frequent menace to genuine religious devotion.**

If we move on to the New Testament, Jesus is clear that although he proclaims himself as a king, he says (John 18:33-36) to Pilate “*My kingdom is not of this world*”. Jesus neither establishes a state himself nor does his great commission to his apostles include establishing a state to spread his gospel. His approach in the gospels is to co-operate with secular rulers, as enshrined in the famous quote found in three of the four gospels (Mark 12:17, Matthew 22:21, Luke 20:25) “*Render to Caesar* (king)*, the things that are Caesar's; and to God the things that are God's*”. This has been taken as the basis to justify the Christian doctrine of the separate institutions of Church and State (the question of whether the Church and State interact is a different question; but the fact that the Church and the State are expected to be different and well-demarcated institutions which play different roles is a standard doctrine in Christian theology). Christianity survived and spread for the first three-hundred years without any establishment of a state (and in fact, with occasional persecutions from the Roman state). The golden age of Christianity that all Christians look up to, whether Catholic, Orthodox, or Protestant, is this period in early Christianity, where the Church was poor and suffering but pure and uncorrupted by worldly wealth or political power. It is referred to by various names such as the“**Church of the Martyrs**” or the “**Church of the Catacombs**” and so on. It is true that only after 312 CE, Christianity would spread much more rapidly by using the state power of kings and emperors, to eventually destroy the pagan faiths of Europe completely, to win converts for itself. But, this beginning of imperial favour to Christianity, is also seen by many as the phase where the Church gets corrupted and deviates from the exemplar of the holy poverty and suffering set up by Christ. So, we see that there is no inner logic directly leading from monotheism to state formation (although Christianity did soon use state power for millennia to spread and destroy competing religions and win converts throughout the world) ; otherwise, we would see them reflected in Judaism and Early Christianity.

Even in the Qurʾān itself, none of the earlier prophets (both Jewish and Arabian), whose stories it recounts, are seen establishing any kind of states. But, in the case of Islam, we see that Muhammad simultaneously established both a religion and a state. And he established a state in a region that never had a state anytime in its historical memory and where it seemed infeasible to do so. Why was this the case with Islam and what implications does it have?

### Muhammad’s need for a state



Because Islam was born with a state, its foundational scriptures and religious traditions naturally incorporate civic, criminal, commercial, and constitutional law, alongside ritual, ethics, and theology. There was never an institutional “Church” that exists separate from the “State” in Islamic theology. Instead, religious and political authority were combined under the office of the **Caliphate,** at least in theory, and in practice as well, throughout the early history of Islam. This is also the reason why the Islamic legal system (**sharī’ah**) encompasses both secular and religious aspects.

In Christianity, the “golden age” is associated with a poor and suffering church with believers being a defenceless minority ready to die at the hands of a state that can persecute them. In contrast, classical Islamic theology views the successful establishment of a just, righteous Islamic polity **(Dar al-Islam)** as a direct arena for implementing God’s commands. Power itself is not viewed inherently as a corruption, but as a divine trust that must be exercised justly.

One key thing that necessitated the establishment of a state is the fact that Muhammad’s monotheistic message was extremely offensive to the rest of pagan society and we saw how offences can lead to easy conflicts and cycles of violence in a highly tribal society. The society of Arabian pagans, despite all the tribalism, was not religiously intolerant, as we saw in the last section. **Ibn Isḥāq** himself (who authored the biography of Muhammad and the main historical source for writing this section) tells us that the Arabian pagans did not turn against Muhammad until he abused their pagan gods. He writes :

When the prophet openly displayed Islam, as God ordered him, his people did not withdraw or turn against him, as far as I have heard, until he spoke disparagingly of their gods. When he did that, they took great offence and resolved unanimously to treat him as enemy.

We then read of a pagan delegation complaining to **Abū Ṭālib** (an uncle of Muhammad who was his guardian as he had no father) : “*Your nephew has cursed our gods, insulted your religion, the religion of your fathers, severed the unity of your people, mocked our way of life, and accused our forefathers of error*”. They demanded that either he put an end to Muhammad’s anti-social preaching or stop protecting him. They were even willing to make a compromise by saying : “*Let him have his religion and we will have ours*”. Ibn Isḥāq clearly mentions that it was Muhammad who was uncompromising and insensitive. Then, the pagans of Mecca began to persecute the Muslims. Ibn Isḥāq says “*Then Quraysh incited one another against the followers of the prophet in each clan .. So, each clan fell upon those of its members who were Muslims, tormenting them and seducing them from their religion*”. But, we find that Muhammad was safe as he was protected by his uncle Abū Ṭālib who was a prominent member in his Qurayshi clan which was the **Banū Hāshim**. When members from the other Qurayshi clans in Mecca attempted to persecute the prophet, his uncle stopped them using his influence in his clan. This narrative makes it clear that one cannot persecute people without taking the tribal structure into consideration. For each clan to persecute its own Muslims was not a problem; but an attempt of one clan member to persecute a Muslim of another clan or another tribe would have led to inter-clan/inter-tribe clashes. So, while Muhammad’s own position was secure for now as long as his uncle was alive, the position of his followers in other clans and tribes were not. Finding protection for his followers from other clans was the first priority issue to be resolved for Muhammad, and he could not do so as long as he stayed in Mecca.

Hence, he started searching for protection from other clans outside Mecca. One option was to seek the protection of a king like how Martin Luther sought the protection of the Duke of Saxony to secure his newly founded Lutheran Church as he set in motion the Protestant Reformation. But, there was no king in Arabia in that period as we saw, and the nearest sympathetic monarch was the ruler of Christian Ethiopia, who was willing to provide a refuge for the Muslims who could make the journey there. Ibn Isḥāq writes :

When the Prophet saw how his followers were suffering, while he himself was quite safe thanks to the support of God and his uncle Abū Ṭālib, and that he could do nothing to protect them from the trials they were exposed to, he said to them: ‘I suggest you go off to Ethiopia, for there’s a king there under whose rule no one is wronged’.

We read that about eighty Muslims took this offer and left but Muhammad himself decided to stay in Ḥijāz because he wanted Islam to have a future in Arabia only. When his uncle died in 619 CE, this search for local protection from another Arabian tribe became all the more important. He first approached a tribe named **Thaqīf** who had a well fortified town but they rejected him. Then, he went to the Meccan fairs during the pilgrimage season and presented himself to the various members from various tribes (who would come to the fairs) and introduce himself and his new religion to them, and appeal to them for their protection. He tries negotiating with a handful of tribes but that did not work out and in one case, we are told that the negotiation failed regarding the issue of whether the tribal leader would inherit Muhammad’s leadership and authority over the Arabs after he died. Muhammad, who could not promise this, replied that it all depends on the will of Allah, and hence we read that the deal did not work out.

Finally, he met with some positive response from six members from the tribe of **Khazraj** in the fair. Khazraj and **Aws** were the two pagan tribes who lived in the oases town of Yathrib (later called Medina), along with three other Jewish tribes. Why did these members of this tribe entertain Muhammad’s request to protect him? Ibn Isḥāq gives several reasons. The first reason is that since these tribes lived alongside Jews, they were more exposed to and hence more open to a monotheistic religion. He also tells us that whenever the three Jewish tribes of Yathrib fought with these two pagan tribes of Yathrib, the Jews would threaten the pagan tribes that there would come a prophet with whom they would ally with and would kill them! So, when these six people met Muhammad, they apparently said to each other “*He must be the prophet the Jews have been threatening us with. Don’t let them (the Jews) get to him first!*”. They converted to Islam and said to Muhammad “*We have left our tribe, for no tribe is so divided by hatred and rancour as them. Perhaps, God will unite them through you. So, let us go to them, and invite them to this religion of yours. And if God unites them in it, then no man will be mightier than you*”. Thus, they returned to Yathrib and Muhammad was now assigned the task of uniting this heavily divided group of clans. Muhammad had to be careful in dealing with clan politics as any decision he made that would appear to show favouritism towards one particular clan or tribe would alienate others and fracture his vision; for example, Ibn Isḥāq says that Muhammad sent a Meccan Muslim to lead the Friday prayers of these newly converted Muslims from Yathrib because if he chose one of the clan members to do so, the others would never accept it. The year after this incident in the fair, Muhammad himself was at the final negotiations to move to Yathrib permanently to be under the protection of these tribes. The tribes ask him a tough question that if they were to cut alliances with the Jewish tribes and helped Muhammad to win over his enemies in the Quraysh, could it be that he then would abandon them and go back to his own people?”. Muhammad reassured them that he would not do so and designated a team of twelve leaders to take charge of the affairs of this community in Yathrib. Then, he himself left for Yathrib (Medina) in the fall of 622 CE and this event is called the **hijrah** (emigration). The scholar of Islam, Michael Cook, writes why Muhammad found it necessary to create a polity.

Ibn Isḥāq’s account of the way in which Muḥammad came to found a state has a tight logic to it. But he does not present it as the unfolding of a grand design that was there from the start; rather, the state was a by-product of a process in which one thing led to another. **Monotheist incivility** led to a **backlash**, the backlash led to a **need for protection**, and in the **absence of a king or a tribe** that would provide it, the **quest for protection led to Yathrib**—or as we can now start to call it, Medina. There, **for lack of a political order, Muḥammad had to create one himself**.

### The Constitution of Medina



The nature of the political order that Muhammad established in Medina can be understood when we read a document that is found in the biography of the prophet that scholars have called “**The Constitution of Medina**” which is widely accepted as genuine. It starts out with :

> This is a prescript of Muhammad, the Prophet and Messenger of God (to
> operate) between the faithful and the followers of Islam (Muslims)
> from among the Quraysh and the people of Medina, and those who may be
> under them, may join them and wage jihād alongside them.

It is made of some 46 clauses and the full constitution can be found in the appendix of this article (the last section) and I urge you to just flip through it sometime if you are interested. But, in this section, I will summarise key elements that are to be found in that document. Some of them are old and some of them are novel. The elements that are old have to do with the tribal structure of pre-Islamic Arabia where in we find that numerous tribal groups are mentioned by name in the constitution. Also, many traditional pre-Islamic practices like revenge killing, blood money, ransom, alliance, clientage, etc.. are mentioned in the document and hence are assumed to carry on as usual. But alongside these old institutions, there are also new superstructures that constrain these older practices.

1.  **Ummah** : Firstly, there now exists a single extra-tribal
    community that constitutes “*one people* **(ummah)** *to the
    exclusion of the others*” defined very lengthily as “*the believers
    and the followers of Islam (Muslims) from among the Quraysh and the
    people of Medina, and those who may be under them, may join them and
    wage jihād alongside them*”. This wording allows for the possibility
    that non-Muslims can be a part of this ummah as well, by allying
    with the Muslims and fighting alongside Muslims. But, the
    multi-religious character allowed in theory by this constitution did
    not work out in practice and would soon break down, as we will see
    in detail in the next subsection when we discuss Muhammad’s military
    campaigns. Also, inside the ummah, special status is accorded to the
    believers or the faithful (**mu’min**) which we will see in the next
    paragraph.

2.  **Mu’min** : Another thing that we see is that whenever these tribal
    institutions and identities collide with the religious identity, the
    religious identity triumphs (read the clauses 12-20). For example,
    although the tribal practice of revenge killings continues, a
    believer will now not kill another believer in retaliation for an
    unbeliever. Although the tribal alliances continue, a believer will
    now not ally with an unbeliever against another believer. The
    constitution explicitly states that “*the peace of the believers is
    one*”; meaning that a believer will not make peace with an
    unbeliever to the exclusion of another believer while fighting in
    the way of God.

3.  **Theocracy** : Another important feature (read clauses 22 and 42)
    is that “*whenever you differ about anything, refer it to God and to
    Muhammad*”. The supreme authority of this community for both
    political and religious matters is God who speaks through Muhammad.
    In other words, this constitution is a theocracy, or the rule of God
    through Muhammad, in this case.

4.  **Jihād / Religious War** : The word jihād (in its verbal form
    **jāhada**) is mentioned right in the first clause of the
    constitution. Clause 17 explicitly mentions “*war in the way of
    God*”.

Thus, what we have is a society where the older tribal elements continue but are constrained by the new religious order related to Islam. Thus, for example, the tribal practice of vengeance continues but is constrained by the rule that a believer is not to kill another believer for avenging an unbeliever.

So, we see that whenever the older tribal customs and institutions continue but are constrained by the religious identity that whenever these imply that a believer harm another believer for benefiting an unbeliever, they are annulled.

### Muhammad’s military campaigns



Upto the time he was in Mecca, Muhammad simply preached and called people to Islam. But, once he emigrated to Medina and began establishing a polity, God gives him permission to fight and attack those who wronged him and warfare became a very regular part of his activities. During the decade he stayed in Medina, Ibn Isḥāq writes that he organised about sixty-four military expeditions against unbelievers (the exact numbers vary in other sources). We see that all of these prominent battles were against Mecca except the last one which was just related to events in Mecca. The Battle of Badr is the first victorious battle he commanded and this is just two years into arrival in Medina. We see that the number of men he recruits to fight gradually rises with time. If these battles above weere the most prominent first class battles he waged and all of these were against Meccans, what about the second class battles? All of these second level battles are confrontations with Jewish tribes. He fought with the three Jewish tribes of Medina in 624-627 after which two of the tribes were expelled while the third tribe had all of its men executed and all of its women and children enslaved. Among war captives, how to distinguish between a man (who is to be killed) and a male child (who is to be enslaved)? We are told that all males were stripped and inspected for pubic hairs and whoever possessed any hair in their privates were regarded as men and killed while those who did not were regarded as children and enslaved. This is the pattern that is repeated in all campaigns. In 628, Muhammad battled against two Jewish tribes in the oases of the northern Ḥijāz - Khaybar and Fadak. After defeating the Jews, he made a deal with them that they would hand over half of their agricultural produce to him each year and also took spoils. There were lot of other minor skirmishes he fought, one of which is worth mentioning here because it is interesting.

The **Banū ʾl-Muṣṭaliq** were nomads of the southwestern Ḥijāz with links to Mecca. Muhammad performed a pre-emptive strike against them around the year 627 and easily emerged victorious. Some of the men were killed, the rest fled, and Muhammad took possession of their land, properties, wives, children, to distribute them as booty to his supporters. In this war, a Khazraji (tribe from Medina that supported Muhammad) had mistakenly killed a Meccan Muslim. The slain man’s brother was living in Mecca and came to Muhammad, saying that he was a Muslim and h needed blood money for his slain brother. But, upon getting the blood money, he reverted back to paganism (or maybe he pretended to be a Muslim to get the blood money from Muhammad). Muhammad did not forget this incident and when he invaded Mecca in 630, he would remember promptly to kill him. But, there was something else that would pose an even more serious threat to the career of Muhammad if he did not handle it carefully. Two men got into a fight - one of them being an ally of the Khazraji clan from Medina and the other being an ally of the Qurayshi clan from Mecca. This fight escalated and soon, the Khazraji man appealed to the Medinan Muslims to support his side while the Qurayshi man appealed to the Meccan Muslims to support his side. This tribal rallying threatened to fracture the nascent Muslim community into two along his Meccan and Medinan Muslims. The chief of a Khazraji clan from Medina (who secretly detested Muhammad and viewed him as an outsider from Mecca) hinted that while they were kind enough to give refuge to these Meccan, now these intruders were taking over them. Under normal circumstances, Muhammad would have this Medinan chief killed but this would be a disaster in reputation for him if he did so because it would alienate his Medinan followers. Now, the son of this chief appears to Muhammad and says that he could kill his father on Muhammad’s behalf and no one would know. Muhammad was now in a dilemma because he would love to have that chief killed without causing political problems but as God’s prophet, he could not allow a son to kill his father. To prevent the Muslims from factionalizing and brooding over the insult, Muhammad immediately ordered the army to force-march without stopping. He kept the army moving through the day and the entire night until the next morning's sun grew hot. By the time he allowed them to halt, the men were so physically exhausted that they immediately fell asleep, completely breaking the momentum of the tribal dispute. By sparing the chief and treating him with public forbearance, Muhammad achieved a total political victory. The Medinan Muslims themselves became disgusted with the chief’s divisive rhetoric and treachery. Over time, whenever the chief spoke out, his own tribesmen rebuked and silenced him. Muhammad later pointed this out to one of his companions, noting that if he had killed the chief initially, tribal war would have erupted, but by waiting, the chief's own people had neutralised him.

There is another interesting episode connected to this situation. The chief of the Banū ʾl-Muṣṭaliq (whom Muhammad had just defeated) had an attractive daughter named **Juwayriya** who was among the captives. She approached Muhammad for help to get a ransom so that she could be freed and then he could marry her. Predictably, this enraged Muhammad’s wife **ʿĀʾisha** but she could not speak out openly. Now, since Muhammad had married this woman, her entire family became the in-laws of the prophet and hence they were also freed. On the way home from the raid, Muhammad apparently suspected about ʿĀʾisha’s fidelity to him and it took a special Qurʾānic revelation to clear ʿĀʾisha’s name and restore her to his favour. The scholar Michael Cook writes regarding this :

Anyone who expects the life of Muḥammad to read like the Gospels will be taken aback by the story of the raid on the Banū ʾl-Muṣṭaliq … It could also tell us a lot about the audience for whom the story was intended: not a community of otherworldly pietists like the early Christian audience of the Gospels, but rather an imperial military and political elite, men for whom, most of the time, this world bulked larger than the next.

By the time he died, Muhammad had established a strong political grip in the Ḥijāz region and to some extent, on the southwestern part of Arabia. He did it through warfare and also some diplomatic manoeuvres where in once he badly defeated and destroyed a tribe, nearby tribes would submit to him, and he would also employ divide and rule by allying with one tribe against another in tribal conflicts.

### The nature of Muhammad’s state



Two features stand out when we analyse the kind of state Muhammad established. One is the lack of any stable intermediate powers and power structures. Muhammad did not establish a hierarchy of men or an office. He did not have a favourite generals or a group of generals, based on any criteria like tribal affiliation or military excellence or religious piety. He did not send any one person too many times to delegate for him either in the battlefield or outside the battlefield. The most probable explanation for this is that he wished to avoid letting any of his followers acquire a power base that would rival his own and pose a threat to him. Muhammad for the most part, personally managed most things himself, but when he had to delegate, he did not make any one particular or one group of his followers as his right hand m(a/e)n. The lack of any stable intermediate powers in Muhammad’s polity is striking and will be evident and serve as the background to understand the chaotic battles of successions that happen after his death and also the Shia-Sunni schism in Islam that happened due to political differences of opinion over a matter of succession (unlike in Christianity where we see first schisms in the Church happening because of differences in doctrine; or in Buddhism where we see first schisms in the Buddhist Sangha happening because of differences in code of conduct).

Another conspicuous feature in his state is the fusion of religious and secular authority in himself which we already saw in the Constitution of Medina. Although he sometimes took advice from other people regarding secular matters, he fused the sacred and secular together at large. This is not surprising since it was religion that made to circumvent Arab tribalism and thereby open the door to the Arab state formation, and hence religion had to be fused with politics as it only rendered politics possible. In fact, in the biography of the prophet, we find some of his followers themselves pondering whether he was a king or a prophet.

# Fundamentals of Islam



### Scripture & Messenger



Superficially, one might expect that the Qur’ān for the Muslims is like the Bible for Christians and that Muhammad for Muslims is like Jesus for Christians. But, it turns out that this analogy is wrong.

In Christianity, Jesus is fully divine and a person of the Godhead who is incarnate on earth; Jesus is the divine Word that became flesh (John 1). In Islam, Muhammad is not divine or an incarnation of God. To Muslims, saying that God has a son or that he comes down to Earth, is a blasphemous thought and runs contrary to the hard monotheism of the absolute oneness of God and the absolute transcendence of God that Islam proclaims. Muhammad is a perfect human and an exemplar for all mankind, but he is still a human and a created entity. On the other hand, in Islam, the Word of God is not a person (like Jesus in Christianity) but the text of the Qur’ān, which Muslims believe is the eternal word of God that came down to earth as a revelation in the seventh century. Muhammad in Islam, despite his huge significance, ultimately is a created human, and an instrument through which the word of God Qur’ān was revealed to humanity. Muhammad’s role in Islam is more appropriately compared to the role of the Bible in Christianity. For Christians, despite the divine inspiration behind the Biblical text, the text of the Bible are not themselves divine and eternal. The Bible is ultimately a human written document (albeit inspired by the Holy Spirit) and an instrument through which the eternal word of God which is Jesus is made known to all humans.

So, Muhammad in Islam is analogous to the Bible in Christianity; and the Qur’ān in Islam is analogous to Jesus in Christianity.

**Qur’ān** → **Jesus** =\> the eternal word God revealed to mankind

**Muhammad** → **Bible** =\> the instrument to know the eternal word of God

This is even more evident when we think about it. Christians read the Bible in translation across most world languages. But for Muslims, the Qur’ān always is chanted in Arabic in all contexts (prayer, liturgy, chants, rituals) even if they do not understand Arabic, and the physical text of the Arabic Qur’ān is venerated immensely. This is because the Arabic text of the Qur’ān is the eternal word of God and hence has to be accessed as it is. Any translation of the Qur’ān is a text that is produced by a human translator, and no matter however good and faithful the translator is, human produced words cannot rival the divine word of God. But, any translation of the Bible still retains the same status as the original Hebrew-Greek text. This is because, even the Bible in its original languages, is anyways understood to be authored by humans (albeit with divine inspiration), and hence any other human-produced faithful translation of the text hence also accords the same status. Also, if you look at the history of early Christianity, there were lot of arguments about how Jesus can be fully God and yet there is only one God, how he can be fully human and yet fully divine, how he can be eternal yet born at a particular time and place, how can Jesus and God both be divine while being different persons, and so on. The Bible, on the other hand, was always understood to be a human produced document, and while there were fierce debates on what books are to be included in the canon of the Christian Bible, there was no metaphysical significance attached to the text. When one looks at the history of early Islam, one finds that the same kind of metaphysical questions that Christians asked about Jesus, were asked by Muslims about the Qur’ān. For example, if the Qur’ān is an eternal text and God is also eternal, how can they there be two eternal things, or how could the Qur’ān be both eternal and yet revealed at a certain time and place, and so on. Muhammad, on the other hand, was always understood to be a human even though there was a lot of work in collecting his authentic sayings (**ḥadīths**).

### Human Nature & Shirk



In Christianity, all humans are born with sin which cannot be redeemed by any effort from their part except by the grace of God that is conditioned on faith in Jesus. Islam rejects the Christian concept of original sin. Humans, in fact, are born with an inclination towards submitting to Allah and doing good, according to Islam, which is called the doctrine of **fiṭrah** . The problem is that they forget this. They drift into a state of pride and arrogance. They begin to act as if they can get by with their lives without God, as if they are the masters of their own destiny. This leads to the greatest sin in Islam which is **shirk** (Qur’ān 31:13 “*Never do shirk, for shirking is truly the worst of all wrongs*”). The Qur’ān says twice explicitly that Allah will forgive any sin any person has committed in his life if he desires, but he never forgives someone who dies with the sin of shirk (Qur’ān 4:48, 4:116 “*Surely Allah does not forgive shirk, but forgives anything else of whoever He wills. Indeed, whoever shirks has clearly gone far astray*) and also states that if a person has shirked, then whatever and how much ever other good deeds he performed are nullified (Qur’ān 6:88 “*Had they shirked, their ˹good˺ deeds would have been wasted*”). What is the meaning of shirk in general, and what all does the term encompass?

This word **shirk** is commonly understood to refer to idolatry but it is not just restricted to idolatry in its literal sense, but also includes making an idol out of our own self. This idol of the self is the dangerous delusion that a human being is self-sufficient because doing so elevates one’s self to godly role (Qur’ān 25:43, 45:23 “*Have you seen, O Prophet, the one who has taken their own desires as their god?*”). While physical idolatry (**wathaniyyah**) in Islam involves bowing to a statue, this form of equally abominable spiritual idolatry or subtle idolatry (**hawá**) happens when a person’s primary loyalty, source of validation, and ultimate decision-maker is their own ego rather than Allah's will. Both forms of idolatry are equally abominable and are grave and unforgivable sins.

This illusion that created humans are sustained without the creator leads to shirk which is associating partners with God. The reason why the pride of self-sufficiency is also viewed as a type of idolatry is worth pondering. When worshipping a created idol, one mistakes the idol as providing sustenance, which is only due to Allah. That is, we associate with an idol, something that is only Allah’s (e.g. sustenance). Thus, shirk is a much broader term and covers any form of hubris where we place anything equal to Allah, or associate partners to Allah ; which means that we associate with someone or something else, what is only Allah’s. The word “**shirk**” literally means “**association**” in Arabic, and even today, the Arabic word for a company or an association is the grammatically feminine form of the same word which is “**shirkah**”. When we are proud, then we think that we are our own sustainer and we place ourselves equal to Allah. Whether it is an idol or the self, shirk is a very big deal and the end result of the most fundamental problem characterising humanity hence is hubris. This hubris is what separates humanity from Allah and leads to the “forgetfulness” of our true nature as servants of the Allah. Thus, *shirk* includes idolatry and polytheism, but also any theological or practical act that attributes divine authority, power, or worship to anything besides God. It is considered the gravest theological error in Islam. Thus, the Christian diagnosis of the human condition is inherited sin that is remedied by divine grace, while the Islamic diagnosis is of innate purity compromised by forgetfulness and hubris, which is remedied by remembrance of God and submission to God.

If the problem is the arrogance of thinking we are independent, the solution is **submission**. The goal of Islamic life is to return to one’s natural state of dependence on God. This surrender results in a “soul at peace” in this life and the reward of **Paradise** in the next. Unlike religions that speak of salvation (being rescued from a fallen state), Islam speaks of success and stability. The imagery is often architectural: building a life that can stand firm. The ultimate destination is the afterlife. The Quran is filled with vivid descriptions of the “Garden” (Paradise)—a place of cool shade, running rivers, and peace—and the “Fire” (Hell), which awaits those who refuse to submit.

### The Five Pillars



How does one conquer the idol of the self and achieve submission? The technique is a rigorous system of ritual practices known as the **five pillars**. These are not merely good deeds; they are the structural supports of the Muslim life.

The central pillar is a simple declaration: “*I testify that there is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the messenger of God*”. To become a Muslim, one need only say this phrase with sincere intent. It affirms Islam’s hard monotheism (the oneness of God) and the authority of Muhammad as the final prophet of God.

Five times a day at dawn, noon, mid-afternoon, sunset, and night, all Muslims stop whatever they are doing to pray. This is not just a conversation with God; but a physical act of submission that is the literal meaning of the word Islam. Devotees face Mecca, the holiest city, and perform a standardised set of movements that involves standing, bowing, and prostrating with foreheads touching the ground. This physical posture of “*kneeling before the Lord of the Universe*” repeatedly and daily is designed to break the pride of the human ego and maintain oneself in the state of submission to God.

The word actually means purification; by giving away a portion of their wealth, Muslims purify the rest of their wealth and also are made to acknowledge that everything that they own actually belongs to God.

During the month of Ramaḍān, Muslims abstain from eating, drinking, smoking, and sexual activity from dawn until sunset. This month commemorates the time when the Qur’ān was first revealed to Muhammad. By controlling their basal bodily instincts, Muslims rise up beyond their appetites to become servants of God. It is a time for discipline, recitation of scripture, and empathy for the less fortunate.

A highlight of this process is circumambulating the **kaʿbah**, the black cube shrine that is placed in the centre of the Grand Mosque, believed to have been established by Abraham and his son Ishmael.

#### Jihād

No term in the world’s religions is as controversial as jihād. It is also sometimes called the unofficial sixth pillar of Islam. While often understood as “holy war,” the word literally means **“struggle”**. Traditionally, Islamic theology has understood this concept to encompass two distinct types of exertion:

1.  **The Spiritual Struggle:** This is the internal battle against
    pride and self-sufficiency. It is the fight against the “idol of the
    self” to maintain submission to Allah.

2.  **The Physical Struggle:** This is the external battle against the
    “house of war” (enemies of Islam). This struggle utilises various
    tactics and also includes war.

Apologists try to insulate Islam from extremism by claiming jihad is *only* a spiritual concept. However, the Quran mentions war, and repeatedly so.

- **2:190-91:** Commands believers to “fight,” “slay,” and “expel”.

- **2:216:** States that fighting is “prescribed for you, though it be
  hateful to you”.

- **9:5 (The “Sword Verse”):** Commands Muslims to “slay the idolaters
  wherever you find them”.

- **9:123:** “O believers, fight the unbelievers who are near to you”.

- **4:76:** “Fight you, therefore, against the friends of Satan”.

While translating jihād as “holy war” is technically incorrect, the plain sense of the term, both in scripture and in practice, includes a strong military dimension to it. While these war verses are historically situated in the conflicts of seventh century Arabia, every scripture (both religious and secular) is bound to its context; nevertheless, the persistent theme of armed struggle against disbelievers remains a structural component of the text that demands recognition. While the Christian New Testament is pacifist and largely ignores the topic of war (leaving Christians to invent “just war” theories later), the Quran explicitly promotes warfare and regulates it by providing a code of conduct for warfare.

### Does Islam mean peace?



Muslims sometimes claim that Islam is a religion of peace because the word “Islam” itself means “peace” etymologically in Arabic. Is it so? Not exactly. The word “Islam” in Arabic means “submission” and the word Muslim means “one who has submitted”. It is true as per Arabic grammar that the word “Islam” is etymologically related to the word “salam” which means “peace” and all these words come from the triliteral Arabic verb root S-L-M. But, the peace in this root also has a connotation in the context of handing over while surrendering in a war which in English is captured by the idiom “making peace”. So, the “peace” that is signified by the root S-L-M is the peace that is gotten by surrendering or handing over something in a war. The images below are from wiktionary (click [here](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/سلم#Arabic) to see) where the various meanings of the various grammatical forms (**awzān**) of the verb root can be seen. Clearly, the connotation is one of making peace in war that comes about by submitting oneself or surrendering oneself. In religious context, this then refers to the peace that is made by submitting to Allah and becoming a Muslim.

Hence, while the grammatical root S-L-M behind the words “Islām” and “Muslim” in Arabic, carries connotations of wholeness and safety conveyed by the English word “peace”, the specific form of the root behind the word Islām (and many other forms as well) designates active submission, resignation, or handing oneself over (historically used in classical Arabic for a captive or soldier laying down arms and surrendering to an opponent). In the religious context, that surrender is directed toward God.

    principles of goodness and justice.

11. And Banu-al-Aws shall be responsible for their own ward and shall
    pay their blood-money in mutual collaboration and every group shall
    secure the release of its own prisoners by paying their ransom, so
    that the dealings between the believers be in accordance with the
    principles of goodness and justice.

12. And the believers shall not leave any one, hard-pressed with debts,
    without affording him some relief, in order that the dealings
    between the believers be in accordance with the principles of
    goodness and justice.  
    Also, no believer shall enter into a contract of clientage with one
    who is already in such a contract with another believer.

13. And the hands of pious believers shall be raised against every such
    person as rises in rebellion or attempts to acquire anything by
    force or is guilty of any sin or excess or attempts to spread
    mischief among the believers; their hands shall be raised all
    together against such a person, even if he be a son to any one of
    them.

14. A Believer will not kill a Believer \[in retaliation\] for a
    non-Believer and will not aid a non-Believer against a Believer.

15. The protection (**dhimmah**) of Allah is one, the least of them
    \[i.e., the Believers\] is entitled to grant protection (**yujīr**)
    that is binding for all of them. The Believers are each other’s
    allies (**mawālī**) to the exclusion of other people.

16. And that those who will follow us among the Jews, will have help and
    equality. Neither shall they be oppressed nor will any help be given
    against them.

17. And the peace of the believers shall be one. If there be any war in
    the way of God, no believer shall be under any peace (with the
    enemy) apart from other believers, unless it (this peace) be the
    same and equally binding on all.

18. And all those detachments that will fight on our side will be
    relieved by turns.

19. And the believers as a body shall take blood vengeance in the way of
    God.

    1.  And undoubtedly pious believers are the best and in the rightest
        course.

    2.  And that no associator (non-Muslim subject) shall give any
        protection to the life and property of a Qurayshite, nor shall
        he come in the way of any believer in this matter.

20. And if any one intentionally murders a believer, and it is proved,
    he shall be killed in retaliation, unless the heir of the murdered
    person be satisfied with blood-money. And all believers shall
    actually stand for this ordinance and nothing else shall be proper
    for them to do.

21. And it shall not be lawful for any one, who has agreed to carry out
    the provisions laid down in this code and has affixed his faith in
    God and the Day of Judgment, to give help or protection to any
    murderer, and if he gives any help or protection to such a person,
    God‟s curse and wrath shall be on him on the Day of Resurrection,
    and no money or compensation shall be accepted from such a person.

22. And that whenever you differ about anything, refer it to God and to
    Muhammad

23. And the Jews shall share with the believers the expenses of war so
    long as they fight in conjunction,

24. And the Jews of Banu ‘Awf shall be considered as one community
    (*Ummat*) along with the believers—for the Jews their religion, and
    for the Muslims theirs, be one client or patron. But whoever does
    wrong or commits treachery brings evil only on himself and his
    household.

25. And the Jews of Banu-an-Najjar shall have the same rights as the
    Jews of Banu ‘Awf.

26. And the Jews of Banu-al-Harith shall have the same rights as the
    Jews of Banu ‘Awf.

27. And the Jews of Banu Sa‘ida shall have the same rights as the Jews
    of Banu ‘Awf

28. And the Jews of Banu Jusham shall have the same rights as the Jews
    of Banu ‘Awf.

29. And the Jews of Banu al-Aws shall have the same rights as the Jews
    of Banu ‘Awf.

30. And the Jews of Banu Tha‘laba shall have the same rights as the Jews
    of Banu ‘Awf. But whoever does wrong or commits treachery brings
    evil only on himself and his household.

31. And Jafna, who are a branch of the Tha’laba tribe, shall have the
    same rights as the mother tribes.

32. And Banu-ash-Shutaiba shall have the same rights as the Jews of Banu
    ‘Awf; and they shall be faithful to, and not violators of, treaty.

33. And the mawlas (clients) of Tha’laba shall have the same rights as
    those of the original members of it.

34. And the sub-branches of the Jewish tribes shall have the same rights
    as the mother tribes.

35. And that none of them shall go out to fight as a soldier of the
    Muslim army, without the permission of Muhammad.  
    And no obstruction shall be placed in the way of any one‟s
    retaliation for beating or injuries; and whoever sheds blood brings
    it upon himself and his household, except he who has been wronged,
    and Allah demands the most righteous fulfilment of this \[treaty\].

36. And the Jews shall bear the burden of their expenses and the Muslims
    theirs.

      
    And if any one fights against the people of this code, their (i.e.,
    of the Jews and Muslims) mutual help shall come into operation, and
    there shall be friendly counsel and sincere behaviour between them;
    and faithfulness and no breach of covenant.

37. And the Jews shall be bearing their own expenses so long as they
    shall be fighting in conjunction with the believers.

38. And the Valley of Yathrib (Madina) shall be a Haram (sacred place)
    for the people of this code.

39. The clients (mawla) shall have the same treatment as the original
    persons (i.e., persons accepting clientage). He shall neither be
    harmed nor shall he himself break the covenant.

40. And no refuge shall be given to any one without the permission of
    the people of the place (i.e., the refugee shall have no right of
    giving refuge to others).

41. And that if any murder or quarrel takes place among the people of
    this code, from which any trouble may be feared, it shall be
    referred to God and God’s Messenger, Muhammad; and God will be with
    him who will be most particular about what is written in this code
    and act on it most faithfully.

42. The Quraysh shall be given no protection nor shall they who help
    them.

43. And they (i.e., Jews and Muslims) shall have each other’s help in
    the event of any force invading Yathrib.

44. And if they (i.e., the Jews) are invited to any peace, they also
    shall offer peace and shall be a party to it; and if they invite the
    believers to some such affairs, it shall be their (Muslims) duty as
    well to reciprocate the dealings, excepting that any one makes a
    religious war.  
      
    On every group shall rest the responsibility of (repulsing) the
    enemy from the place which faces its part of the city.

45. And the Jews of the tribe of al-Aws, clients as well as original
    members, shall have the same rights as the people of this code: and
    shall behave sincerely and faithfully towards the latter, not
    perpetrating any breach of covenant. As one shall sow so shall he
    reap. And God is with him who will most sincerely and faithfully
    carry out the provisions of this code.

46. And this prescript shall not be of any avail to any oppressor or
    breaker of covenant. And one shall have security whether one goes
    out to a campaign or remains in Madina, or else it will be an
    oppression and breach of covenant. And God is the Protector of him
    who performs the obligations with faithfulness and care, as also His
    Messenger Muhammad

Thus, this concludes the survey of monotheism starting with an Egyptian Pharaoh and ending with the rise of Islam in Arabia. I know that both in the previous article on Christianity and this one on Islam, I have not talked in detail about the spread of the faith due to lack of space, and I intend to do so in the future in other articles because I think they are too vast to be condensed here. The subject of Islamic law (**sharī'ah**) too which is important, was not covered by me here as I believe that it deserves a dedicated subject rather than a condensed section.
