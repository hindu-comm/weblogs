There is a moment, somewhere around three thousand years ago, when a man in eastern Iran asks a question into the dark:

> “To what land to flee? Where shall I go to flee? They exclude (me)
> from my family and from my clan. The community with which I have
> associated has not satisfied me, nor those who are the deceitful
> rulers of the land. How, then, shall I satisfy Thee, Wise Lord?”

He goes on, in the next breath, to give his god an inventory of his poverty: “I know that (reason) because of which I am powerless, Wise One: by my condition of having few cattle, as well as (that) I am a person with few men. I lament to Thee. Take notice of it, Lord, offering the support which a friend should grant to a friend.”

A man with few cattle and fewer friends, thrown out by his own clan, complaining to his god the way you complain to someone who owes you money. That is Zarathustra in his own hymns, the seventeen songs called the Gathas, the oldest things in any Iranian language. Not a bearded lawgiver on a mountain. Not the founder of anything, yet. A working poet and priest in a cattle herding society, in trouble.

Almost everything commonly said about this man is said in vocabulary that did not exist when he lived. Prophet, religion, scripture, heaven, hell, faith: every one of those words carries a millennium of later luggage, most of it stamped in places Zarathustra never saw. He comes before. This essay reads him the way the evidence presents him: through his own verses and the world they assume, and through the scholars who spent their lives on the philology, Mary Boyce, Stanley Insler, Martin West, Helmut Humbach, Prods Oktor Skjærvø. Readers who know the Vedic world will find, again and again, that the fastest way to understand Zarathustra is to stop thinking of Moses and start thinking of a ṛṣi, a Vedic seer poet. That is not a devotional flourish. It is where the philology points.

## The world he was born into



Start with the language. The Gathas are composed in a tongue so close to the Sanskrit of the Rigveda that scholars can sometimes convert one into the other with sound laws alone. Boyce puts it plainly: “The language of the Gathas is archaic, and close to that of the Rigveda (whose composition has been assigned to about 1700 B.C. onwards); and the picture of the world to be gained from them is correspondingly ancient.” Skjærvø agrees that the Gathas and the oldest Rigvedic hymns share one horizon. How deep that horizon lies is its own scholarly argument, Boyce opening the Rigveda’s composition around 1700 BCE where others begin its oldest hymns nearer the middle of the second millennium. Either way the two poetries are contemporaries, and on the older reckoning their conversation opens in the seventeenth century before the common era.

That kinship is the master key to everything. The Iranians and the Vedic Indians were, not long before Zarathustra, one people with one poetry and one ritual system. The priest who pours the offering is hotṛ in the Veda and zaotar in the Avesta: the same word. The sacred order that holds the world together is ṛta in the Veda and aša in the Avesta: the same word. The glowing plant pressed for the ritual drink is soma in the Veda and haoma in the Avesta: the same word. The old title of the great lords among the gods is asura in the Veda and ahura in the Avesta: the same word. When Zarathustra addresses his god as Ahura Mazdā, the Wise Lord, a Vedic ear hears the asura called “wise,” and the Rigveda itself addresses Varuṇa in exactly those terms. The two traditions are estranged brothers, and they even quarrel like brothers: gods called daēva were declared demons in Iran, while in India the asuras slid, over centuries, from lords to demons. Each side kept the whole vocabulary and reversed half the signs.

The world of the Gathas: Central Asia and eastern Iran, 1500 to 1000 BCE.

The society behind the hymns is a herdsman’s world. Wealth is counted in cattle, the poem’s imagery runs on pasture and raid, and the fee a poet expects for his praise songs is livestock: in Yasna 44 Zarathustra pointedly reminds his god of “the ten mares with stallion, and the camel” that were promised him. West notes drily that this “assimilates the situation to that of the priest-poet who, like the Vedic Rishi, expects and receives substantial rewards of livestock from his patron.” The power in this world belongs to petty princes the hymns call kavi. In India kaví means the inspired poet sage. In Iran it had become a title of rulers, and most of them, along with the sacrificing priests called karapan, were Zarathustra’s enemies.

When did he live? A late tradition, preserved in Zoroastrian books, put Zoroaster “258 years before Alexander,” which yields a sixth century BCE date, and older scholarship repeats it. The philologists broadly abandoned that: a sixth century man does not compose in a language contemporary with the oldest Rigveda. Boyce argued for something like 1700 to 1500 BCE; a common middle position is the late second millennium. This essay does not need to settle it. What matters is the world the hymns themselves show: Bronze Age, pastoral, pre imperial, and far closer to the Rigveda than to the Persia of Cyrus.

## The man: a priest with a grievance



Zarathustra tells us his profession. “The priest who is just in harmony with truth is the offspring from the best spirit,” he sings in Yasna 33, speaking of himself; West’s comment on the verse is blunt: “he is an unimpeachable minister (zaotar-, the traditional word for the priest who poured out the libation).” Nigosian summarizes the situation exactly: “he calls himself a ‘zaotar,’ the Vedic ‘hotar’ meaning priest.” A zaotar was not a shepherd surprised by lightning. He was a trained professional, master of an intricate verse craft of fixed stanzas and counted syllables, kin to the meters of the Rigveda, formed by years of apprenticeship in a hereditary guild.

So the reformer came from inside the ritual system, and his instrument of reform was the system’s own high art: the praise hymn. This is why the ṛṣi comparison earns its keep. Like a Vedic seer, he composes metrical mantras to be sung in the presence of the sacred fire; like a Vedic seer, he expects his patron’s mares and camels; like a Vedic seer, he claims direct vision of the divine. And the vision passages are the most electric things in the Gathas. “I have already realized Thee to be virtuous, Wise Lord, when I saw Thee to be the First One at the creation of the world,” he sings in Yasna 43, and in Yasna 31: “I realized Thee to be (ever) young in mind, Wise One, when I grasped Thee in a vision to be the Father of good thinking.” A seer’s darśana, his direct sight, of god at the world’s beginning, put into first person verse.

The strangest and most intimate moment comes in Yasna 43, where the direction of speech reverses. The god’s spirit approaches and asks: “Who art thou? To which side dost thou belong?” And the man answers with his own name: “(I am) Zarathustra.” Nothing in later Zoroastrian tradition, and little in any scripture anywhere, sounds like that exchange: a god running an identity check, a poet answering like a man at a border crossing. The Gathas are full of this two way traffic. Yasna 44 is built entirely as a chain of questions, each stanza opening with the same formula, “This I ask Thee. Tell me truly, Lord”: who fixed the path of the sun and stars, who holds the earth below and the sky from falling, who yoked speed to horses. The form has a close Vedic cousin in the hymn where Vasiṣṭha questions Varuṇa about his guilt, and West traces the question formula itself to poetic habits the two traditions inherited together. India never let go of the form. Centuries later the Bhagavad Gītā was built on the same frame, one man and one god in conversation on the edge of a decision, though there it is the god who gives the answers.

## The reform, point by point



What did this priest actually change? Six things, each visible in the hymns themselves. Take them one at a time.

**1. One wise Lord, with a family of powers.** The Gathas are addressed overwhelmingly to one god, Ahura Mazdā, the Wise Lord, maker and orderer of everything. Around him stand a small family of capitalized qualities: Good Thought, Best Order, Desirable Dominion, Devotion, Wholeness, Immortality. Later tradition would count them as the Amesha Spentas, the Life giving Immortals, and assign each a creation to guard. In the Gathas they are still fluid, aspects of the god, virtues of the worshipper, and divine persons all at once, and sometimes literally his household:

> “I know the Wise One who created it to be the Father of effective good
> thinking. And His daughter is piety of good actions. The All-viewing
> Lord is not to be deceived.” (Yasna 45, Insler)

Zarathustra says he will worship these powers “with their own names” and serve them “with love.” One supreme lord, a family of powers at once distinct from him and identical with him: the architecture will feel familiar to anyone raised on the Indian side of the family.

**2. A universe sorted by Order, entered by choice.** One word rules the system: aša, cosmic Order, cognate with Vedic ṛta. Everything falls under aša or under its enemy, druj, the Lie. And the sorting is done by decision. The famous verses of Yasna 30 stage the drama: two primal spirits, one choosing rightly and one not, and then the instruction to the audience to reflect with a clear mind, “man by man for himself,” upon “the two choices of decision.” Humbach’s note on the passage insists the choice is existential rather than mythological. This is the piece of Zarathustra with no good Vedic parallel, and it may be his most consequential invention: a religion whose entry requirement is a decision. Every membership religion since has walked through the door he cut.

**3. The cow gets legal standing in heaven.** Yasna 29 may be the strangest opening scene in any sacred poetry. Before the reform is announced, before the poet even appears, the soul of the cow files a complaint in the divine court:

> “To all of you the soul of the cow lamented: ‘For whom did ye shape
> me? Who fashioned me? (For) the cruelty of fury and violence, of
> bondage and might, holds me in captivity. I have no pastor other than
> you. Therefore appear to me with good pasturage.’” (Yasna 29, Insler)

Humbach’s note explains what “the soul of the cow” means: not one animal but the soul of the species itself, and in this scene specifically the cow that the deceitful have seized to kill for sacrifice. The court deliberates. The Wise Lord answers with a ruling about what the cow is actually for:

> “Milk and butter, this is the prescript that the Lord, of one mind
> with Right, made for the cow.” (Yasna 29, West)

West’s comment is even blunter than his translation: “The cow is there to provide dairy products (not meat, as the sacrificers think).” And who will protect her? The court’s answer is not a warrior but a poet, Zarathustra, “the only one who has given ear to our commandments.” The cow is unimpressed:

> “But thereupon the soul of the cow wept: ‘I who have recognized that
> my caretaker is powerless, (merely) the voice of a man without might,
> although I wish him to be one who possesses rule through power...’”
> (Yasna 29, Insler)

She wanted a king and got a poet, which is either the most human touch in ancient literature or its driest joke. Elsewhere the hymns indict the whole religious establishment by name for what it does to her: the karapan sacrificing priests and the kavi princes “have delivered the cow unto fury” and “have not been eager to prosper her and her pasturage with truth” (Yasna 44, Insler). For Indian readers this should land with a shock of recognition. The Rigveda already calls the cow aghnyā, “she who is not to be killed,” and the Purāṇas would later stage the same courtroom, the earth in the form of a weeping cow appealing to Viṣṇu for protection. The oldest layers of both civilizations put the mistreated cow at the moral center of the world, and each built a religion that answered her. Honesty note: this is protection from cruelty and wanton slaughter, not vegetarianism; Zoroastrian ritual went on offering animals for millennia, and scholars still argue over exactly what practice Zarathustra was condemning. But that the cow’s suffering is the opening grievance of the entire Gathic revelation is not an interpretation. It is the text.

**4. The sacred drink called by its real name.** The haoma is the pressed ritual intoxicant of the old Indo Iranian cult, the same word and the same rite as the Vedic soma, which the Rigveda celebrates with an entire book of hymns. Zarathustra’s hymns treat it as the fuel of everything he hates, the drink at whose ceremony “the cow is to be killed” (Yasna 32, Insler), and his question about it is one of the angriest in the Gathas:

> “When, Wise One, shall men desist from murdering? When shall they fear
> the folly of that intoxicating drink, through the effects of which the
> Karpans as well as the evil rulers of the lands torture our (good)
> intentions in an evil way?” (Yasna 48, Insler)

West translates the drink’s epithet in that verse without ceremony: “this liquor piss.” Humbach reads “this urine-like intoxicant.” Three philologists, one verdict on the tone: contempt. There is a nuance worth keeping, and Humbach states it: the polemic may target the misuse of haoma, the drunkenness and the cow slaughter around its rite, rather than the plant itself. Either way, what happened next is the great irony of Zoroastrian history. The Younger Avesta rehabilitated haoma completely; in the later texts the same word names the death averting drink of immortality, with its own hymn of praise, and haoma pressing stands at the center of Zoroastrian ritual to this day. The reformer’s angriest verse lost to the liturgy he came from. Indian readers may permit themselves a smile here: the cousin religion also kept its soma, whatever the seers’ quarrels over it, long after anyone remembered which plant it was.

**5. The offering turned inward.** Zarathustra does not abolish sacrifice; he redirects it. What his god eats is the worshipper’s own integrity:

> “Yes, both completeness and immortality are for Thy sustenance.”
> (Yasna 34, Insler)

Insler’s footnote spells out the theology: the completeness and immortality created by the faith and devotion of the truthful “is the only sustenance which Ahura Mazda requires.” In Yasna 33 the poet puts himself on the altar and offers “the breath of even his own person.” Readers of the Gītā will place this move instantly: the sacrifice internalized, the leaf and flower and water that God actually eats, patraṁ puṣpaṁ phalaṁ toyam, in second millennium Iranian dress.

**6. The old war gods dethroned.** The daēvas, the gods of raid and intoxicated frenzy, the Indra type, are cast out as false. The Rigveda itself preserves the fork in the road, in the extraordinary hymn where Varuṇa and Indra state their rival claims in turn. Boyce sets the two speeches side by side. Varuṇa:

> “Lordship belongs indeed to me, the perpetual sovereign, as all the
> Immortals (acknowledge) to us... I let the dripping waters rise up,
> through rta I uphold the sky. Through rta I am the lord who rules
> through rta.” (Rigveda 4.42, in Boyce’s rendering)

And Indra:

> “Men who drive swiftly, having good horses, call on me when surrounded
> in battle. I provoke strife, I the bountiful Indra. I whirl up the
> dust, my strength is overwhelming... When draughts of soma, when songs
> have made me drunk, then both the unbounded regions grow afraid.”
> (Rigveda 4.42, in Boyce’s rendering)

The sovereign of Order against the intoxicated storm of battle: the whole Indo Iranian religious crisis in one hymn’s debate. India held both claims in tension for centuries and worked out its own settlements. Iran, in one poet’s lifetime, ruled for the sovereign by decree, and sent Indra’s kind into the outer darkness under the name daēva.

## Exile, the patron, and the price



The reform got him expelled. That is the meaning of the verse this essay opened with: excluded from family and clan, unsatisfied by the community, harassed by “the deceitful rulers of the land.” A herding society has no police and no priestly councils to appeal to; a priest who declares the princes’ gods false has exactly one option, which is to find a prince of his own.

He found one. The hymns name him with gratitude: “Which truthful person is thy committed friend for the great task? ... Yes, it is Kavi Vishtaspa, with whom thou art allied.” And again: “Kavi Vishtaspa reached this understanding of our task, which he respected in harmony with truth.” One kavi, one prince of the old poet king title, took the exile in, and the entire subsequent history of the religion hangs on that single act of Bronze Age patronage.

Vīštāspa deserves a longer look than history usually gives him, because he is the first royal convert of a world religion whose name we actually know, and the Gathas let us watch the conversion pay for itself. His name is a herdsman aristocrat’s name: it contains aspa, horse, the same word as Sanskrit aśva, and princes across both sister cultures wore horse names like regimental colors. That he kept the title kavi matters too. The other kavis are the villains of the Gathas; Vīštāspa is the one poet prince who crossed over, which is perhaps why the tradition never dropped his title even after the title’s whole class had been damned. Around him the hymns show a court taking shape. Frashaoshtra of the Hvōgva family is summoned by name, “come thou hither together with those sincere men whom we two wish to be in His favor”; Insler’s note identifies Frashaoshtra and his brother Jāmāspa as noblemen of Vīštāspa’s circle, and tradition makes Jāmāspa the prince’s adviser and, eventually, the husband of Zarathustra’s own daughter. The last of the seventeen hymns is that wedding’s song: in Yasna 53 the poet marries Pourucistā, “young one among Zarathustra’s daughters,” into the Hvōgva line, with Vīštāspa and Frashaoshtra named among the company. Reform, patron, marriage alliances: not an institution being founded but a kinship network being welded, which is exactly how anything durable was built in the Bronze Age. Later ages repaid Vīštāspa with an afterlife nearly as large as the poet’s. The Younger Avesta made him the last of the Kayanian line of kings; Ferdowsi’s Shahnameh remembers him as Goshtasp. And in one of history’s small ambushes, the same name returns a thousand years later as Hystaspes, the father of Darius the Great, a coincidence of aristocratic naming that may have helped ancient chronographers confuse the two men and drag the poet forward in time by a full millennium.

The community that formed around poet and patron appears in the Gathas under the word maga, a bond or exchange of gifts between god, singer, and sponsor, and some scholars have connected it to the same word family as the later magu, from which Greek gave us “magus” and every language got “magic.”

What did the poet promise in return? Healing, in the largest sense. His words for the coming helpers of the world are plural and modest: the saošyants, “strengtheners,” a title any righteous person could earn. “Those men shall be the saviors of the lands,” he sings of them, “fated to be the expellers of fury.” Once he begs his god for a single such figure, a “world-healer.” And he speaks of the great collective project in a verb that later doctrine would turn into a noun: “May we be the ones who will make this world splendid.” Frašō.kərəti, the Making Splendid, the renovation of the universe, one of the most influential ideas in the history of religions, sits in the Gathas as a first person plural wish. Not an apocalypse to wait for. A job to do.

He also told his followers what their choices would cost them. The dead, in his hymns, come to a crossing he calls the Bridge of the Judge: “with all these I shall cross over the Bridge of the Judge,” he sings of himself and his companions, while the souls of the deceitful, arriving at the same span, are turned away by their own consciences toward “the House of Deceit.” Requital comes “with the molten iron” at existence’s final turn. Judgment after death, weighed on conduct rather than on offerings, each soul alone at the crossing: the whole machinery that later traditions across half the world would inherit is sketched in these verses, in miniature, by a poet worried about his neighbors’ choices.

And the plural, human “strengtheners” of his hymns grew, in the centuries after him, into something more baroque. Later Avestan tradition expected a single wondrous Saošyant at the end of the age, born of the poet’s own preserved seed when a maiden bathes in a lake, arriving to drive the Lie out of the world once and for all. The legend is the tradition’s, and the tradition’s alone; but notice what it did. It took a job title that any righteous herdsman could hold and turned it into a miracle to wait for. Every reform, given enough centuries, is reabsorbed by the mythology it interrupted. Zarathustra’s was no exception, and the same centuries dressed the man himself in wonders: a mother who conceived him at fifteen in virginity, a laugh instead of a cry at birth. The working priest with few cattle disappeared behind the gold and legend of his own tradition, and it took modern philology to find him again.

## What happened to his god



Zarathustra’s own story ends offstage; the Gathas do not narrate his death, and the legends (the virgin conception, the laugh, murder at the altar at seventy seven) are exactly that, legends, and late ones. What can be followed in evidence is the career of his god, and it is one of the great political stories of antiquity.

Roughly a millennium after the poet, a dynasty from southwestern Iran built the largest empire the world had yet seen, and its kings carved his god’s name at the top of their proclamations. Darius at Behistun credits every victory to Ahuramazdā. Boyce relayed the suggestion that the six noble Persians who stand with Darius on his tomb sculpture mirror the six Immortals around the Wise Lord: the king as the seventh figure of an earthly heptad, ruling as Ahuramazdā’s counterpart below. And Xerxes, his son, left the most Zarathustrian paragraph any king ever published, the so called daiva inscription found at Persepolis: “there was (a place) where previously the Daivas were worshipped. Afterwards, by the grace of Ahuramazda I destroyed that sanctuary of the Daivas, and I proclaimed: ‘The Daivas shall not be worshipped!’ Where previously the Daivas were worshipped, there I worshipped Ahuramazda with proper rites, in accordance with asha.” The poet’s war on the daēvas, fought with verses in the eastern pastures, reappears centuries later as imperial policy in the west, in stone. Whether Cyrus and Darius were Zoroastrians in any strict sense is a real scholarly fight, and this essay flags it as one; that their dynasty spoke the reformer’s language of Mazdā, aša, and the daivas is simply what the inscriptions say.

The dynasty’s later kings show the old inheritance still shifting underfoot. Artaxerxes II set a triad at the head of his inscriptions, Ahuramazdā, Anāhitā, Mithra, and Boyce traced what that promotion likely cost: the river goddess Anāhitā “came to be accorded, in Achaemenian worship, the place of the third Ahura, the ‘Son of the Waters,’ \*Varuna Apąm Napat. Thereafter \*Varuna faded into the background in popular devotion, although he retains his place to this day in Zoroastrian priestly rituals and prayers.” Readers on the Indian side should feel the rhyme in their bones: Varuṇa, the wise asura whom the Rigveda addresses in the very syllables of Ahura Mazdā’s name, faded in India too, shrinking from cosmic sovereign to god of the waters. The same great god lost the same throne twice, once in each brother civilization.

The story keeps moving east again after Alexander. On the coins of the Kushan emperors who ruled from Central Asia into India, the largest group of gods is Zoroastrian: Asha Vahishta and Khshathra Vairya, Mithra and Verethraghna, the divine Glory itself as Pharro, riding royal money through the Buddhist heartlands, side by side with the Buddha. The two estranged brothers of the Indo Iranian family, having reversed each other’s gods a thousand years before, ended up jingling together in the same purse.

## The Rishi reading



Put the pieces in one frame. A hereditary priest of the old Indo Iranian ritual, master of the mantra craft, has a vision of the one wise Lord behind the world’s order. He recasts the inherited religion around that vision: one god, a family of luminous qualities, a universe sorted by Order against the Lie, entry by individual choice, sacrifice redirected from slaughter and intoxication toward devotion, the old war gods dethroned. He is exiled for it, finds a royal patron, founds a community bound by gift exchange, and promises that righteous men, plural, will heal and eventually make splendid the world. His hymns are memorized, syllable by syllable, for well over a thousand years before anyone writes them down, by priests who no longer fully understand the dialect, which is precisely why we can trust them: you do not smoothly forge in a language you cannot speak.

Every element of that portrait has its closest kin in India. The form of his hymns is Vedic. His job title is Vedic. His god’s title is Vedic. His central concept is Vedic ṛta under another pronunciation. His fee schedule is Vedic. His question dialogue with god has a Vasiṣṭha hymn for a twin and the Gītā for a distant descendant. Even his cow appealing to heaven has her sisters in the Purāṇas, where the earth in cow form comes weeping to Viṣṇu. The Western habit of reading him as a proto biblical prophet gets the man structurally wrong: prophets are seized by a word from outside; this man was a professional seer who worked in meter and billed in mares. Kellens and Skjærvø built the modern scholarly case for exactly this reading, and the Gathas reward it on every page.

He asked his god, in one hymn, for ten mares with a stallion and a camel. What his god eventually collected was the largest empire on earth, an ethics staked on each soul’s own choice, the idea of a world made splendid at history’s end, and a fire that his priests, from Yazd to Mumbai, have not let die. The exchange rate on Bronze Age poetry has never been matched.

  Yasna 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 43, 44, 45, 46, 48, 51, 53.

- M. L. West, *The Hymns of Zoroaster* (2010). Three quotations follow
  West: the mares and camel of Yasna 44.18, the milk and butter
  prescript of Yasna 29.7, and “make this world splendid” in Yasna 30.9.
  His comments on Yasna 29.7, 33.6 and 44.18 are quoted as marked.

- Helmut Humbach and Pallan Ichaporia, *The Heritage of Zarathushtra*
  (1994). Renderings compared throughout; his notes on Yasna 29.1, 32.14
  and 48.10 and his remark on the two spirits are cited as marked.

- Mary Boyce, *Zoroastrians: Their Religious Beliefs and Practices* and
  *A History of Zoroastrianism* Vol. II. Historical backbone of the
  Achaemenid section; the Varuṇa and Indra speeches of Rigveda 4.42 and
  the Xerxes daiva inscription (Persepolis H) are given in her
  renderings; the Kushan coin pantheon follows her account.

- Prods Oktor Skjærvø, *The Spirit of Zoroastrianism*. Dating of the
  Gathic horizon and comparative material.

- S. A. Nigosian, *Indo Aryan Religion in Achaemenid Persia* (1970).
  Used with caution on chronology.

- The rishi reading of Zarathustra follows the school of Jean Kellens
  and Skjærvø.

- On the disputed date of Zarathustra: Boyce argues for the early second
  millennium BCE against the traditional “258 years before Alexander”
  reckoning; the disagreement is flagged in the text.
