One would have seen Dakshinamurthy in Shiva temples as one of the Koshta Devatas, usually occupying the southern niche of the sanctum. Dakshinamurthy represents the Guru aspect of Shiva and is therefore depicted as a youthful ascetic seated beneath a banyan tree, imparting wisdom to sages.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!nF40!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffe58c4b1-5d2f-49fe-aa80-a182a4e4e4fe_429x715.heic)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!nF40!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffe58c4b1-5d2f-49fe-aa80-a182a4e4e4fe_429x715.heic)





In the conventional iconography, His lower right hand displays the Jnana Mudra, while the upper right hand holds a Japa Mala. The upper left hand carries a flame, symbolising the destruction of ignorance, and the lower left hand holds a palm-leaf manuscript representing sacred knowledge. His right foot is firmly placed upon the demon Apasmara, the embodiment of ignorance, while the left leg is folded in a yogic posture.

However, there exists a unique Dakshinamurthy image in the Adipurisvara Temple at Thiruvottiyur near Chennai that departs significantly from this familiar iconography. Here, Dakshinamurthy is seated in Padmasana and possesses four arms. He wears a lofty Jata Makuta, with wide-open eyes and a prominently displayed third eye. In the upper right hand He holds a Trishula (the upper portion of which is now broken), while the lower right hand is shown in Vyakhyana Mudra, the gesture of exposition and teaching. The upper left hand carries a Kapala, and the lower left hand is held across the chest. He is adorned with an Upavita and two distinct necklaces.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!6l--!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F54160410-2cb3-43ef-a9bd-84d29ab515d0_1011x1555.heic)





Owing to the seated posture, the teaching gesture, and the damaged Trishula, several scholars identified this image as Lakulisa, the founder of the Pasupata sect of Saivism, assuming that the broken Trishula was originally a Danda (staff). However, a similar sculpture within the same temple clearly preserves the Trishula, removing any doubt regarding the attribute held in the upper right hand. The image is therefore not Lakulisa.

This raises an intriguing question: why was Dakshinamurthy represented in such an unusual form at Thiruvottiyur? The answer lies in the rich and diverse Saiva traditions that flourished in medieval Tamil Nadu, particularly the influence of the Soma Siddhanta and Kalamukha schools, which appear to have exercised considerable influence over the temple during the Chola period. The unique iconography of this Dakshinamurthy may well preserve the memory of these now-forgotten Saiva traditions.

Thiruvottiyur is one of the most ancient Saiva centres of Tamil Nadu, with its history extending back at least to the 6th century CE. Epigraphical evidence reveals that the temple was not merely a place of worship but also an important centre of learning and ascetic traditions.

One of the earliest references comes from an inscription of the Rashtrakuta king Kannaradeva (Krishna III), who ruled parts of northern Tamil Nadu after defeating the Chola prince Rajaditya. This inscription mentions a Matha associated with a certain Chaturanana Pandita. It is quite likely that this ascetic-scholar played a significant role in the renovation and development of the temple. Over time, the heads of this institution appear to have inherited the title “Chaturanana Pandita,” resulting in a succession of ascetics bearing the same designation.

The importance of this lineage is further evident from an inscription of Rajendra Chola I, which records the reconstruction of the temple in black granite as a grand Tritala Vimana.

चतुराननचोदितेन तेन त्रितलन्निर्गतदूषणं विमानम्(।)

रविणाधिपुरीशस्य शम्भोः कृतमेत(द्व)रवीरचोळतक्ष्णा।।

The work was executed by the architect Ravi, also known as Virachola Taksha, under royal patronage of Chaturanana Pandita. This demonstrates the considerable influence exercised by the ascetic establishment in shaping the temple’s architectural development.

Further insight into the intellectual life of the temple comes from an inscription of Rajadhiraja Chola II. It records the presence of a Chaturanana Pandita and a Vagisa Pandita attending a temple festival along with the king. Significantly, Vagisa Pandita is described as expounding the Saiva treatise known as Somasiddhanta. This reference is of great importance because the Somasiddhanta tradition has been examined in the book “*The Kapalikas and Kalamukhas”* by David Lorenzen. The association of Vagisa Pandita with the teaching of Somasiddhanta strongly suggests that the Kalamukha tradition enjoyed considerable influence at Thiruvottiyur during the Chola period.

The Kalamukhas were an influential Saiva sect that originated in Karnataka and enjoyed considerable prominence in Tamilakam during the Chola period. Along with the Pasupatas, they are referred to as Mahavratins in several Chola inscriptions. Many Chola rulers appear to have been patrons of these ascetic Saiva traditions, while certain kings are known to be the followers of Pasupata order.

The theological foundation of the Kalamukha tradition, often identified with the Soma Siddhanta, rests on the recognition of three eternal entities — Pati (the Lord), Pasu (the individual soul), and Pasa (bondage). For the Kalamukhas, Rudra-Mahadeva was the Supreme Lord and manifested Himself in Dakshinamurti.

Seen in this context, the unusual image at Thiruvottiyur becomes easier to understand. The presence of Vagisa Pandita expounding Soma Siddhanta at the temple, coupled with the strong influence of the Chaturanana Pandita Matha, suggests that the Dakshinamurti enshrined here represents a distinctly Kalamukha interpretation of the deity. The image is therefore not an anomalous form, but rather a visual expression of the theological beliefs that once flourished within the temple.

At the same time, the iconography also reveals the close interaction between various Mahavratin traditions. The historical links between the Kalamukhas and the Pasupatas are well attested, and this may explain the resemblance of the image to Lakulisa, the great Pasupata teacher. The seated posture, teaching gesture and ascetic character of the image appear to reflect this shared spiritual heritage.

There are also unmistakable traces of Kapalika influence. The deity is shown wearing sphatika kundalas and two prominent necklaces. Significantly, Ramanuja, while discussing heterodox Saiva sects, refers to the Kantika and Rucaka ornaments as characteristic features of the Kapalikas. The presence of these attributes suggests that the sculptor consciously incorporated iconographic elements associated with the Kapalika tradition as well.

The Thiruvottiyur Dakshinamurti thus stands as a remarkable synthesis of several medieval Saiva currents. It preserves within a single image the influences of the Kalamukha, Pasupata and Kapalika traditions, all of which were collectively known as Mahavratin sects. Alongside the more familiar Saiva Siddhanta tradition, these ascetic schools occupied an important place in the religious landscape of the Chola age. The sculpture therefore serves not merely as an object of worship, but as a rare historical testimony to the rich diversity of Saivism that flourished in medieval Tamil Nadu.
