The scene could have come straight out of a gripping historical film. On the morning of 17 June 1911, the Tirunelveli–Maniyachi Mail stood at the second platform of Maniyachi Junction, awaiting the arrival of the Indo-Ceylon Boat Mail from Thoothukudi. Tirunelveli District Collector Robert William d’Escourt Ashe and his wife, Mary, were seated in a first-class compartment. They were travelling to Kodaikanal, where their children were already spending the summer vacation.

A young man, barely twenty-five, approached the compartment. Dressed in a traditional panchakachcham and a coat, he appeared outwardly calm. He entered the carriage and reportedly greeted the Collector: “Good morning, Ashe.” Irritated by the intrusion, Ashe ordered him to leave. But the young man did not move. Instead, he drew a concealed Browning automatic pistol and fired at Ashe from point-blank range. The Collector collapsed before his horrified wife.

The person who shot Ashe, immediately leapt from the compartment and ran along the platform. Those who attempted to pursue him hesitated when they saw that he was still armed. He first entered a lavatory, realised that it was meant for women, rushed out and then locked himself inside the men’s lavatory. Moments later, a gunshot rang out. When the door was opened, he was found dead. He had placed the pistol in his mouth and fired, choosing death rather than capture. The weapon was still in his hand.

When police came, they recovered this letter from his pocket.

*I dedicate my life as a small contribution to my motherland. I am alone responsible for this.*

*The [mlechas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mleccha) of England having captured our country, tread over the sanathana dharma of the Hindus and destroy them. Every Indian is trying to drive out the English and get swarajyam and restore sanathana dharma. Our Raman, Sivaji, Krishnan, Guru Govindan, Arjuna ruled our land protecting all dharmas, but in this land, they are making arrangements to crown George V, a [mlecha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mleccha), and one who eats the flesh of cows.*

*Three thousand Madrasis have taken a vow to kill George V as soon as he lands in our country. In order to make others know our intention, I who am the least in the company, have done this deed this day. This is what everyone in Hindustan should consider it as his duty.*

*I will kill Ashe, whose arrival here is to celebrate the crowning of cow-eater King George V in this glorious land which was once ruled by great samrats. This I do to make them understand the fate of those who cherish the thought of enslaving this sacred land.*

*I, as the least of them, wish to warn George by killing Ashe.*

*VandeMataram. VandeMataram. VandeMataram*

*R. Vanchi Iyer, Shencottah*

Vanchinathan was born in 1886 at Sengottai, which was then part of the princely state of Travancore. Born to Raghupathi Iyer and Rukmini Ammal, he was named as Sankaran though everyone called him as Vanchi. His father served in the Sengottai temple establishment, reportedly as a “maniyam” or temple clerk. Vanchi was the eldest son in a family of two sons and four daughters.

After completing his early education at Sengottai, Vanchi pursued higher studies in Thiruvananthapuram. He married Ponnammal when he was about twenty-three and later secured employment in the Forest Department of the Travancore government at Punalur. He knew Malayalam, English, Sanskrit and Hindi, in addition to his mother tongue, Tamil.

**Tirunelveli upheavals**

Although Vanchi lived and worked in Travancore, he was deeply influenced by the political upheavals unfolding in the neighbouring districts of Tirunelveli and Thoothukudi.

At the centre of this nationalist awakening stood V.O. Chidambaram Pillai. Through the Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company, V.O.C. mounted a direct commercial challenge to the monopoly of the British India Steam Navigation Company on the Thoothukudi–Colombo route. His successful leadership of the Coral Mill workers’ strike further strengthened his position as a mass leader. These activities alarmed the colonial administration, which increasingly regarded him as a serious political threat.

The release of the nationalist leader Bipin Chandra Pal was to be celebrated as \*Swarajya Day\* in March 1908. Despite the administration’s refusal to grant permission, V.O.C., Subramania Siva and Padmanabha Iyengar proceeded with nationalist meetings and speeches. They were arrested on 12 March 1908 on charges of sedition. Ashe was then serving as the Sub-Collector of Thoothukudi and was closely involved in the colonial administration’s campaign against the Swadeshi movement. The formal arrests were made under the authority of the district administration headed by Collector L.M. Wynch.

The arrests triggered widespread protests in Tirunelveli and Thoothukudi. Government buildings were attacked, telegraph lines were damaged and the unrest was met with police firing and severe repression. Ashe was in the forefront of the use of brutal force against the freedom fighters. Four Indians were killed. Punitive measures were subsequently imposed upon the population, while V.O.C. and Subramania Siva received extraordinarily harsh sentences. Ashe tried his best to bring down the the Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company. Denial of port facilities, delays in customs and medical clearances, pressure upon merchants and shareholders, and official support for its British competitor eventually crippled the indigenous enterprise.

Because of his tyrannical actions, Ashe came to be regarded by the revolutionaries as one of the principal instruments of this repression. His subsequent elevation as Collector of Tirunelveli intensified the resentment against him.

**Bharatha Matha Sangam**

Vanchinathan was among those who burned with anger at these developments. Determined to contribute actively to the struggle, he came into contact with the revolutionary organiser Erukkur Neelakanta Brahmachari. A journalist and associate of nationalist circles in Pondicherry, Neelakanta Brahmachari had begun organising young men through a secret revolutionary body generally known as the Bharatha Matha Sangam

One of the important gatherings of the organisation was held at Tenkasi in the house of “Madathukkadai” Chidambaram Pillai. Among those associated with the revolutionary circle were Sankarakrishna Iyer, Thoothukudi Arumugam Pillai, “Vande Mataram” Subramania Iyer, Sengottai Dharmaraja Iyer, Ottapidaram Madasamy Pillai and Alappuzha Harihara Iyer. These men formed part of a wider underground network extending through Tirunelveli, Thoothukudi, Sengottai, Punalur, Alappuzha and French-controlled Pondicherry.

Addressing the gathering, Neelakanta Brahmachari reportedly spoke passionately against the tyranny of British rule. Revolutionary organisations had already emerged in Bengal and other parts of northern India, he declared, and their members were prepared to sacrifice everything for the liberation of the country. The South, however, lacked a comparable revolutionary movement. It was therefore their duty to create one.

The members vowed to dedicate themselves to the overthrow of British rule and to preserve the secrecy of the organisation even at the cost of their lives.

**VVS Iyer and Vanchi**

Subsequently, Neelakanta Brahmachari and Vanchinathan travelled to Puducherry, then a French possession and therefore beyond the immediate reach of the British Indian police. Puducherry had become an important refuge and meeting place for Indian nationalists and revolutionaries. From there, they discussed their future course of action and maintained contact with members of the Bharatha Matha Sangam.

It was during this period that Vanchi came into close contact with V.V.S. Iyer. Iyer had taken refuge in Puducherry after the British authorities issued a warrant for his arrest in connection with his revolutionary activities abroad. While in London, he had been associated with India House, had worked closely with Veer Savarkar and had become connected with the revolutionary organisation Abhinav Bharat.

Iyer’s militant outlook made a powerful impression on Vanchi. Iyer trained Vanchi in the handling of firearms and in revolver practice.

After returning to Sengottai, Vanchi became increasingly active in expanding the Bharatha Matha Sangam. New members were recruited, and meetings were held at different places to discuss strategies for resisting British rule.

The members were particularly enraged by the severe treatment meted out to V.O. Chidambaram Pillai and Subramania Siva in prison. Reports that V.O.C. had been placed in chains and forced to perform gruelling prison labour, including turning an oil press, deeply shocked the nationalist public. The association sent him a threatening communication warning him to desist from his oppressive actions.

Amid these political developments, tragedy struck Vanchi’s personal life. His wife, Ponnammal, gave birth to a daughter, but the child died soon afterwards. Vanchi’s father informed him of the death and asked him to return home to perform the final rites. Vanchi refused, declaring that he was occupied with service to the nation.

As British surveillance of the Bharatha Matha Sangam intensified, several of its members came under suspicion. Fearing arrest, Vanchi once again travelled to Puducherry, where he renewed his association with V.V.S. Iyer and other nationalist exiles.

Around this time, differences emerged between Neelakanta Brahmachari and V.V.S. Iyer over the direction and pace of the revolutionary movement. Iyer favoured more immediate and decisive armed action, while Neelakanta appears to have become increasingly cautious about undertaking an assassination that could expose and destroy the entire organisation. Heated arguments between them over the methods to be adopted against British rule made them to part ways.

The dispute also affected Neelakanta’s relationship with Vanchi. Neelakanta attempted to restrain him from acting under Iyer’s influence, but Vanchi refused to distance himself from Iyer.

His anger over the suppression of the Swadeshi movement, his firearms training and his growing conviction that symbolic protests were no longer sufficient gradually brought him towards an irreversible decision. The revolutionary network had begun to identify Collector Ashe as the embodiment of colonial repression in Tirunelveli. Vanchi was now prepared to translate that anger into action.

V.V.S. Iyer impressed upon Vanchi the urgency of undertaking decisive action. King George V was expected to visit India later that year for the Delhi Durbar, where his coronation as Emperor of India would be formally proclaimed. To militant nationalists, the ceremonial celebration of a foreign monarch’s sovereignty over Bharat was a humiliating reminder of the country’s enslavement.

Iyer declared that Bharata Matha could no longer be allowed to remain in bondage. Vanchi replied that he had already resigned from his position in the Travancore Forest Department and that he now considered service to the nation his only duty. He spoke of avenging the persecution of V.O. Chidambaram Pillai and Subramania Siva and revealed that he intended to assassinate Collector Ashe.

Vanchi’s associates asked him to demonstrate his marksmanship by shooting a bird perched on a tree. Vanchi refused to kill an innocent living creature merely to prove his ability. Instead, he fired at a scarecrow demonstrated the accuracy he had acquired during his firearms training.

**D-Day**

He then returned to Sengottai and met with his friends Madathukkadai Pillai, Madasamy and Muthusamy. Vanchi told them about his plan to shoot Ashe and his friends agreed. One of his other friend, Sankarakrishnan informed about Ashe’s plan of going to Kodaikanal on June 17th. Vanchi along with Sanakarakrishnan and Muthusamy visited a Devadasi named Thayi, in Tirunelveli to confirm this. Her house was frequented by the government servants and she in fact confirmed that both Ashe and his wife were travelling to Kodaikanal in the 9:30 AM train.

1911, June 17th Saturday. Vanchi sent Sankarakrishnan to the collector office to inform him about the departure of collector. He completed his breakfast. Sankarakrishnan came and he got the tickets for four of them in the same Mail. Vanchi, Sankarakrishnan, Madathukkadai Chidambaram Pillai and Muthusamy all boarded the train in the second class while the collector and his wife were in the first class. When the train halted at Maniyachi, Vanchi went into the compartment while Sankarakrishnan was standing near the door. Both Pillai and Muthusamy were standing in the platform. After hearing the gun shots, all the three went into different directions. Madathukkadai Chidambaram Pillai’s wife in fact, gave brith to a baby on that same day. But he couldn’t see her as he was on the run. Soon police captured all of them including the key members of Bharatha Matha Sangam.

Madasamy, who was associated with the Sangam, evaded arrest and disappeared. His ultimate fate remains uncertain. Two other men associated with the revolutionary network died before they could be brought to trial. Sengottai Dharmaraja Iyer took poison, while Venkateswara Iyer, a lawyer from Punalur, cut his own throat.

British authorities refused to surrender Vanchi’s remains to his family and disposed of the body themselves, leaving his father to perform only the customary rites without the body. Ashe was given a formal burial at Palayamkottai, attended by British officials, and a memorial were subsequently erected in his honour. Vanchi, by contrast, was denied the dignity of a public funeral and was officially branded a murderer and conspirator.

Unfortunately, even the memory of Vanchinathan has not escaped the distortions of contemporary politics. Certain partisan Dravidian narratives have attempted to rehabilitate Ashe as a social reformer while portraying Vanchi’s act as the product of caste prejudice. Stories are repeatedly circulated claiming that Ashe opened the waterfalls of Courtallam to all communities and that he once escorted a pregnant Dalit woman through the Sengottai agraharam, thereby provoking Vanchinathan to assassinate him.

These claims deserve to be tested against basic historical and geographical facts. At the time, both Sengottai and Courtallam formed part of the princely state of Travancore, whereas Ashe served in the neighbouring Madras Presidency as the Collector of Tirunelveli. He possessed no administrative authority over either Sengottai or Courtallam. Moreover, the Sengottai agraharam was then a dead-end street; it was not a thoroughfare through which anyone could have been escorted to another destination. Narratives built upon such obvious inconsistencies cannot be allowed to replace documented history.

Even today, a group gathers at Ashe’s memorial on 17 June to offer what they call Veera Vanakkam. Yet the young revolutionary who sacrificed his life on that very day receives far less public remembrance. One may debate Vanchinathan’s chosen method. But there can be no honest denial of his courage, his spirit of sacrifice and his uncompromising commitment to the freedom of the nation.

Vanchinathan sought neither office nor wealth nor personal glory. He abandoned his career, accepted separation from his family and knowingly walked towards certain death for what he believed to be the liberation of Bharat. To erase that conviction, or to reduce his sacrifice to a fabricated story of caste resentment, is not historical interpretation, it is an injustice to the memory of a patriot.

The least we owe Vanchinathan is to remember him truthfully: not as a convenient symbol in present-day political battles, but as a young revolutionary who believed that the subjugation of his motherland was intolerable and who was prepared to offer his own life in defiance of it.
