# Rajendra Chola: The Emperor at the Feet of Shiva

Rajendra Chola I is remembered primarily as a conqueror. His armies marched across the subcontinent, reached the Ganga, crossed the seas and carried Chola power deep into Southeast Asia. He founded a magnificent new capital, Gangaikonda Cholapuram, and presided over one of the most expansive phases of the Chola empire. Yet behind this image of the warrior-emperor was another Rajendra: a deeply committed devotee of Shiva whose personal faith left a lasting imprint on temples, inscriptions, sacred literature and religious institutions across the Chola country.

His devotion was not something that developed only after he became emperor. Rajendra belonged to a dynasty in which Saivism had long occupied an important place. His father Rajaraja Chola famously bore the title Sivapada Sekhara, “he who bears the feet of Shiva upon his crown.” Rajendra too adopted a similar devotional expression, **Sivacharana Sekhara**, proclaiming that the feet of Shiva were the ornament upon his head. In the political vocabulary of the Cholas, such titles were not merely ornamental. They communicated the king’s conception of sovereignty: however powerful the emperor might be in the temporal world, before Shiva he remained a devotee. He has inscribed this name in many of his charters, the below one is from Thiruvalanchuli

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gHo9!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7ff11cb7-46ce-4747-a9f9-153c72486fdb_809x102.png)

The foundations of this devotion appear to have been laid during Rajendra’s youth. As a prince, he was trained not merely in warfare, administration, horse riding and elephant riding, but also in the Vedas, Sastras and Agamic traditions. His early spiritual education was associated with Isana Sivacharya, who was also the preceptor of Rajaraja Chola. The Chola court thus combined kingship with a strong tradition of religious scholarship and guidance under Saiva acharyas.

The religious milieu surrounding Rajaraja and Rajendra also indicates the influence of Pasupata Saivism. Saiva ascetics, teachers and monasteries belonging to different Saiva traditions enjoyed royal support, and the relationship between the Chola monarchs and their spiritual teachers frequently appears in inscriptions. Rajendra’s devotion therefore has to be understood not merely in terms of personal temple worship, but as participation in a larger institutional world of Saiva theology, Agamic ritual, teachers, disciples and temple establishments.

When Rajendra assumed the throne, military campaigns and imperial administration naturally occupied much of his attention. Yet temple construction continued alongside conquest. Shiva temples appeared across his territories, and older temples were enlarged, rebuilt or endowed. His greatest religious monument was certainly Gangaikonda Cholapuram, but it formed part of a much wider programme of Saiva patronage.

**Temples built by Rajendra**

An interesting example survives at Koozhamandal near Kanchipuram. A Shiva temple there was associated with Rajendra’s spiritual lineage and with Isana Siva Pandita, his paramaguru. Rajendra played an important role in establishing the temple, which appropriately came to be known as Gangaikonda Cholisvaram. The very naming of the shrine after the emperor’s imperial title shows how royal achievement and devotion to Shiva were intertwined.

The same pattern can be seen at Thiruvotriyur, near present-day Chennai. The temple was already ancient by Rajendra’s time. It had received Pallava patronage and had been celebrated in the hymns of Appar, Sambandar and Sundarar. During Rajendra’s reign, however, the temple underwent an important architectural transformation. An inscription records that the sanctum was reconstructed in stone under the direction of the Saiva acharya Chaturanana Pandita, with Vira Chola Taccan associated with the work. Inscriptions at Thiruvotriyur mentions these details.

स्वस्तिश्री। तक्ष्णैतच्च्री(\*च्छ्री)विमानं बहुविधचरणैस्तोरणैः कूटकोष्ठैन्नी(र्न्नी)डैर्दभ्रैरदभ्रैर्म्मृगपतिवदनैर्न्नासिकाद्यैर्विचित्रम्(।) देवेशस्याधिपुर्यामति

बहुलतरैरश्मभिः कृष्णवर्णै राजेन्द्रस्य प्रसादात् कृतमिह रविणा राजराजस्य सूनोः உ चतुराननचोदितेन तेन त्रितलन्निर्गतदूषणं विमानम्(।) रविणाधि

पुरीशस्य शम्भोः कृतमेत(द्व)रवीरचोळतक्ष्णा

*This Sri-vimana, through the patronage of Rajendra, the son of Rajaraja, was constructed at Adhipuri (Tiruvotriyur) by the architect Ravi using strong blocks of black granite. It was adorned with various architectural elements, including different types of mouldings, toranas, halls and niches, large and small kudus, and nasikas with lion faces.*

The temple’s graceful gajaprishta, or apsidal, vimana remains one of its striking architectural features.

At the southern end of modern Chennai stands another important shrine connected with Rajendra: the Marundisvarar temple at Thiruvanmiyur. Thus two major ancient Shiva temples situated at opposite ends of today’s Chennai bear the marks of Rajendra’s patronage and the religious activity of his reign.

If one temple reveals Rajendra’s architectural patronage particularly vividly, it is Tiruvarur.

Tiruvarur occupied a special position in Chola religious memory. Tradition connected it with Manu Needhi Chola, and successive Chola rulers lavished gifts upon the temple. Among Chola shrines, its prestige was perhaps second only to Chidambaram. Rajendra continued this tradition on a remarkable scale, assisted by his close companion and devotee Paravai Nangaiyar.

The shrine of Tyagaraja, situated close to the principal sanctum, had originally been a comparatively modest brick structure. During Rajendra’s twentieth regnal year, it was rebuilt in stone and architecturally integrated with the main temple complex. But reconstruction was only one part of his contribution.

According to the inscriptions, enormous quantities of precious metal were given for embellishing the shrine. More than 20,000 kalanju of gold was employed for gilding, while tens of thousands of palam of copper were used for covering other portions of the structure, including architectural elements such as pillars and doors. These were not token donations. They represented an extraordinary royal investment in transforming the shrine into a monument worthy of Shiva.

It is also at Tiruvarur that we encounter an important personal detail about the emperor. An inscription records Rajendra’s natal star as Tiruvadirai in the Tamil month of Adi. The temple also has the sculptures of Rajendra Chola and Paravai Nangai.

Rajendra’s Saiva faith was also expressed through memorial architecture. At Pattisvaram he constructed a pallippadai temple for Panchavan Madeviyar, one of Rajaraja Chola’s queens. Pallippadai temples, constructed in association with the burial or memorialisation of deceased members of the royal family, are particularly important for understanding Chola religious practice. Rajendra’s patronage of such a shrine provides another window into the Saiva ritual world within which the imperial family functioned, including practices that have often been connected with Pasupata traditions.

Another remarkable Rajendra-period temple is the Naganathaswami temple at Manambadi. Though today the monument has suffered badly and has faced dismantling and displacement owing to modern infrastructure works, its sculptures offer an extraordinary glimpse into royal devotion.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!NSa4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fefb762ce-cefc-4fb6-8584-785f3d22350e_1448x1086.heic)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!NSa4!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fefb762ce-cefc-4fb6-8584-785f3d22350e_1448x1086.heic)





One sculptural composition depicts members of Rajendra’s family worshipping Nataraja. Rajendra appears with his queens on one side, while his sons and their consorts appear on the other. The scene is significant because imperial power disappears before the deity. The emperor who commanded armies across India and beyond the seas is represented simply as a devotee, standing with folded hands before the dancing Shiva.

This idea reaches its architectural culmination at Gangaikonda Cholapuram.

**Gangai Konda Cholapuram**

Rajendra founded the city after his celebrated northern expedition and victory associated with the Ganga. The new capital itself commemorated the event: Gangaikonda Cholapuram, “the city of the Chola who took the Ganga.” But at its centre he raised not merely a royal palace or military monument. He built a colossal temple for Shiva.

The connection between the northern expedition and the temple was deeply symbolic. Sacred Ganga water was brought south and used in connection with the consecration of the new capital and its sacred establishments. The water that had flowed from the Himalayas was now ritually incorporated into the Chola imperial landscape.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!2lc6!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F829a2ca6-f584-4cd8-9dd3-893de32fec8f_3888x2592.heic)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!2lc6!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F829a2ca6-f584-4cd8-9dd3-893de32fec8f_3888x2592.heic)





Yet one of the most fascinating aspects of the temple is its relationship with Rajaraja’s Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur. Rajendra was perfectly capable of surpassing his father’s monument in scale had he wished to do so. Instead, the vimana at Gangaikonda Cholapuram was deliberately made lower than the towering vimana at Thanjavur. Whether we interpret this strictly as an act of filial reverence or as an architectural choice, the visual dialogue between the two temples is striking.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!loU6!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fddab0163-c47a-444c-b252-d257106178b6_416x482.heic)





Rajendra, however, did not diminish the majesty of the deity within. The enormous Shiva linga at Gangaikonda Cholapuram rises to approximately 3.3 metres and is among the largest surviving lingas of its period. Here the designation Peruvudaiyar — the Great Lord — seems particularly appropriate.

Rajendra’s own devotion appears to have been given sculptural form at the temple. On the southern side of the complex, above the figure of Lakshmi, is a royal devotional representation traditionally identified with Rajendra worshipping Shiva (the picture at the beginning of the article). Once again, the king appears not as conqueror but as worshipper.

Gangaikonda Cholapuram also contains another intriguing architectural feature. Rajendra caused two subsidiary Shiva shrines to be built on the northern and southern sides of the main temple, known as Vada Kailasam and Then Kailasam — the Northern Kailasa and Southern Kailasa. These were effectively miniature Shiva temples with their own sanctums and surrounding devakoshta deities

Their inspiration may have come from Tiruvaiyaru, where queens associated with Rajaraja’s court had constructed comparable northern and southern shrines. Rajendra was therefore not merely creating a new imperial sacred centre. He was consciously carrying forward architectural and devotional traditions already cultivated within his family.

The sculptural programme at Gangaikonda Cholapuram makes his engagement with Shaiva theology even clearer. Around the main shrine appear powerful manifestations and episodes connected with Shiva: Lingodbhava, Ardhanarisvara, Kalasamhara, Kamadahana and other forms. The temple effectively transforms Shaiva mythology into stone. It is theology expressed through architecture.

But among all the sculptures at Gangaikonda Cholapuram, one composition is particularly revealing of Rajendra’s devotional imagination: Chandesa Anugrahamurti.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!rjI9!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F43c77d47-4e7e-492d-a3be-993fceea104f_1315x1111.heic)





The story of Chandesa is one of the most powerful narratives of complete devotion to Shiva. The young Visarasarman was so absorbed in worshipping the linga that he became oblivious to the world around him. When his father angrily disturbed his worship and kicked away the offering intended for Shiva, the enraged devotee struck at his father’s leg. Shiva then appeared, recognised the intensity of his devotion and elevated him as Chandesa, eventually regarded in temple tradition as the custodian of Shiva’s possessions.

In most Shiva temples, Chandikesvara occupies a modest shrine near the gomukha through which the abhisheka water flows from the sanctum.

Rajendra gave Chandesa extraordinary prominence at Gangaikonda Cholapuram.

A magnificent sculptural panel depicts Shiva placing a garland or crown upon Chandesa’s head — the famous Chandesa Anugraha Murti. Around it are sculptural episodes narrating the story of Chandesa’s devotion. The Chandikesvara shrine itself was positioned in a manner that establishes a visual relationship with this great panel.

Chandesa represented the ideal Shiva devotee: absolute loyalty, complete surrender and responsibility for the property of the Lord. For an emperor responsible for administering a vast realm while simultaneously regarding himself as the servant of Shiva, the symbolism must have been especially powerful. Rajendra seems to have found in Chandesa an ideal of devotion that he wished to celebrate in the very heart of his imperial temple.

**Compilation of Thirumurais**

Rajaraja Chola had initiated the momentous effort to recover the hymns of the great Tamil Saiva saints and organise them into the sacred corpus later known as the Tirumurai. Nambiyandar Nambi played the central role in this enterprise. Under Rajaraja, the hymns of Sambandar, Appar and Sundarar formed the first seven Tirumurais.

Rajendra continued the work his father had begun.

Nambiyandar Nambi continued to receive patronage, and the process of organising the remaining Shaiva canonical literature progressed during Rajendra’s period. The works that came to constitute the eighth, ninth, tenth and eleventh Tirumurais were brought within the expanding canon. Thus Rajendra’s contribution to Saivism was not merely the construction of stone temples; it also involved the preservation and institutionalisation of Tamil Shaiva sacred literature.

The recitation of these hymns was equally important. The Cholas did not treat the Tirumurai merely as manuscripts to be preserved. Oduvars were appointed in temples to sing them as part of regular worship, and grants were made to maintain this tradition. Inscriptions from temples such as Tiruvaiyaru provide evidence for the continuing institutional support given to sacred recitation.

Perhaps the finest testimony to Rajendra’s religious personality, however, comes from his relationship with his guru, Sarvasiva Pandita.

Rajendra held Sarvasiva Pandita in extraordinary reverence. An inscription in the Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur records Acharya Bhoga (provisions and endowments made for the maintenance of the acharya) not merely for Sarvasiva Pandita but also for his disciples.

The relationship between king and guru therefore extended beyond personal reverence. Rajendra materially sustained the intellectual and spiritual lineage associated with his teacher.

At Esalam, this guru-bhakti took architectural form. Rajendra caused the temple of Tiruviramesvaram to be established in accordance with his guru’s wishes. The famous Esalam copper plates connected with the foundation contain Sanskrit verses that give us perhaps the most intimate literary portrait of Rajendra as a Shiva devotee.

One verse describes the emperor metaphorically as a bee hovering around the parijata flowers that were the feet of Shiva:

***“Rajendra, the repository of noble qualities, built in the city of Gangaikondacholapuram created by himself an abode for Mahesvara. In his devotion, he was like a bee at the parijata flowers of the Lord’s twin feet.”***

The image is striking. This is the same emperor whose inscriptions boast of kings defeated, countries conquered and oceans crossed. Yet when his relationship with Shiva is described, imperial imagery disappears. Rajendra becomes a small bee seeking the fragrance of Shiva’s feet.

Another verse from the same record turns towards Rajendra’s guru and asks, in effect, what further words of praise could adequately describe him. The guru is portrayed as Mahesvara himself visible before one’s eyes, while the crowned heads of kings bow at his feet.

This inversion of hierarchy tells us much about Rajendra’s world. Before subordinate rulers Rajendra was Chakravartin. Before his acharya, he was a disciple. Before Shiva, he was a bhakta.

Thiruvindalur copper plates of his sons, in fact, captures the king’s final thoughts and his devotional temperament beautifully. As death approached, Rajendra summoned his ministers and officials. He called his son Dhabrasabhapathi (Rajendra II), seated him on his lap, and declared before the assembled dignitaries that his son was the heir apparent and would rule after him. After they had all departed, Rajendra became deeply absorbed in thought.

Rajendra imagined wondering what would happen when he reached Shiva’s abode. Would Shiva’s attendants take him respectfully by the hand?

प्रतिप्रणतिसत्वरप्रमथसङ्घसम्पूजितं करग्रहणलालनप्रणयिनंदिसम्प्रापितम्।

नमन्तमसकृज्जयेत्यपि वदन्तमाह्लाद्य मां स्मितेन किमु दर्शयिष्यति शिवः प्रियायै प्रभुः।।

Would they lead him into the divine presence? When he repeatedly cried out in salutation to the Lord, would Shiva receive him smilingly and introduce him lovingly to the Goddess?

The inscription then says that the king, completely absorbed in this thought, finally attained the abode of Shiva — while the kingdom he left behind sank into grief.

History remembers Rajendra as Gangaikonda Chola, Kadaramkonda Chola and the emperor whose armies carried the tiger banner across extraordinary distances. Those achievements deserve their place in history. But they represent only one side of his personality.

The other Rajendra stands silently in the temples he created.

He is the prince learning Vedas and Agamas from his acharya. He is the king rebuilding ancient Shiva shrines. He is the patron covering the Tyagaraja shrine with gold. He is the emperor standing with his family before Nataraja. He is the son who built Gangaikonda Cholapuram without seeking to overshadow his father’s Brihadisvara. He is the devotee who placed Chandesa’s story prominently within his greatest temple. He is the patron who continued the preservation of the Tirumurais. He is the disciple who provided Acharya Bhoga for his guru and his guru’s students.

And finally, in the beautiful words of the Esalam plates, he is the mighty emperor reduced willingly to a bee at the lotus-like feet of Shiva.

That perhaps tells us more about Rajendra Chola than any list of his conquests.
