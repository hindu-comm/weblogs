As we observe the Guru Puja of Nandanar, one of the celebrated 63 Saiva saints, it is pertinent to revisit his actual life history, which has, over time, been considerably distorted in popular narratives. His story is widely known, but the version that has entered popular memory is considerably different from the account given by Sekkizhar in the Periyapuranam. His story is a classic example of how history, when it gets altered to enhance its entertainment quotient, can be used for narrative setting and propaganda.

**The distorted Nandanar Charitram**

Gopalakrishna Bharati’s famous Nandanar Charitram departed substantially from the narrative in the Periyapuranam and introduced several elements that subsequently became inseparable from the popular understanding of Nandanar. The story gained even greater reach when it entered cinema, including through the famous film associated with S. S. Vasan. The dramatised narrative eventually became so familiar that it came to be regarded by many as the original story.

But if we return to the Periyapuranam, the principal source for the lives of the 63 Nayanmars, what does it actually tell us?

Nandanar was born in the Pulaiyar community. He carried out the profession of making announcements by playing the drum, connected with the rights and provisions granted for such work.

Sekkizhar records:

ஊரில் விடும் பறைத்துடவை உணவு உரிமையாக் கொண்டு  
சார்பில்வரும் தொழில் செய்வார்.

But it was his devotion to Lord Siva that defined Nandanar’s life. From birth, he was a staunch Siva Bhakta. His devotion was not confined merely to worship. He also contributed to the requirements of Siva temples around him. The Periyapuranam speaks of his providing materials connected with musical instruments such as the Veena and Yazh, apart from articles required for the worship of the Lord:

வீணைக்கும் யாழுக்கும் நிலை வகையில்  
சேர் உற்ற தந்திரியும் தேவர் பிரான் அர்ச்சனை கட்கு  
ஆர்வத்தின் உடன் கோரோசனையும் இவை அளித்து உள்ளார்.

Nandanar also visited various Siva temples seeking Darshan. However, Nandanar himself carried a deep hesitation in his mind, feeling that, because of his birth, it would not be appropriate for him to enter the temple. It is important to note that nowhere in the Periyapuranam does Sekkizhar state that anyone prevented Nandanar from entering a temple. The hesitation, as the narrative presents it, came from within Nandanar himself.

It was during one such visit to Thirupungur that one of the most famous episodes associated with his life took place. Standing outside, Nandanar prayed to Lord Siva. But Nandi, seated directly before the Lord, obstructed his view. Seeing the devotion of Nandanar, Lord Siva caused Nandi to move aside and granted His devotee an unobstructed Darshan.

The Periyapuranam says:

புன்கூர்க் கண் நுதலார் திருமுன்பு போர் ஏற்றை விலங்க அருள் புரிந்து அருளிப் புலப்படுத்தார்.

Nandanar was overwhelmed by the grace bestowed upon him. He subsequently dug a pond near the temple.

But there was one pilgrimage that remained constantly in his mind, that is, Thillai, Chidambaram.

His greatest desire was to worship Lord Nataraja at Chidambaram. Yet he repeatedly postponed the journey. His hesitation arose from his consciousness about his own birth and caste. He wanted to go to Thillai, but something within him continued to hold him back. Eventually, his devotion prevailed over his hesitation.

Nandanar gathered the courage to travel to Thillai. On reaching the sacred town, he saw the mutts, Yagasalas and the religious establishments surrounding it. Yet the hesitation that had troubled him earlier returned. He could not bring himself to enter. He therefore went around the town and finally remained outside for the night.

It is here that the Periyapuranam narrative reaches its most important moment.

That night Lord Siva appeared in Nandanar’s dream. At the same time, He appeared in the dreams of the Thillai Andhanars, the Brahmins associated with the temple.

The divine instruction was that a fire should be prepared and that Nandanar should pass through it, thereby freeing himself from the doubts and hesitation concerning his birth that continued to remain within him. The Thillai Andhanars themselves were hesitant when they received this extraordinary command. But it was the command of the Lord, and therefore they resolved to carry it out.

Sekkizhar describes the moment:

தம்பெருமான் பணி கேட்ட தவ மறையோர் எல்லாரும்  
அம்பலவர் திருவாயின் முன்பு அச்சமுடன் ஈண்டி  
எம்பெருமான் அருள் செய்த பணி செய்வோம் என்று ஏத்த

Accordingly, the fire was prepared. Nandanar did not hesitate now. With complete faith in Lord Siva, he entered the flames.

Having passed through the fire, his mind got freed. He proceeded towards the temple and entered to worship Lord Nataraja. On entering the sanctum, Nandanar disappeared into the divine presence, attaining eternal union with Siva.

This is where the distinction between the Periyapuranam narrative and many later retellings becomes particularly significant. In Sekkizhar’s account, no individual is described as preventing Nandanar from entering the temples he visited. At Thirupungur, when Nandi obstructed his Darshan, Siva Himself caused Nandi to move aside. At Thillai, it was Nandanar’s own consciousness about his birth that made him hesitate to enter.

The barrier that had to be overcome was therefore within Nandanar himself. And it was Lord Siva who removed it.

The passage through fire represents that decisive transformation. Nandanar walks through it in complete faith, leaving behind the hesitation that had prevented him from approaching the Lord. Once that barrier disappears, nothing remains between the devotee and the deity.

This is very different from the version that subsequently became popular through Nandanar Charitram.

Gopalakrishna Bharati expanded the story considerably and introduced several dramatic elements that are not part of Sekkizhar’s original narrative. One such addition was the character of the ‘Aandai’, a landlord under whom Nandanar was portrayed as working. The Periyapuranam, however, does not present Nandanar as an impoverished labourer dependent on a landlord for his livelihood. In Bharati’s version, when Nandanar seeks permission to leave for Thillai, the ‘Aandai’ imposes an impossible condition upon him — that all the fields must be sown overnight before he can leave. This entire episode, which became a prominent part of the later Nandanar narrative, is absent from Sekkizhar’s Periyapuranam.

Whatever may have been the reasons for these additions, the enormous popularity of Bharati’s work, followed by its adaptation into cinema, ensured that the later version entered public memory far more deeply than the account in the Periyapuranam.

Consequently, discussions about Nandanar often begin with the later dramatised narrative rather than with Sekkizhar.

Periyapuranam, the original text which portrays the life of the Shaiva Saints, is very clear in terms of Nandanar’s story. Here we find Nandanar as a man born in the Pulaiyar community, carrying out his hereditary profession, contributing to Siva temples through his work, travelling from shrine to shrine seeking Darshan, and above all possessing an extraordinary devotion towards Lord Siva.

He longed for Thillai. His own hesitation repeatedly kept him away from it. But ultimately his Bhakti took him there. And when even that final hesitation stood between the devotee and his Lord, Siva Himself intervened to remove it.

Nandanar entered Thillai and finally became one with the Lord whom he had worshipped throughout his life.

Even today, the Thillai Vaazh Anthanars worship Nandanar with due reverence during important Poojas, and on his Guru Puja, special Abhishekams are performed in his honour.

There is yet another popular narrative that the southern gate of the Chidambaram temple was closed because Nandanar entered the temple through that gate. Nothing could be further from the traditional account. Among the four great Saiva saints, it was Thirugnanasambandar, himself a Vaidika Brahmin, who is traditionally associated with entering Thillai through the southern gate. How, then, can the closure of the gate be attributed to Nandanar having entered through it?

An administrative arrangement involving the closure of one of the temple gates has thus been given a caste-based interpretation and woven into the larger narrative surrounding Nandanar. Such later claims need to be distinguished from what the traditional sources actually record. It is time that the original narrative is brought back into focus and the story of Nandanar is understood from the sources themselves.
