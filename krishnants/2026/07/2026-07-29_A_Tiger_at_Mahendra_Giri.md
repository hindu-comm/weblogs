Mahendra Giri is a picturesque hill in the Gajapati district of Odisha. It is the second highest mountain peak in the state and a trekkers paradise. It is also bio diversity park having a number of medicinal plants. There are lot of legends associated with it right from Ramayana & Mahabharata days. It is said that Parasurama used to meditate here.

***Yudhistira Temple***

Atop the hill, there are three temples which are called as Yudhistira, Bheema and Kunti temples. All the three temples are dedicated to Shiva. The legends say that Pandavas built these temples during their Vanavas. Many historians date these temples during Gupta period and some later than that.

When Rajendra Chola completed the magnificent temple at his new capital, Gangaikonda Cholapuram, he desired that it should be consecrated with the sacred waters of the Ganga. Yet the expedition he conceived was to be far more than a pilgrimage. Like Samudragupta before him, Rajendra sought to undertake a grand digvijaya—a victorious march across the northern lands that would proclaim the power of the Cholas far beyond the traditional frontiers of Tamilakam.

For this formidable mission, Rajendra entrusted his northern army to the experienced commander Arayan Rajarajan. Under his leadership, the Chola forces began their long march towards Bengal. Kingdom after kingdom encountered the advancing army. Chakkarakottam fell first, followed by Ottaram, Dandabhukti, Dakshina Lada and Uttara Lada. The Chola banners continued to move northward until the army finally confronted Mahipala, the powerful ruler of the Pala dynasty. Even the Pala forces could not halt their progress, and Mahipala was defeated.

The Chola army had now reached the sacred Ganga.

The Tiruvalangadu Copper Plates preserve a striking account of what followed. According to the inscription, a large number of elephants were made to stand across the river, forming a living bridge. Over this remarkable passage, the sacred waters of the Ganga were carried in numerous pots. The army then began its triumphant journey southward, bearing with it the waters that would sanctify Rajendra’s new capital and give the city its immortal name—Gangaikonda Cholapuram, the city of the Chola who conquered the Ganga.

Rajendra was staying at Kanchipuram when news of the northern victory reached him. Filled with pride at the achievement of his army, he resolved that the sacred waters should not merely be received at the capital. He would personally welcome the victorious forces at the northern frontier of his empire.

Rajendra therefore set out towards Kalinga with another army, commanded by Rajaraja Marayan. His decision was not merely ceremonial. The northern army was returning through distant and potentially hostile territories, carrying both the sacred water and the symbols of Chola victory. Rajendra wanted to ensure that no rebellion or resistance along the frontier would interrupt its homeward march.

Advancing northward, Rajendra reached Rajamahendravaram, the present-day Rajahmundry. There, on the banks of the Godavari—one of the seven sacred rivers of Bharata—he took a ceremonial bath and awaited the returning army.

However, when the Chola forces entered Kalinga, the local ruler attempted to resist their advance. Rajendra swiftly defeated both the Kalinga king and his brother. Their elephants were seized as trophies of war, adding yet another victory to the emperor’s northern campaign.

At this moment, Arayan Rajarajan’s army, marching south after its astonishing conquests, arrived with the sacred waters of the Ganga. The two Chola armies had now converged: one returning victorious from Bengal and the other triumphant over the forces of Kalinga.

Rajendra decided that such a momentous double victory must be commemorated for posterity. He proceeded to Mahendragiri, where he erected a vijaya stambha—a pillar of victory. In the Yudhishthira temple on the hill, he caused an inscription to be engraved, recording the Chola triumph.

Written in Sanskrit using the Telugu script, the inscription proudly proclaimed:

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!aHmT!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2ac09b51-a078-43ec-94ac-3a07009bdb1d_320x235.jpeg)

“Rajendra Chola won over many countries including the ones with mountains and defeated Kalingapathi. To celebrate this, he made this Vijaya Sthampa”

There are also three incomplete Tamil inscriptions in Kunti temple.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!rpjF!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff55d4344-b543-4511-a146-2bd5bc8efec5_320x125.jpeg)

The inscriptions at Mahendragiri preserve more than a simple record of conquest. They offer brief but fascinating glimpses into the men who fought beside Rajendra Chola and the honours bestowed upon them after victory.

From the second inscription, it appears that Rajaraja Marayan, the commander who accompanied Rajendra into Kalinga, was ceremonially presented with a Veera Ankusa, an emblem of martial valour. He was also honoured with the title “Vitti Varanamallan” at the Shiva temple known as Mahendragirisvaram. The title itself evokes the image of a warrior renowned for confronting and overcoming mighty elephants on the battlefield.

A third inscription refers to a person named Vimaladitya. Though his precise identity remains uncertain, he may have been one of the rulers defeated during Rajendra’s Kalinga campaign. His name, preserved among the victory records of Mahendragiri, stands as a faint echo of the many regional powers that attempted to resist the Chola advance.

Yet the most striking testimony to the Chola presence is not found in words alone.

At the entrance of the Yudhishthira temple, carved upon the stone, appears the unmistakable figure of a tiger—the royal emblem of the Cholas. Even on this distant mountain in Kalinga, far from the fertile plains of the Kaveri, the Cholas left behind the symbol of their imperial authority.

But the carving contains another fascinating detail. Before the tiger appears the Irattai Kayal, the twin-fish emblem of the Pandyas.

The Cholas had never forgotten their long rivalry with the Pandya kingdom. Even after establishing their supremacy over the south, they continued to celebrate their victories over their traditional adversaries. By placing the Pandya twin fish before the Chola tiger, the sculptors conveyed a powerful political message: the Pandyas had been subdued, and their emblem now stood beneath the authority of the imperial Cholas.

Under Rajaraja Chola and Rajendra Chola, the tiger had entered its golden age. It had crossed seas, conquered kingdoms and marched from the banks of the Kaveri to the waters of the Ganga. Now, upon the summit of Mahendragiri, it stood carved in stone, overlooking the lands of Kalinga as a silent witness to the northern victories of the Chola armies.

This, then, is the story of the Chola tiger at the Yudhishthira temple atop Mahendragiri—a small carving that preserves the memory of an emperor’s ambition, a commander’s valour and a dynasty that carried its symbols of victory far beyond Tamilakam.
