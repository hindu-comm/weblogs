# The Administration of Justice in Ancient Tamil Nadu

One of the most important indicators of good governance is the system through which justice is administered in a country. The judiciary, which is regarded today as one of the four pillars of democracy, enjoyed similar importance even during the age of monarchy. Our Dharmashastra texts assign a significant place to those responsible for dispensing justice and to the procedures they were expected to follow.

It was also important to deliver justice in a timely manner, and given the large number of cases, it was impossible for a single authority to handle them all. Although the king possessed the highest authority to administer justice under a monarchical system, it was obviously impossible for him alone to hear and decide every case arising throughout the kingdom. Therefore, courts functioning at different levels existed even during those times. A study of Tamil Nadu’s inscriptions provides considerable information about the different kinds of judicial assemblies that existed, the manner in which their members were selected, and the extent of their authority.

The village courts of that period were known as “Manradu.” The qualifications required of the village judge who administered justice there—known as the “Manradupavar” or “Manradi”—were clearly defined. The judicial assembly itself possessed the authority to hear cases and deliver judgments. During that period, most disputes were ordinarily settled at the level of the village judicial assembly. Only a small number of cases were taken to regional courts or to the king’s royal court of justice.

For these reasons, it was essential that village judicial assemblies should be constituted as strong and effective institutions. In many places, the king personally intervened in the formation of such assemblies. In certain other places, the respective village assemblies themselves possessed the authority to constitute their judicial bodies.

When new villages were established, judicial bodies were created for them at the same time. A thirteenth-century inscription in the Shiva temple at Porur records that the ruler ordered the boundaries of a new village to be demarcated, the village to be formally constituted, and a judicial assembly to be established for it. The order stipulated that the judicial assembly should consist of seven members and that both Brahmins and Vellalars should be appointed to it. This shows that such judicial assemblies included representatives from different sections of society.

One of the earliest inscriptions to provide information about the judicial system of ancient Tamil Nadu is found at Manur, near Tirunelveli. Located in the Ambalavana Swamy Temple, the inscription was issued during the reign of the Pandya king Parantaka Nedunjadaiyan and is dated to 898 CE. It offers valuable information about the organisation and functioning of village courts.

The inscription lays down the qualifications of the members of the village judicial assembly in the following words:

“Those who seek to serve in the people’s assembly as adjudicators must be well-versed in at least one Dharmashastra, proficient in the Mantras and Brahmanas, and be persons of good conduct. Only one person from each family shall serve as an adjudicator in the assembly.”

In other words, anyone aspiring to become a member of the judicial assembly had to be proficient in at least one of the Dharmashastras. Just as cases today are examined and decided according to civil and criminal laws, legal disputes in ancient times were heard and judgments were delivered on the basis of the Dharmashastras. In many instances, more than one Dharmashastra was consulted before a verdict was pronounced.

Therefore, apart from the widely known Manusmriti, a prospective member had to possess expertise in at least one of the Dharmashastras attributed to sages such as Yajnavalkya, Brihaspati or Parashara. This was the foremost qualification. It makes clear that judgments in village courts were based on established principles of Dharmashastra and were not determined by the personal likes and dislikes of individuals.

Another important qualification was that the member of the judicial assembly had to be a person of good character and conduct—a suvritta. The inscription further states that only one member from each family could be represented in such assemblies.

**Thirunindravur Inscription**

An inscription from Tiruninravur, belonging to the reign of the Chola king Parantaka I and dated to 930 CE, reveals that members appointed to judicial bodies could remain in office only for a fixed period. A person selected as a member of the court could hold office for one year. Thereafter, for five years, he could not serve either as a member of that judicial body, as a member of the village assembly, or in any of its associated committees.

Moreover, his close relatives—including his parents, brothers and sons—were barred from holding any of these positions for two years. All these regulations clearly demonstrate that, in matters concerning the judiciary, everyone, including the king, sought to uphold justice without compromise.

The inscription records the regulations in the following terms:

“From this year onwards, whenever members are selected for the judicial body and the committees, the Mahasabha shall assemble in full strength at the Brahmasthana. Only persons acceptable to the Mahasabha shall be selected.

Those who have already served as judges or as members of committees during the prescribed five-year period shall not be selected again for the judicial body or the committees.

When names are written for committee membership, persons who have served on committees during the preceding two years, as well as their brothers, fathers and sons, shall be excluded.

Those selected according to this procedure shall assume their responsibilities in the presence of the Mahasabha.

Anyone who assumes office without following this procedure shall be considered to have committed an offence against the village and shall lose the benefits attached to committee membership.

Those who have completed their service as judges or committee members shall present their accounts before the assembly that succeeds them, formally hand over responsibility, and then relinquish office.

We have established this regulation accordingly. Anyone who violates it shall, at the request of the responsible authorities of our village, be liable to pay a fine of twenty-five kalanju of gold to the Dharmasana.”

The inscription further states that, once their term of office in the court had ended, the members could leave their positions only after presenting proper accounts before the village assembly and formally handing over their responsibilities. Anyone who refused to comply with these regulations was required to pay a fine of twenty-five kalanju of gold.

**Judgements**

Some of the judgments delivered by these courts are quite remarkable. Since their decisions were based on texts dealing with dharma, the courts considered not merely the offence itself but also the meaning of dharma and the circumstances under which the offence had occurred.

For example, according to an inscription at Olakkur, Appan Sattan and Udaiyan Perumal had gone hunting together. An arrow shot by Udaiyan at a bird accidentally struck Appan Sattan and killed him. After examining the case, the court concluded that the act had not been committed intentionally. As an act of expiation, it ordered Udaiyan to maintain a perpetual lamp in the shrine of Vatapi Vinayaka within the Tiruvagatisvarar temple and to donate thirty-two cows and one bull for that purpose.

Similarly, an inscription at Tamarappakkam records how a family dispute was resolved. During a quarrel between two brothers, the younger brother killed the elder. However, their parents had no other heirs and possessed no income-generating property. Therefore, in order to preserve the family line and ensure the parents’ support, the younger brother was not sentenced to death. Instead, he was ordered to maintain a perpetual lamp in a temple.

**Land disputes**

It also appears that certain complicated disputes had to be adjudicated directly by the king. There are three copper plate inscriptions of Pandyas which in fact provides a detailed view of this. The oldest of them is Ilayanputhur copper plate inscription of Pandyan Arikesari Parankusa Maravarman (Nindra Seer Nedumaran).

The Pandya king Nedumaran granted a village in the minor territorial division known as Asinadu as a Brahmadeya to Narayana Bhatta Somayaji, a Brahmin from the Chola country. But that was usurped by a chieftain. Hence Nedumaran had to wage a battle against the chieftain to recover the land and grant it to Somayaji. The copper-plate charter states that the king defeated Kambalai, a chieftain who had exercised authority over Asinadu, renamed the land belonging to him as Ilaiyanputtur, and donated it as a Brahmadeya with both karanmai and miyatchi rights—that is, cultivation rights as well as superior proprietary rights.

It is noteworthy that the Sanskrit verse at the end of the copper plates also records that the Pandya king conquered this land by the strength of his bow and subsequently granted it as a Brahmadeya.

The Asinadu mentioned in the charter comprised the region around present-day Kovilpatti in southern Pandya country. It extended on both sides of the route from Kayathar to Kalugumalai. Ilaiyanputtur must have been the name of one of the villages situated in this region.

The Dalavaypuram copper plates of the Pandya king Parantaka Viranarayana record a different kind of dispute. The Pandya king Kadungon had granted the village of Srimangalam to twelve Brahmins. Subsequently, another Pandya king, who is said to have attained siddhi at Kalathur, granted the village of Somasikurichi exclusively to Kadaga Somasiyar.

The copper-plate charters relating to these villages were lost during a period of political unrest. In the meantime, a person named Kadattrirukkai Kilavan demarcated the boundaries of Somasikurichi, appropriated a portion of it for himself and converted the remaining area into an inhabited settlement called Madurataranallur.

Narayanan Kesavan, a resident of Srimangalam and the patikavalan, or guardian, of Somasikurichi, appealed to the king to rectify the situation, combine the two villages and issue a fresh charter granting them to him.

In this case, although the original documents had been lost, the king considered the background of the petitioner and the fact that, as a palace official, he enjoyed the confidence of the ruler. The copper plates state that the king accordingly issued a new charter granting both villages to Narayanan Kesavan.

The well-known Velvikudi copper plates records the fact that a Brahmin named Narsingan stood at the entrance of the palace of the Pandya king Parantaka Nedunjadaiyan and loudly presented his grievance. Hearing him, the king summoned him and enquired into the matter.

Narsingan stated that one of his ancestors, Korkai Kilaan, had been granted the village of Paganur Kurram during the performance of a sacrifice by Palyagasalai Mudukudumi Peruvaludi, an ancient Pandya king of the Sangam age and an ancestor of Nedunjadaiyan. When the Kalabhras seized control of Madurai, they annulled the grant and confiscated the land.

Nedunjadaiyan asked Narsingan to produce documentary evidence supporting his claim, and Narsingan submitted the relevant document. Accepting it as valid, the king issued a fresh charter restoring the village to him. This is the account recorded in the copper-plate inscription.

The Dharmashastra of Yajnavalkya lays down that when legal documents have been destroyed or lost, the king should prepare and issue a replacement document as an authenticated charter. This procedure was known as granting a suddhi, or formal revalidation. The judgment in this case must have been delivered in accordance with this principle.

This carefully organised system of administering justice, which functioned on the basis of the Dharmashastras, was radically transformed by foreign invasions and later by the Western judicial system introduced by the British.

Today, when vast numbers of cases remain pending in the lower and higher courts, it is necessary to reconsider whether village-level judicial institutions still have a meaningful role to play and whether our present laws operate in accordance with the ethical and social principles that have prevailed in our society from ancient times.
