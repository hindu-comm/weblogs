The Kalabhras occupy an unusual place in the history of Tamilakam. They are often described as rulers who displaced the ancient Tamil dynasties and held power for several centuries. Yet we know far less about them than we do about the Cheras, Cholas, Pandyas or Pallavas. That gap has encouraged both speculation and sweeping claims. To understand the Kalabhras, we must begin with what the evidence actually says.

First, we must look at the political landscape of South India after the Sangam age which started before the common era and continued for two centuries into the common era. The Cheras, Cholas and Pandyas shared the region with numerous chiefs, including the Velir. Figures such as Pari, Adiyaman and Nannan appear prominently in Tamil literature. At different times, such chiefs could act as allies of the larger kingdoms, oppose them or exercise power independently. Political authority was spread across many centres, and its boundaries could change.

Because the region was divided among many rulers, no single empire emerged in Tamilakam, even though powerful kings occasionally arose among the three crowned dynasties. Meanwhile, the Satavahana Empire in central India began to weaken. The Pallavas and the Chalukyas began to expand their territories. Pressured by these developments, a ruling family from southern Karnataka moved south. They were the Kalabhras.

The original place of the Kalabhras was the region around Nandi Hills in present day Karnataka. A verse quoted in the commentary on the *Yapperungalam* praises a ruler named Achuta and mentions Nandi hill.

**Aruḷuṭai perumpukaḻ Accutar kōvē  
Iṇaiyai ātaliṉ paṉimati tavaḻum  
Nanti māmalaic cilampa  
Nanti niṟ paravutal nāvalarkku aritē.**

An inscription from the Chikkaballapur region refers to *Kalavara Nadu* and Nandi hill.

**“Nikarila Cōḻa maṇṭalattuk Kaḷavara nāṭṭu Nanti-malai mēl Mahā Nantīśvaram Udaya-mahādēvarkku.” (*****Epigraphic Carnatica Vol 10 - Chikballpur Inscription no. 21*****)**

The *Kalladam*, an eleventh-century CE work, speaks of a “Karnata king who attacked with his fourfold army, drove out the Pandya and seized Madurai” (*Kalladam*, 56). Similarly, the account of Murti Nayanar in the *Periya Puranam*describes a “powerful king of the Vaduga Karnata country” who forcibly took possession of the land. These passages have been interpreted as references to the Kalabhras and as evidence that they came from the Karnataka region.

The clearest statement about the Kalabhras’ rise comes from the Velvikkudi copper plates. They say that a ruler called Kalabhran displaced numerous kings and took control of the country. They later describe the Pandya Kadungon’s recovery of power.

**Aḷavariya ātirācarai nīkki akaliṭattaik  
Kaḷapraṉ eṉum kaliyaracaṉ kaikkoṇṭāṉ.**

They preserve crucial evidence that Kalabhra power interrupted an earlier political order.

The Dalavaypuram plates mention the Kalappalar as below.

**Ālaṅkāṉat tamarveṉṟu ñālaṅkāval naṉkeytiyum  
Kaṭiñāṟu kaviṉalaṅkāṟ kaḷappāḻar kulaṅkaḷaintum.**

Thus, the Kalabhras who came from the north ruled Tamilakam for nearly three centuries. They followed Jainism, which was then widespread in Karnataka, and Buddhism. Buddhadatta, a Buddhist monk, mentions that he was supported by Achuta Kalappalan, who ruled Uraiyur. The Kalabhras also worshipped Shaiva and Vaishnava deities. The *Tiruttondar Tiruvantati* identifies Kutruva Nayanar, one of the Nayanmars, as a Kalabhra, calling him “Kutruvan, the Kalappalan of the long, garlanded spear.” The epithets *Kaliyan* and *Kalikandri* show that Tirumangai Alvar might have belonged to the Kalabhra lineage.

Further, the following lines from the *Yapperungalam* say that the Kalabhras worshipped Tirumal and received a vast territory:

**“Iruḷaṟu tikiriyōṭu valampurit taṭakkai oruvaṉai vēṇṭa irunilam koṭutta Nanti-mālvarai cilampu Natti.**” (Seeking the one whose mighty hand bears the radiant discus and the conch, Nandi of the great mountain slopes received a vast land.)

The Kalabhras’ mother tongue was Suraseni, a branch of Prakrit. Even so, they did not hinder the growth of Tamil. Most of the works of the *Pathinenkilkanakku* were composed during their period. The Velvikkudi copper plates state that the Pandya king Kadungon ended their rule in the sixth century CE:

**Kaḷapraṉeṉuṅ kaliyaracaṉ kaikkoṇṭataṉai iṟakkiyapiṉ, paṭukaṭal muḷaitta paritipōl pāṇṭiyāti rācaṉ veḷippaṭṭu, viṭukatir aviroḷi vilaka vīṟṟiruntu.** (After overthrowing what the Kali king called Kalabhran had seized, the Pandya king appeared like the sun rising from the vast ocean and took his seat, dispelling the darkness with his spreading rays.)

Similarly, the Pallava king Simhavishnu defeated the Kalabhras who ruled in the Chola country. Thereafter, the Kalabhras ruled parts of Tamilakam as subordinate chiefs under the Pandyas and Pallavas. They also became integrated with the Tamil people.

The Kalabhra period was called a “Dark Age” because literary, inscriptional and numismatic evidence about them was unavailable for a long time. For that matter, before the Sangam works became available, much about the earlier period was also unknown. Literary evidence now available, along with inscriptions discovered at Pulankurichi and Ponniwadi, has begun to shed light on the Kalabhra period.

Tamilakam’s history did not pause between the Sangam rulers and the later Pandyas and Pallavas. Power changed hands, communities continued to live and write, and new rulers became part of the region’s story. The Kalabhras were among those rulers. Their origins and the full extent of their rule are still uncertain, but their place in Tamil history is not. The task now is to recover that history patiently, one reliable piece of evidence at a time.
