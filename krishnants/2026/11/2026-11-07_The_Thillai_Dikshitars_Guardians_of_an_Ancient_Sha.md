# The Thillai Dikshitars — Guardians of an Ancient Shaiva Tradition

The Dikshitars of Chidambaram, known as the *Thillai Vaazh Andhanars* in the Shaiva tradition, occupy a unique and revered position in the religious history of Tamilakam. Their association with the sacred temple of Lord Nataraja at Thillai stretches back into antiquity, and their greatness has been celebrated by the Nayanmars, the Alwars and the great exponents of the Shaiva tradition.

For Shaivites, Chidambaram is not merely another temple. It is simply Kovil, The Temple. It is the sacred abode where Lord Shiva performs His Ananda Tandava, the cosmic dance, and where generations of Dikshitars have dedicated their lives to His worship. Their history is inseparable from the religious and cultural history of Chidambaram itself.

#### **The greatness of Thillai Dikshitars**



[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4SIg!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4aa33ca7-f6a6-44ce-857d-8be4b8a78a07_250x367.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4SIg!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4aa33ca7-f6a6-44ce-857d-8be4b8a78a07_250x367.jpeg)





Sekkizhar, in his celebrated *Periyapuranam*, which narrates the lives of the great devotees of Lord Shiva, devotes several verses to extolling the greatness of the Thillai Vaazh Andhanars.

He describes them as those who dedicate their lives to the service of Lord Nataraja, preserving the sacred traditions entrusted to them with unwavering devotion. They perform their religious duties, observe austerities and consider service to the divine feet of Lord Shiva the very purpose of their existence.

They carry the sacred articles required for worship, perform the prescribed rituals, chant the Vedas and discharge their responsibilities in accordance with their religious traditions. Sekkizhar specifically refers to their **Agampadi Thondu (அகம்படித் தொண்டு)** the sacred service rendered within the temple, in the immediate presence of the Lord.

Their religious life is firmly rooted in Vedic learning and practice. They maintain the three sacred Vedic fires (Garhapatya, Ahavaniya and Dakshinagni) and possess knowledge of the four Vedas and the six Vedangas. They consider Dharma to be their true wealth and follow the prescribed path of religious discipline.

Sekkizhar further praises their spiritual knowledge, charity, austerities and observance of *Manai Aram*, the Dharma of household life. This is particularly significant, for the Thillai Dikshitars are householders who combine their familial responsibilities with their sacred duties to Lord Nataraja.

Referring to them as the three thousand Brahmins of Thillai, Sekkizhar declares that their greatness is beyond description. He even states that they have attained the supreme blessing of experiencing the Lord in this very lifetime and that none can be compared to them except themselves.

Such is the extraordinary position accorded to the Thillai Brahmins in one of the most important works of Tamil Shaiva literature.

Their primacy is further reflected in Sundaramurthy Nayanar’s celebrated *Thiruthonda Thogai*. According to Shaiva tradition, Lord Shiva Himself gave Sundarar the opening words of the hymn:

**தில்லைவாழ் அந்தணர்தம் அடியார்க்கும் அடியேன்**

*I am the servant even of the servants of the Brahmins who dwell in Thillai.*

It is remarkable that the hymn celebrating the great Shaiva devotees begins with the Thillai Brahmins. This is not an incidental reference, but a reflection of their exalted place in the Shaiva devotional tradition.

#### The three thousand Dikshitars and the divine association



A traditional account further illustrates the sacred status accorded to the Thillai Dikshitars.

It is said that Lord Brahma invited the three thousand Brahmins of Thillai to participate in a great Yajna. Following its completion, when they returned to Chidambaram at the behest of Sage Vyaghrapada, they discovered that one among them was missing.

As they wondered what had happened, a divine voice revealed that Lord Shiva Himself was counted among the three thousand.

This account, preserved in the sacred tradition of Chidambaram, conveys the intimate relationship between Lord Nataraja and the Thillai Dikshitars. The Lord Himself is regarded as one among them.

Their greatness is also celebrated by the Chola king Kandaraditya in his *Thiruvisaippa*:

**முத்தீயாளர் நான்மறையர் மூவாயிரவர் நின்னோடு  
ஒத்தே வாழும் தன்மையாளர் ஓதிய நான்மறையைத்  
தெத்தே என்று வண்டு பாடும் தென் தில்லையம்பலத்துள்  
அத்தா உன்றன் ஆடல் காண அணைவதும் என்று கொலோ**

Kandaraditya describes the three thousand Vedic Brahmins who maintain the three sacred fires and live in harmony with Lord Nataraja. He longs for the day when he may behold the divine dance of the Lord at Thillai, where even the bees seem to echo the sounds of Vedic chanting.

The verse is particularly important because it demonstrates that the association of the three thousand Brahmins with Chidambaram was firmly embedded in the Shaiva religious tradition during the Chola period.

#### Their role in temple administration and the Chola tradition



The Dikshitars were not merely priests performing daily rituals. They were integral to the religious life of Chidambaram and its temple institutions.

Chola period inscriptions reveal the existence of administrative functionaries connected with the temple, including those referred to as *Sri Kariyam Seivargal* and *Kovil Nayagam Seivargal*. These references help us understand the elaborate system through which temple affairs were managed during the Chola age.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lgQu!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbcb10568-240a-47c2-be63-6743d72b0390_563x117.heic)

The close relationship between the Thillai Brahmins and the Chola royal tradition is also reflected in *Periyapuranam*.

In the account of Kootruva Nayanar, a ruler traditionally associated with the Kalabhra lineage, Sekkizhar narrates how he approached the Thillai Brahmins and requested that they perform his coronation. They refused, declaring that the right to receive the crown from them belonged to the hereditary Chola rulers.

This episode illustrates the importance attached to the coronation tradition and the position occupied by the Thillai Brahmins in the Shaiva literary memory of the Chola country.

Even in the Nayanmar panels at Darasuram, constructed during Rajaraja II, the Thillai Dikshitars are shown as someone who are taking care of the temple (pic at the beginning of the article)

#### Revered by the Vaishnava Alwars too



An equally significant aspect of the Thillai Brahmins is their association with the worship of Lord Govindaraja Perumal at Thiruchitrakoodam within Chidambaram.

Thirumangai Alwar, in his *Periya Thirumozhi*, sings

**மூவாயிர நான்மறையாளர் நாளும்  
முறையால் வணங்க அணங்காய சோதி  
தேவாதி தேவன் திகழ்கின்ற தில்லைத்  
திருச்சித்ர கூடம்**

The Alwar describes Thillai Thiruchitrakoodam as the sacred abode where the three thousand Brahmins, learned in the four Vedas, offer their worship in the prescribed manner.

Kulasekhara Alwar similarly praises the Lord at Thillai:

**தில்லை நகர் திருச்சித்ர கூடந் தன்னுள்  
அந்தணர்களொரு மூவாயிரவர் ஏத்த  
அணிமணி ஆசனத்திருந்த அம்மான் தானே**

These references are noteworthy because they show that the three thousand Vedic Brahmins of Thillai were celebrated not only in Shaiva literature but also in the sacred hymns of the Vaishnava Alwars.

The association of the Thillai Brahmins with both traditions forms an important part of the religious history of Chidambaram, although the precise arrangements governing worship at the Govindaraja shrine have changed over the centuries.

#### Centuries of upheaval and the preservation of tradition



The history of Chidambaram has not been without periods of extraordinary danger and destruction.

During Malik Kafur’s invasion of the Tamil country in 1311 CE, the Persian chronicler Amir Khusrau described an attack on a place called Brahmastpuri, where a wealthy temple was plundered, sacred images destroyed, the heads of Brahmins rolled and considerable wealth seized, including 250 elephants. Brahmastpuri has been identified by some historians with Chidambaram.

Chidambaram also passed through later periods of warfare, political instability and disruption. Yet the worship of Lord Nataraja and the religious traditions associated with Thillai survived these turbulent centuries.

The continuity of these traditions owes much to generations of those who preserved them, including the Dikshitars whose entire way of life is centred on the service of Lord Nataraja.

For them, Chidambaram is not simply a place of employment or an institution from which they derive a livelihood. Their family life, religious discipline, ritual obligations, spiritual identity and inherited traditions are deeply intertwined with the temple.

This distinction is essential to understanding the present controversy.

#### The continuing struggle of the Thillai Dikshitars



It is therefore deeply unfortunate that a community accorded such an exalted position in the sacred literature of Tamilakam has been drawn into decades of legal disputes, administrative conflicts and public controversies over the very temple with which its identity is inseparably connected.

The struggle over the administration of the Chidambaram Nataraja Temple is not new. Successive attempts to bring the temple under greater governmental control have resulted in prolonged litigation. The Dikshitars have repeatedly been compelled to defend their traditional administrative rights through the courts.

Questions concerning temple administration, access to the Kanakasabai, the preservation of heritage structures and the management of temple properties have continued to generate disputes.

That a community with such an extraordinary religious heritage should once again find itself fighting to retain its traditional role in the very temple to which it has dedicated generations of service ought to concern every person who values the continuity of our religious institutions.

The Dikshitars should not have to live under the perpetual uncertainty that their traditional role in the temple may be curtailed whenever a fresh administrative dispute arises.

For centuries, the Thillai Dikshitars have been praised by the greatest voices of Tamil devotional literature. Sundarar placed them at the beginning of his *Thiruthonda Thogai*. Sekkizhar devoted verses to their religious discipline, scholarship and greatness. Kandaraditya celebrated their Vedic learning and their intimate association with Lord Nataraja. Even the Vaishnava Alwars recorded their place in the sacred landscape of Thillai.

A community revered in our sacred literature should not be reduced, in contemporary public discourse, to a perpetual object of suspicion and confrontation.

The larger question goes far beyond the Dikshitars themselves. It concerns the future of India’s living religious traditions.

Our temples are not merely ancient monuments. They are living institutions sustained by uninterrupted worship, inherited knowledge, ritual discipline and communities that have dedicated their lives to their service. The loss of such traditions cannot be measured merely in terms of buildings, properties or administrative control.

A tradition that has survived dynastic changes, invasions, political upheavals and centuries of historical uncertainty deserves thoughtful protection, not perpetual confrontation.

The Thillai Dikshitars are not merely custodians of a temple. They are custodians of a sacred continuity that forms an integral part of the religious heritage of Tamilakam.

Respecting the Dikshitars does not mean placing them above the law. It means ensuring that the law is applied fairly, that administrative concerns are resolved proportionately, and that their constitutionally protected religious rights are not treated as dispensable.

It would be a profound irony if a tradition that survived the turbulence of a thousand years were weakened, not by foreign invasions or the collapse of kingdoms, but by the inability of our own society to respect and preserve its living religious institutions.
