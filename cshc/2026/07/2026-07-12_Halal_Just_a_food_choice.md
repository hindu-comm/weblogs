Within contemporary socio-political discourse, the term ‘Halal’ is frequently reduced to a mere synonym for ritualistic animal slaughter. In colloquial South Asian parlance, for instance, idioms such as ‘halal karna’ have transitioned from denoting specific religious rites to serving as generalized metaphors for the act of killing. Even within politically informed circles, Halal is predominantly conceptualized as a religious dietary restriction. However, such a narrow interpretative lens fails to adequately capture the expansive sociopolitical and economic dimensions of the concept.

If the Halal paradigm is deliberately positioned as a vehicle for global economic influence, as articulated by figures such as Dr. Ceric, it becomes imperative to investigate whether it functions as a broader, systemic economic framework rather than a simple matter of consumer food choice. This article provides an analytical exploration of the multifaceted nature of the Halal economy, examining its scriptural foundations, its evolution into a regulatory mechanism, and its modern institutionalization within global trade.

### **What is Halal?**



Etymologically, ‘Halal’ derives from the Arabic root designating that which is “permissible” or “lawful.” In the context of Islamic jurisprudence (Fiqh), it denotes actions, behaviors, or objects that are expressly sanctioned under Sharia law. The Cambridge Dictionary aligns with this definition, characterizing Halal as being *“correct according to Islamic law.”* **\[2\]** Crucially, this classification extends far beyond the culinary realm; it functions as a comprehensive binary regulatory system alongside its antithesis, ‘Haram’ (forbidden). For instance, while polygyny is considered Halal under Islamic theology, the practice of charging Interest (*riba*) is strictly categorized as Haram. Consequently, the Halal-Haram dichotomy operates as a foundational ethical and legal compass that governs nearly all aspects of an adherent’s life, ranging from complex financial transactions to daily social conduct.

The determination of a product or practice’s Halal status relies on a hierarchical structure of theological authority, beginning with the primary Islamic scriptures: the Quran and the Hadith. While these texts provide an overarching moral and legal framework, they do not explicitly categorize every modern technological practice or synthesized product. Consequently, Islamic jurisprudence employs a foundational principle of permissibility, *Ibahah*: any action or substance is inherently deemed Halal unless it directly contradicts a specifically established prohibition. **\[3\]** Scriptural injunctions delineate these explicit prohibitions, thereby guiding the faithful. As outlined in Surah Al-Ma’idah:

> *“O believers! Honour your obligations. All grazing livestock has been
> made lawful to you… Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, and swine;
> what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah; what is
> killed by strangling, beating, a fall, or by being gored to death;
> what is partly eaten by a predator unless you slaughter it; and what
> is sacrificed on altars. You are also forbidden to draw lots for
> decisions. This is all evil… Say, “What is good and lawful. Also what
> is caught by your hunting animals and birds of prey which you have
> trained as instructed by Allah. So eat what they catch for you, but
> mention the Name of Allah over it ˹first˺”... Today all good, pure
> foods have been made lawful for you. Similarly, the food of the People
> of the Book is permissible for you and yours is permissible for
> them.”* **\[4\]**

These directives establish a clear boundary, categorizing substances such as carrion, blood, and porcine products as ‘Haram.’ Furthermore, specific methods of killing, such as strangulation or blunt force render an animal impermissible for consumption. Central to this process is the requirement that the slaughter be performed in the name of Allah, thereby excluding any meat sacrificed to other deities. These theological constraints form the basis for what is now known as the Halal industry, necessitating specialized oversight to ensure compliance throughout the supply chain.

The transition from individual adherence to institutionalized regulation has given rise to Halal Certification Bodies. These entities function as intermediaries that verify product compliance with Sharia. By scrutinizing the entire lifecycle of a product, from raw material sourcing to consumer delivery, these bodies ensure that no ‘Haram’ elements contaminate the process. This institutionalization marks the evolution of Halal from a personal religious obligation into a formalized global standard of quality and compliance.

### **Halal Certification Bodies**



Halal Certification Bodies (HCBs) are predominantly non-governmental organizations, private trusts, or corporate entities tasked with auditing and authenticating the Halal status of consumer goods and commercial services. Unlike state-mandated regulatory agencies, such as national food safety boards, these bodies operate as independent entities within a privatized regulatory market. Due to the decentralized nature of these Halal Certifying Bodies, there exists no single, universally codified global standard for Halal compliance. However, HCBs generally adhere to a shared set of baseline guidelines derived from core scriptural interpretations, resulting in significant procedural overlap across different international jurisdictions.

In the Indian domestic market, the regulatory landscape is heavily influenced by organizations such as the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind Halal Trust (JUHHT) and Halal India. The Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind ****\[5\]**, founded in 1919, originally emerged as a prominent assembly of Islamic scholars during the Khilafat Movement. This movement represented a significant geopolitical endeavor by Indian Muslims aimed at the preservation and restoration of the Ottoman Caliphate, which they regarded as their supreme spiritual and political authority. The historical lineage of such organizations indicates that the modern architecture of Halal certification is, in certain instances, deeply intertwined with broader historical religio-political movements, carrying institutional legacies that predate modern corporate frameworks.

In stark contrast to the historical and political origins of the JUHHT, organizations like Halal India ****\[6\]** represent the modern, neoliberal corporate facet of this expanding industry, having been established purely as a Private Limited Company in 2009. The following sections will analyze the historical trajectories of these organizations, with a particular focus on the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind, to better dissect their contemporary roles and history of its activities.

### **How to Obtain a Halal Certificate**



Having established the ontological and institutional foundations of the Halal paradigm, it is necessary to examine the operational guidelines and regulatory compliance mechanisms required for corporate certification. Because there is no universally codified, monolithic regulatory framework governing global Halal standards, examining the exact compliance criteria can be highly complex. However, analyzing the procedural guidelines stipulated by the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind Halal Trust (JUHHT) and Halal India, two of the dominant certification bodies within the Indian market provides critical insight into the mechanics of this privatized regulatory system.

It is crucial to disabuse the common misconception that Halal certification is solely a mechanism for regulating the meat and poultry sector. While the slaughter industry remains the most visible aspect of Halal compliance, it represents only a fraction of a sprawling, multi-sectoral economic ecosystem. Halal Certification Bodies like Halal India explicitly list a vast array of industries subject to their regulatory oversight, encompassing pharmaceuticals, fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG), cosmetics, packaging materials, and complex logistics and supply-chain networks. **\[7\]**

According to regulatory guidelines previously published by Halal India, which are now removed from its website but accessible through Archive Links **\[8\]**, detailing the protocols for Islamic slaughter, the certification process imposes strict, identity-based labor mandates across the production chain. These procedural requirements generally encompass the following operational stages:

- **Pre-slaughter Inspection:** The auditing and health inspection of
  the livestock to ensure they are free of disease must be conducted
  exclusively by Muslim personnel.

- **The Slaughtering Process:** The act of slaughter is restricted
  solely to qualified Muslim slaughterers, who must adhere to specific
  theological protocols. These include the recitation of the *Bismillah*
  (invocation of God), the precise severing of the windpipe and jugular
  vein, and aligning the animal toward the *Qiblah* (the direction of
  the Kaaba in Mecca). Furthermore, labor regulations mandate that these
  slaughterers rotate duties at strict half-hour intervals to maintain
  focus and adherence to ritual purity.

- **Post-slaughter Monitoring:** The verification of the animal’s death
  and the total exsanguination (draining of blood) must be supervised
  and confirmed exclusively by Muslim employees.

- **Packaging and Labeling:** The final affixation of the Halal
  compliance label onto the verified meat products is similarly
  restricted to Muslim staff.

These guidelines reveal a foundational structural reality of the Halal certification process: compliance is fundamentally tethered to religious identity within the labor force, mandating the employment of Muslim staff at nearly every critical node of production, from initial inspection to final consumer packaging and labelling. While these specific guidelines represent the framework of Halal India, similar labor religion based quotas are enforced by other regulatory bodies.

The JUHHT, for example, explicitly requires religious quotas for corporate applicants. According to its published standards, any restaurant or retail outlet seeking Halal classification must employ a minimum of two Muslim chefs. **\[9\]** Furthermore, certified slaughterhouses and production centers are obligated to hire a sufficient number of JUHHT trained and approved, on-site Muslim supervisors to physically verify ongoing compliance. **\[10\]** The financial burden of employing this specialized regulatory staff falls entirely upon the client corporation. Beyond the mandatory inclusion of Muslim supervisors, non-Muslim personnel within the facility are often required to undergo specialized training in Islamic jurisprudence, specifically the Halal and Haram dichotomy to ensure operational compliance across the workforce. **\[11\]**

Not only is it needed to employ a certain number of Muslims but it is also required that the companies applying for the Halal Certificate pay a certain fee to the Islamic trust or body from which they are obtaining the Halal Certificate. The acquisition of Halal certification introduces significant financial liabilities for the applicant corporation. The fees levied by these independent Islamic trusts and certification bodies are not nominal administrative processing charges; rather, they represent a substantial, tiered financial extraction model. A critical component of this fee architecture is the imposition of a “per-product”auditing charge. Crucially, in this regulatory context, a “product” does not denote a broad category of goods (e.g., “shampoo” or “juice”). Instead, the fee is levied on every individual variant or Stock Keeping Unit (SKU). For instance, if a cosmetics manufacturer produces ten distinct fragrances of a single shampoo line, each variant is classified as a separate product requiring individual certification fees. Similarly, in the food and beverage sector, each distinct flavor profile of a single item incurs a separate, recurring financial charge.

Furthermore, this financial model extends beyond static product certification to encompass dynamic supply chain logistics. Both the JUHHT and Halal India impose additional “per-container” or “per-shipment” fees. Consequently, an ongoing tariff is exacted on the volume of commercial goods exiting the manufacturing facility, fundamentally functioning as a privatized export tax on corporate logistics.

### **The Ever-Expanding Scope of Halal Certification**



Historically, within the Indian domestic market, the purview of Halal certification was largely confined to the meat and poultry sectors. However, contemporary market trajectories indicate a paradigm shift: the concept of Halal has transcended dietary constraints to encompass a vast, omnipresent economic ecosystem. The prevailing public assumption that Halal is merely a methodological classification for animal slaughter is analytically obsolete. The modern regulatory framework has aggressively expanded to govern not only manufactured physical goods but also intangible consumer services.

As outlined by the regulatory scope of Halal India **\[14\]**, the mandate for certification has permeated the FMCG sector, encompassing essential items of daily use, cosmetics, and personal care products. The regulatory umbrella now extends into agricultural inputs (such as animal feed), industrial manufacturing (packaging materials), and complex scientific sectors (pharmaceuticals). More significantly, the Halal paradigm is currently capturing the tertiary sector of the economy, encompassing services like logistics networks, tourism boards, healthcare facilities, and even Sharia-compliant real estate developments.

To comprehend the sheer scale of this expansion, it is necessary to examine how the concept of Halal is practically applied to the spatial and operational design of complex services:

- **Halal Hospitals:** The architecture and operational protocols of a
  Halal-certified hospital are fundamentally structured around the
  theological requirements of Muslim patients. Procedural mandates
  dictate the construction of designated prayer rooms on every floor,
  fully equipped with prayer garments, mats, and the Quran. Crucially,
  the delivery of medical care is subjected to strict gender
  segregation, ensuring that male physicians treat exclusively male
  patients, and female physicians treat female patients. Furthermore,
  the pharmaceutical supply chain and all medical equipment must be
  rigorously Halal-certified, completely devoid of porcine derivatives
  or alcohol. The institutional framework also requires the presence of
  a Muslim chaplain for spiritual counseling, specialized facilities for
  ritual ablution (Wudu) including the provision of purified sand
  (Tayammum) when water is unavailable and mandatory Halal compliance
  training for all hospital staff. **\[15\]**

- **Halal Residence:** The conceptualization of a Halal residence
  dictates that urban planning and interior architecture yield to the
  spatialization of religion. Consequently, apartment layouts are
  heavily oriented around religious practice: living spaces face Mecca
  (Qiblah), and the structural orientation of washrooms and prayer areas
  must adhere strictly to Islamic jurisprudential codes. Technological
  integration includes the installation of clocks synchronized with
  daily prayer timings, and acoustic architecture designed to ensure the
  Adhan (call to prayer) from adjacent mosques is audible throughout the
  complex. Additionally, residential blueprints mandate segregated
  spaces for ablution and specific sanitary fixtures, such as hand
  bidets, to facilitate ritual purity. **\[16\]**

- **Halal Tourism:** The Halal tourism industry mirrors the structural
  segregation and compliance seen in healthcare and real estate. Tourism
  itineraries and hospitality services are meticulously curated to
  eliminate exposure to Haram elements. Hotels seeking certification
  must guarantee that their dining facilities are exclusively
  Halal-certified and alcohol-free. Spatial planning within the hotel
  must accommodate prayer rooms, indicate the Qiblah in all guest
  suites, and enforce strict gender segregation across recreational
  facilities, including spas, gymnasiums, and swimming pools.
  Furthermore, travel itineraries are structurally modified to
  incorporate designated intervals for the five daily prayers (Salah),
  subordinating standard tourist scheduling to religious obligation.
  **\[17\]**

### **Analysing the Guidelines for Halal**



A critical synthesis of the aforementioned regulatory guidelines reveals a consistent structural mandate: the compulsory hiring of Muslim personnel and Madrasa-trained scholars (Alim) as embedded ‘Islamic affairs experts.’ **\[18\]** These individuals must be retained at the corporate client’s expense to physically monitor ongoing Sharia compliance. However, a significant disjuncture exists between these modern corporate mandates and traditional Islamic jurisprudence.

Scripturally, the necessity of a Muslim practitioner is strictly limited to the direct act of slaughter. Extending this religious requirement to ancillary supply chain roles—such as quality inspection, facility logistics, and product labeling—lacks explicit theological backing in primary texts. Consequently, these expanded mandates appear to function less as strict religious necessities and more as engineered labor market quotas. By structurally reserving employment opportunities for a specific religious demographic, the certification process inherently institutionalizes exclusionary employment practices that disadvantage non-Muslim labor.

This institutionalization of identity-based hiring quotas raises profound questions regarding systemic economic exclusion and its reception in public discourse. In contemporary Indian political debates, calls by localized Hindu organizations for the economic boycott of Muslim-owned businesses frequently attract significant domestic and international media condemnation. **\[19\]** Political entities, such as the Communist Party of India (CPI), routinely denounce such reactionary calls, often employing severe historical labels equating them to fascist and Nazi ideologies. **\[20\]** Ironically, Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind, the same organisation that theologically mandates discrimination and pushes for more Muslim employees to grant Halal certification also condemned these calls when they are nothing but a reflection of its own policies. **\[21\]**

Yet, a comparative analysis reveals a stark dichotomy in how systemic economic exclusion is framed. While localized boycott calls, often reactionary responses to immediate communal tensions are widely and correctly scrutinized, the Halal certification framework is frequently shielded from similar critique. Despite structurally mandating the exclusion of non-Muslim labor across vast swathes of the supply chain, this framework is routinely defended or normalized under the rubric of “religious freedom” or “minority rights.” This creates an analytical paradox within a constitutionally secular republic: discriminatory labor practices are corporatized and legitimized under the guise of religious compliance, effectively serving as an institutionalized economic boycott of non-Muslims.

The imposition of these theological guidelines within constitutionally secular spaces further strains the social fabric. As observed in the tertiary sector, the push for ‘Halal-certified’ hospitality and tourism requires the carving out of exclusive religious spaces (prayer rooms), articles (The Quran, Prayer mats) and markings (Qiblah). Regressive practices such as gender segregation must be enforced and that even all Non-Muslim Employees must undergo an Islamic Religious training to serve Muslim patients.

The public reception of these practices sharply contrasts with other cultural debates in the medical field. For instance, when the National Medical Commission (NMC) of India updated its official institutional emblem to include Lord Dhanvantari, a traditional Hindu Deity associated with Ayurveda **\[22\]** the move drew severe condemnation from the Indian Medical Association (IMA), the largest organisation of Doctors in India. The IMA publicly stated,

> *“The recent change in the NMC logo is not acceptable to the modern
> medical fraternity. The new logo gives a wrong message and will harm
> the scientific and secular nature of the commission… The new logo of
> the NMC is in contradiction with our fundamental values as doctors… It
> is not in conformity with the oath and the duty of doctors which is
> not towards any particular religion. Such a logo is also inconsistent
> with the dignity and decorum of an institution such as the NMC”*
> **\[23\]**

It is analytically striking that a symbolic, cultural modification to an institutional logo drew widespread accusations of compromising scientific secularism, while the actual, physical restructuring of medical facilities to enforce gender segregation and religious compliance escapes similar scrutiny. Instead, the corporatization of religious mandates in healthcare is frequently celebrated by market analysts as a pragmatic strategy for tapping into new consumer demographics, highlighting a profound double standard in how institutional secularism is defended.

Finally, the financial architecture of the Halal certification industry warrants rigorous scrutiny, particularly regarding its opaque fee structures. Beyond the initial fixed certification costs, the imposition of variable ‘per-product’ and ‘per-container’ levies presents a significant economic puzzle. Client corporations are already mandated to financially sustain the ‘Halal Affairs Experts’ embedded within their facilities. If these inspectors are already compensated for their labor in ensuring end-to-end Sharia compliance, the economic justification for additional, volume-based tariffs on departing shipments remains highly questionable.

In the absence of regulatory transparency and standardized financial reporting, these tiered levies begin to resemble what economists term ‘rent-seeking behavior.’ Within critical discourse, this financial extraction operates as an informal corporate tax or compliance levy, compelled by the implied threat of losing access to the global Muslim consumer base.

Even after paying and complying with all the guidelines for Halal Certification Muslims might not even buy from that business as generally advised by Islamic Scholars and Fatwas to purchase only from Muslims rather than Non-Muslims and have labelled it as Haram,

*“When a Muslim chooses not to buy from his Muslim brother for no reason, i.e., he has not been cheated or deceived, and the goods are not of poor quality, and he simply prefers to buy from a kaafir rather than from a Muslim with no justification – this is haraam, because it weakens the trade of the Muslims and makes them lose sales if this becomes a habit among the Muslim (consumers).”* **\[24\]**

### **From Halal Products to Halal Supply Chain to Halal economy**



Till now you already may have got an idea as to how starting from Halal certification individual products an entire Halal Supply Chain can be built. The transition from certifying isolated consumer goods to regulating entire supply chains represents a critical evolutionary phase in the Halal economy. For a supply chain to achieve comprehensive Halal compliance, every constituent node ranging from raw material procurement and manufacturing facilities to logistics and warehousing must independently secure certification. This vertically integrated compliance model necessitates the payment of recurring certification fees at multiple stages of production and distribution. From a critical political economy perspective, this structure functions as a mechanism of systemic rent extraction, whereby market access to a specific consumer demographic, Muslims, is made strictly contingent upon remitting cascading tariffs to private religious Islamic bodies.

The institutionalization of these supply chains fosters what can be structurally defined as a ‘parallel economy’, an exclusive macroeconomic subsystem characterized by non-transparent, privatized regulatory frameworks. A fundamental operational constraint of this system is its structural exclusivity: a certified Halal facility must entirely prohibit non-Halal production lines or cross-contamination. Consequently, corporations face a strategic dilemma: maintain bifurcated, redundant supply chains (Halal and non-Halal) or consolidate operations to reduce overhead costs.

Given that the broader demographic majority (such as Hindu consumers in the Indian market) generally exhibits price elasticity or consumer apathy regarding a product’s Halal status, corporations invariably default to a unified, Halal-compliant supply chain for the sake of economic efficiency. This market dynamic inevitably leads to supply chain convergence, systematically eroding consumer choice. Crucially, this convergence generates significant negative externalities for other religious minorities. For example, Sikh consumers, whose explicit theological injunctions mandate the consumption of Jhatka meat and expressly forbid Halal find themselves structurally marginalized as non-Halal alternatives disappear from the consolidated market.

The macroeconomic scale of this parallel economy is formidable. Contemporary market analyses value the global Halal ecosystem at approximately \$7 trillion USD, with aggressive growth projections estimating an expansion to \$9.5–\$10 trillion USD by 2030. A sectoral breakdown illustrates the expansive, cross-industry penetration of this regulatory framework:

- **Food and Beverages:** \$1.67 trillion USD

- **Modest Fashion/Clothing:** \$375 billion USD

- **Halal Pharmaceuticals:** \$108 billion USD

- **Halal Cosmetics:** \$84 billion USD

- **Halal Tourism & Hospitality:** \$189 billion USD

- **Islamic Finance:** \$4.5 trillion USD (As of 2022)

The dominance of Islamic finance—encompassing Sharia-compliant banking, investment, and insurance demonstrates that the Halal economy has evolved far beyond consumer retail, establishing itself as a foundational pillar of global capital markets.

Proponents of the Halal industry frequently cite the pervasive consumption of Halal products by non-Muslims as empirical evidence of a universal market preference driven by superior quality and hygiene standards. However, a rigorous market analysis suggests this consumption pattern is a byproduct of monopolization rather than active, informed consumer preference. The high volume of Halal consumption by out-groups is largely attributable to information asymmetry, general consumer apathy, and the aforementioned structural erosion of non-Halal alternatives. Assertions that Halal-certified products inherently possess a scientifically superior standard of quality remain empirically unsubstantiated.

Furthermore, the prevalent defense that Halal certification ensures hygienic, sanitized food preparation introduces a glaring regulatory redundancy. Modern secular states already operate robust, statutory food safety and sanitation apparatuses (such as the FSSAI in India or the FDA in the United States). These state authorities mandate stringent hygiene protocols across all commercial food production, independent of religious compliance. Consequently, private Halal certification bodies do not offer scientifically superior hygienic standards beyond those already enforced by secular state law; rather, they merely superimpose theological compliance onto pre-existing, state-mandated health regulations, monetizing a baseline standard of cleanliness that the state already requires.

### **Why do Muslims promote Halal?**



Despite the staggering macroeconomic valuation of the global Halal ecosystem, it is necessary to analyze how this multi-trillion-dollar parallel economy translates into tangible, structural advantages. Recall the foundational statement introduced at the beginning of this analysis, wherein Dr. Mustafa Ceric explicitly articulated the ambition of “Conquering the world through the Halal movement”. As multinational corporations increasingly consolidate their operations into singular, Halal-compliant supply chains to minimize overhead costs, non-Halal alternatives are systematically disappearing from the mainstream market. This structural convergence effectively forces Shariah-compliant products onto a captive demographic of non-Muslim consumers who, under conditions of true market sovereignty, might not have actively elected such consumption.

However, the rapid expansion of this ecosystem is driven by profound macroeconomic and geopolitical rationales rather than mere lifestyle regulation. As articulated by Sheikh Abdullah Saleh Kamel, chairman of the Islamic Chamber of Commerce and Development (ICCD), the Halal economy is strategically positioned as “the next engine of growth for the Islamic world… that will lift 57 Muslim-majority nations into sustained prosperity”.

By structurally mandating identity-based employment quotas and institutionalizing a rigid system of recurring certification tariffs, the Halal economy functions as a highly effective mechanism for capital accumulation and wealth transfer. These regulatory frameworks guarantee specialized employment opportunities exclusively for Muslim labor while ensuring the continuous extraction of corporate capital by private Islamic trusts. Consequently, the proliferation of this system inherently generates localized and transnational economic advantages that directly empower Islamic institutions.

To facilitate this commercial expansion beyond strictly religious demographics, there has been a concerted, strategic effort to universalize the appeal of Halal products. Certification bodies and industry advocates continuously rebrand the Halal paradigm as a universal benchmark of quality assurance, frequently conflating theological compliance with ethical, hygienic, and health-conscious manufacturing attributes. This calculated positioning is designed to capture not only the observant Muslim demographic but also secular, health-conscious, and ethically driven consumer markets worldwide, thereby obfuscating the underlying religious mandates beneath a modern veneer of consumer safety and ethical capitalism.

Ultimately, these concerted market strategies serve as a potent vehicle for expanding geoeconomic soft power and achieving regulatory capture across global trade networks. The paramount example of this state-sponsored strategy is the Department of Islamic Development Malaysia (JAKIM). Operating as a transnational regulatory authority, JAKIM functions as a sophisticated instrument of religious legitimacy and bureaucratic soft power. Because its institutional certification is formally recognized across 47 countries, securing JAKIM compliance is highly advantageous—and frequently mandatory—for multinational corporations seeking access to lucrative Muslim-majority markets. Thus, the deliberate global promotion of the Halal economy transcends religious observance; it operates as a systematic mechanism for consolidating global market share, accumulating institutional wealth, and projecting systemic soft power on the international stage.

### **The History of Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind**



To fully comprehend the institutional ethos driving the contemporary Halal certification apparatus, a historical analysis of its primary regulatory bodies is essential. Therefore, it is necessary to examine the foundational ideology and subsequent sociopolitical activities of the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind (JUH) since its inception in 1919.

The organizational genesis of the JUH occurred following the Khilafat Conference held in Delhi on November 23, 1919. In the aftermath of this gathering, twenty five prominent Islamic scholars convened to establish a distinct clerical assembly. The broader Khilafat Movement was fundamentally anchored in Pan-Islamism, a geopolitical and theological ideology that fostered extra-territorial allegiances, inevitably dividing the civic loyalties of Indian Muslims. By effectively mobilizing the Muslim populace, irrespective of socioeconomic status, toward the preservation of the Ottoman Caliphate and the sovereign authority of the Khalifa in Turkey, the movement entrenched transnational religious solidarity over Indian nationalism. Consequently, Pan-Islamism was established as the foundational ideological bedrock of the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind.

The ideological genealogy of the JUH can be directly traced back to Shah Waliullah Dehlavi, an influential 18th-century Islamic revivalist. Historical analyses of Shah Waliullah’s treatises reveal a staunch Pan-Islamist framework and a rigid adherence to Islamic supremacy, characterized by a deep-seated hostility toward the indigenous Hindu populace. Notably, Shah Waliullah actively petitioned the Afghan ruler Ahmad Shah Abdali to invade the Indian subcontinent in order to curtail the expanding military and political dominance of the Maratha Empire. The following excerpts from Shah Waliullah’s writings vividly illustrate the militant and supremacist theological roots from which the JUH draws its ideological inspiration:

> *“I saw myself in a dream that I am Qaem al-Zaman (master of the age).
> Which means that when God Almighty wanted to establish a system of
> goodness and benevolence, then He made me a tool and medium for the
> fulfillment of this noble cause. And I saw that the king of the
> infidels took over the land of the Muslims and looted their property.
> He enslaved their women and children and in the city of Ajmer he
> declared the rites of disbelief. He eradicated the rites of Islam.
> Then after that I saw that the Almighty became angry and very angry
> with the people of the earth. And I witnessed the embodiment of the
> wrath of the Almighty in the heavens. And then dripping from there,

Regarding the cultural segregation of the Muslim diaspora and supremacy of Arab culture, Shah Waliullah further decreed:

> *“Muslims, no matter where they live, wherever they spend their
> youthful days, they should in any case be completely separated from
> the natives of that country in their culture, traditions and
> mannerisms. And wherever they are, they must be immersed in their

These passages unequivocally demonstrate that Shah Waliullah advocated for a consolidated, transnational Muslim Brotherhood grounded in civilizational separation. Analytically, such rigid ideals are fundamentally incompatible with the pluralistic, integrative frameworks required by modern, secular nation-states.

This Pan-Islamist paradigm is deeply embedded within the foundational texts of the JUH. The organization’s fourth president, Syed Hussain Ahmad Madani, authored the widely cited treatise *Composite Nationalism and Islam*. In contemporary discourse, self-proclaimed secularists frequently elevate this text as the definitive epitome of religious harmony, citing it as proof of a Muslim scholar boldly opposing the ‘Two-Nation Theory.’

However, a rigorous textual analysis reveals this to be a profound mischaracterization. In reality, the text functions as a Pan-Islamist manifesto wrapped in the palatable terminology of composite nationalism. Within this very book, Madani writes:

> *“The Prophet of Islam, Hazrat Muhammad created a bond among Muslims
> that is superior to all bonds. In comparison, bonds such as
> nationalism are pale and colorless. A Muslim, whoever and wherever he
> is, is the brother of another Muslim and he has right over other
> Muslims. This bond of rabita (relationships) exists only among those
> who have accepted Islam. In the case of those who have not embraced
> Islam, this bond cannot be established.”*

Despite its title, Madani’s treatise ultimately discards the framework of nationalism in favor of supreme, transnational religious allegiance. Read through this lens, the JUH’s historical opposition to the Partition of India and the Two-Nation Theory was not rooted in a profound sense of Indian patriotism or secular national identity. Rather, the Jamiat opposed partition because the geographic division of the subcontinent would result in a fractured Ummah (global Muslim community), thereby weakening the demographic hegemony and consolidated political power of Muslims within South Asia.

This ideology of Pan-Islamism is not merely a relic of pre-partition India; it remains the operational philosophy of the JUH today. A prominent example occurred following the devastating 2005 earthquake in Pakistan, during which the Jamiat provided extensive humanitarian aid. While providing disaster relief to aggrieved populations is objectively commendable, the institutional justification provided by the JUH reveals its underlying priorities. As stated on their official website:

> “The gesture also demonstrated that when it comes to helping those in
> distress, the Jamiat keeps past enmities and strife aside for the sake
> of Islam and the Prophet, and has maintained the duty of the believer
> to help those in need.”

By explicitly framing humanitarian assistance through the lens of “helping fellow believers” and acting “for the sake of Islam,” the JUH clearly articulates a Pan-Islamist worldview that supersedes geopolitical boundaries. This rhetoric invites a critical inquiry regarding the extent of their extra-territorial allegiances: in the event of an armed conflict with Pakistan, would the Jamiat theoretically cite this same duty to set aside national strife to assist fellow believers across the border?

This tendency was further evidenced by the JUH’s intervention regarding Rohingya Muslims seeking refugee status in Bangladesh. ### **Where does the money go?**



IGiven the lucrative nature of the Halal certification industry, it is imperative to investigate the financial trajectory of the capital accumulated by organizations like the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind. Considering the organization’s historical and ideological predispositions, the allocation of these unregulated corporate fees warrants intense scrutiny.

Fundamentally, as Islamic institutions, Halal certifying bodies are theologically mandated to disburse *Zakat* (obligatory Islamic charity). According to primary Quranic injunctions, the permissible avenues for the utilization of Zakat funds are strictly codified:

> “Zakat is only for the poor and the needy, for those employed to
> administer it, for those whose hearts are attracted ˹to the faith˺,
> for ˹freeing˺ slaves, for those in debt, for Allah’s cause, and for
> ˹needy˺ travellers. ˹This is˺ an obligation from Allah. And Allah is
> All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

In classical Islamic jurisprudence, Zakat does not function as a universal, secular philanthropy; rather, it is an exclusive, intra-religious financial transfer. The “poor and needy” referenced in the scripture strictly designates Muslims. The sole theological allowance for dispersing Zakat to a non-Muslim is explicitly for the purpose of religious conversion (”those whose hearts are attracted to the faith”). Traditional jurisprudential texts consistently affirm: “It is not permissible to give zakat to a non-Muslim.”

Furthermore, one of the primary recipients of Zakat is designated as “for Allah’s cause.” In classical exegesis, this is universally interpreted as funding for those engaged in Jihad. As detailed in the authoritative manual of Islamic jurisprudence, The Reliance of the Traveller:

> “Those fighting for Allah, meaning people engaged in Islamic military
> operations for whom no salary has been allotted in the army roster (O:
> but who are volunteers for jihad without remuneration). They are given
> enough to suffice them for the operation, even if affluent; of
> weapons, mounts, clothing, and expenses (O: for the duration of the
> journey, round trip, and the time they spend there, even if prolonged.
> Though nothing has been mentioned here of the expense involved in
> supporting such people’s families during this period, it seems clear
> that they should also be given it).”

While scriptural mandates outline the theological justification for utilizing institutional funds for military or terror-related activities, empirical realities must dictate the analysis of the JUH. Accusations of terror funding cannot rely solely on ancient scripture; they must be evaluated against the contemporary actions of the organization.

In examining the institutional activities of the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind, a clear pattern emerges regarding their allocation of legal and financial resources. The JUH has controversially positioned itself as a premier provider of legal aid for Muslims accused of terrorism. The organization has financed the legal defense of at least 700 individuals charged with terror-related offenses. Through aggressive litigation, the JUH has secured acquittals in 192 cases—frequently leveraging procedural technicalities or citing a lack of evidentiary proof.

The institutional capital accumulated by the JUH has been utilized to actively defend the accused in several of India’s most devastating mass-casualty events. A representative catalog of the high-profile terror cases litigated by the Jamiat includes:
