In the seventy eighth year of a king remembered as the Great Moga, on the fifth day of the month Panemos, an official named Patika buried relics of the Buddha on the outskirts of Taxila and built a monastery over them. We know because he wrote it down. His scribe hammered the record into a sheet of copper, and the copper survived where the monastery did not.

That one document repays some staring. Panemos is a Macedonian month; Alexander’s calendar was still running in the Punjab three centuries after his soldiers left. The script is Kharoshthi and the language is Prakrit, the ordinary speech of the northwest. Patika’s father, Liaka Kusulaka, held the rank of satrap, which is a Persian office, and governed the district of Chukhsa for an overlord. The overlord is Moga, whom historians call Maues, and he was none of the above, neither Greek nor Persian nor Indian. He was a Scythian, a horse archer whose people had come down off the central Asian steppe within living memory, the first of them ever to rule an Indian city. And what the satrap’s son did with the family money, a generation or so into the conquest, was endow a Buddhist shrine. Nobody involved seems to have found any of this worth remarking on.

The keepers of the Sanskrit tradition would not have described the scene at Taxila so calmly. In their vocabulary a foreigner with no place in the four ranks of society was a mleccha, a barbarian whose speech sounded like babble, and a barbarian on an Indian throne was not politics at all. It was an omen, the kind of thing a chronicler recorded alongside failed rains and comets. In the Mahabharata the sage Markandeya tells Yudhishthira exactly what the reign of such people will mean. He is describing the arrival of the dark age itself:

> Andhras, Shakas, Pulindas and Yavanas become kings... Not a single
> brahmana makes a living from his own dharma then.
>
> āndhrāḥ śakāḥ pulindāś ca yavanāś ca narādhipāḥ \|
>
> kāmbojā aurṇikāḥ śūdrās tathābhīrā narottama \|\|

The Shakas of that verse are Patika’s people. So the tradition’s official position was that Saka kingship meant the age itself was dying, and among the earliest Saka royal documents found in India is the founding record of a Buddhist monastery. A historian named Sudhakar Chattopadhyaya once compressed the ending into two sentences, and they can stand at the head of the whole account:

Ancient India has always captured her captors. The Sakas were also captured and completely Indianised, adopting Indian mode of life.

This essay is an extended version of his second sentence.

### **King of Kings**



Begin with the man on the copper plate, because almost everything about him had to be pieced together from his money.

Somewhere around 85 BCE, the Greek kingdoms of the northwest, the leftovers of Alexander’s Bactrian adventure that had ruled Gandhara for a century, stopped issuing coins in one city after another, and a new name starts appearing in the sequence: Maues. The numismatist David MacDowall, who spent his life sorting these mints, puts it dryly. Into the tidy sequence of Greek kings, he writes, “the Indo Scythian Maues king of kings suddenly intrudes.”

He had married into one of the Greek royal families, and on his coins he claimed an inheritance: he copied the elephant headdress of Demetrios, the Bactrian king who had first carried Greek arms into India, announcing himself the next conqueror in that line. He copied, too, the horse and bow of Mithradates of Parthia. A man with no ancestors in the country was manufacturing a pedigree out of other kings’ coins, Greek on one face, Iranian on the other.

The title he chose is the most telling theft of all. The Greek kings of India had been content with basileus, king. Maues took basileos basileon megalou, Great King of Kings, the style of the Persian emperors, which the Parthian rulers of Iran had claimed for themselves. He grabbed it in the very years when Parthia, convulsed after the death of its great king Mithradates, could not defend the title’s dignity. A refugee chieftain in the Punjab was announcing, in Greek letters, on Indian silver, that he was the heir of Cyrus. The Kharoshthi face of the same coins translates it for his subjects: rajatirajasa mahatasa, of the great king of kings. His people had come to India at the end of a long chain of defeats. On the Chinese frontier the Xiongnu had broken the Yuezhi; the Yuezhi, moving west, broke the Sakas; and the Sakas, with the grasslands closed behind them, came down through the passes into the Punjab. Maues turned that refugee column into a kingdom within one reign

The administration underneath was Persian and practical. A great satrap held a district, a son served beside him as junior satrap and learned the trade, and the office passed down the family. Liaka Kusulaka held Chukhsa near Taxila on those terms, and it was his son Patika who buried the Buddha’s relics under a monastery in year 78 of the Moga era. Away to the west, in Sistan and Arachosia, a related royal house ran the same arrangement at a higher level, Vonones as King of Kings with his brothers Spalahores and Spalirises serving as viceroys and succeeding in their turn. Keep the structure in mind, because nearly every Saka in the rest of this essay holds one of these offices. The oldest document of their rule in India that survives on metal is, as we have seen, a Buddhist endowment by a Persian office holder writing Prakrit.

After Maues the throne of the northwest passed to a house whose coins carry the name Azes, and here the money tells a story of Indian consolidation. Azes finished what Maues began: the last Greek kings of the Punjab, Hippostratus west of the Jhelum and the Stratos east of it, disappear from the record, their mints taken over, their monograms continued by Scythian engravers who simply kept working for new management. An era was counted from Azes, year one falling in the 50s BCE, and Buddhist donors in the hills would go on dating their reliquaries by “the year of the late Great King Azes” for a century after he was dust, the way a man keeps his father’s watch running. Whether there was one Azes or two, an Azes I and an Azes II with a king called Azilises between them, is still argued among the coin men; the old reconstruction sees three reigns, a newer one folds them into a single long rule. It changes nothing in the larger story. What matters is what the dynasty put on its silver. The royal portrait, the Greek bust that every king from Menander onward had worn on the front face of his coins, was dropped. In its place rides a king on horseback with a couched lance, a steppe rider at full seat, and he would stay there, reign after reign, the trademark of Saka kingship. On the reverse, Greek gods went on standing guard, Zeus, Athena, Herakles. But new figures kept slipping in among them, and they were not Greek. On silver of the house appears a goddess flanked by two elephants that raise their trunks and pour water over her head. Any Indian holding that coin knew her at sight: Gaja Lakshmi, the goddess of royal fortune bathed by elephants, one of the oldest good luck images in India. A Scythian king was paying his soldiers with the blessing of an Indian goddess.

The dynasty reached east as well. At Mathura, the holy city on the Yamuna, a line of Saka satraps established itself: two obscure figures named Hagamasha and Hagana first, then a governor called Rajuvula, whose coins say two different things depending on which face you read. The Greek legend styles him King of Kings, the Saviour. The Kharoshthi on the back records the actual job, chatrapasa, the satrap. His monument survives, a red sandstone capital of two lions back to back, found at Mathura and now in the British Museum; Rapson, describing it a century ago, called its style strikingly Iranian. The Kharoshthi crowded over its surface is mostly donation records, and the donors are mostly women: Rajuvula’s chief queen installing relics of the Buddha, her kinswomen adding gifts of their own to the sangha, the Buddhist order. One name among them is an old acquaintance, Patika of the copper plate, grown by now into a great satrap himself. Rajuvula’s son Sodasa succeeded him at Mathura, took the great satrap title, and left dated votive tablets among the Jains as well. Two generations in from the steppe, and this one family was funding most of the religions on offer in the city.

Off in the Bajaur hills, on the empire’s mountain rim, a small allied dynasty called the Apracarajas kept its own court, and its surviving archive is of a kind no other royal house in the ancient world can match: it consists almost entirely of receipts for piety. Follow the caskets down the generations:

- **Vijayamitra the king** finds an old relic deposit of the Buddha
  broken and neglected, and personally restores it, recording that the
  relics “are not honoured and so have perished over time.”

- **Prince Indravarma** takes relics from a crumbling stupa of the
  Mauryan age, two centuries old already, and re buries them “in a
  central location that is without danger, without trouble.”

- The same Indravarma receives a gift from the Saka lord Kharahostes: a
  silver drinking cup, chased with an ibex in the animal style of the
  steppe, the kind of vessel Scythian chiefs had drunk from since
  Herodotus. Indravarma has it inscribed and buries it as a Buddhist
  reliquary, which is where the excavators found it.

- **Utara, a general’s wife**, founds a stupa, a relic mound, “at a
  previously unestablished location,” ordinary piety by a woman whose
  name we would otherwise never know.

- **Queen Rukhana’s** dedication is dated three ways in a single
  sentence: in the twenty seventh year of the reign of Vijayamitra, in
  the seventy third year of Azes, in the two hundred and first year of
  the Greeks. Three calendars running at once, Scythian, Indian
  frontier, Macedonian, and the thing being dated by all three is a
  Buddhist relic.

- And one family reliquary ends its list of honoured relatives with a
  line that leaps every fence of dynasty and tribe: “The community of
  all beings is honoured. All beings are brought to nirvana.”

The northwest empire itself ended the way it had begun, at the hands of newcomers off the same roads. First came Gondophares, a Parthian, whose dynasty folded the Saka kingdoms into an Indo Parthian realm around 19 CE. His court has the odd distinction of a walk on part in Christian scripture. The apocryphal Acts of Thomas, written within two centuries of his reign, opens with the apostle being sold into service for India:

> a certain merchant, an Indian, whose name was Habban; and he was sent
> by the king Gudnaphar, that he might bring to him a skilful carpenter.

Gudnaphar is Gondophares, and his name, worn down through the centuries, became Gaspar, which is why one of the three Magi of the Christmas story bears it to this day. A Saka era king of the Punjab stands in every nativity scene in Europe. A visitor of the same generation, the sage Apollonius of Tyana, is said to have reached Taxila around the year 44 and found a city “about the size of Nineveh, walled like a Greek city,” ruled by a king schooled by brahmanas. His biographer embroiders freely, but the picture fits what the excavations found.

Then, within a generation, the Kushans arrived, kings of those same Yuezhi who had first shoved the Sakas off their pastures a century and a half before. The shove had followed them all the way to the Indus. Excavators at Taxila found the transition inside a single buried hoard: coins of Gondophares’ governors, of his successor Pacores, and of the Kushan Vima, all put away together. Further east the change of masters survives in an inscription from Sarnath, dated in the third year of the Kushan emperor Kanishka, which names the great satrap Kharapallana and the satrap Vanaspara, Saka officers now serving a Yuezhi lord. The dynasty of Maues had lasted about a hundred and fifty years.

In outline the northwest story is short. Maues took Taxila and Gandhara around 85 BCE and called himself King of Kings. The house of Azes finished off the Greek kingdoms after him and left behind the era that donors counted by. Liaka Kusulaka and Patika governed Chukhsa for them; Hagamasha, Hagana, Rajuvula and Sodasa held Mathura; Vijayamitra’s Apracarajas held Bajaur and buried the relic caskets; men like Kharahostes and his son Mujatria, Zeionises and Aspavarman filled the offices in between. Gondophares and his Parthians took the whole structure over around 19 CE, and the Kushans took it from them within a generation or two, keeping the Saka satraps at their desks.

The Saka story, though, was only half done. Its weight had shifted six hundred miles south, to the richest coastline in India, where the longest lived of their kingdoms was getting established. Worth carrying one object south from the wreck of the northwest: a coin of the house of Azes, the steppe lancer on one face, Lakshmi on the other.

### **The kingdom that would not die**



The western Sakas began, as their cousins had, with almost nothing on the record but money. The first of them, Bhumaka, is a name on coins and nowhere else, but the coins carry real information. They carry the arrow and thunderbolt of the old northwestern issues, and they carry the device of a lion capital, the same emblem that crowned the Saka monument at Mathura. Someone from the northern satrapal world had come south, down the Indus and around the coast, and set up in Gujarat.

His successor Nahapana is far better documented, because for him we have an outside witness with no stake in any dynasty’s claims. Around the middle of the first century CE a Greek merchant captain wrote a pilot’s handbook of the Indian Ocean trade, the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a working document about winds, anchorages and what sells where. Passing the mouths of the Indus he records what had become of the old Saka heartland under its new Parthian masters:

> inland behind it is the metropolis of Scythia, Minnagara; it is
> subject to Parthian princes who are constantly driving each other out.

But when he reaches the gulf of Barygaza, the great port near modern Bharuch where Roman wine and silver came ashore and cotton and ivory went out, he names the king whose officers a trader will deal with: Nambanus, which is Nahapana with Greek vowels. Whatever the scriptures had said about riders of the end of the age, what a foreign merchant actually met on this coast was a working customs administration with a Saka king above it. Nahapana’s own inscriptions let you watch him climb: plain raja in one record, kshatrapa a little later, and at Junnar, near the end of his reign, mahakshatrapa svami, great satrap and lord. (Sircar thought the Maratha royal title Chhatrapati may preserve a distant echo of that same word, kshatrapa, though the derivation is argued.)

The most vivid man at his court was his son in law. Ushavadata, married to Nahapana’s daughter Dakshamitra, governed the southern districts and carved his good deeds into the cave temples at Nasik, in a long inscription written to be read. The inscription itemises the record. Three hundred thousand cows given away. Gold, and flights of steps down to the river Barnasa. Sixteen villages to gods and brahmanas. A hundred thousand brahmanas fed every year, his own count. Eight brahmana weddings at Prabhasa, paid from his purse. Free ferries on seven rivers he names one by one, with rest houses, wells and tanks on the roads between. And on the hill at Nasik itself, a cave for the Buddhist monks, with cisterns, plus money lodged with the town guilds so the yearly interest would buy the monks their cloth. Standard royal charity, done at scale, for both religions at once. When the neighbouring Malayas besieged an allied tribe, Nahapana sent this same man to break the siege, and his inscription reports the result with a soldier’s economy: the Malayas fled, he says, as it were at the sound of my approach.

The Kshaharata house fell in one war. The Satavahanas, the great power of the Deccan plateau whom Nahapana had been squeezing out of their northern districts, struck back under Gautamiputra Satakarni, and the physical evidence of what happened turned up in a field at Jogalthambi in 1906: a hoard of more than thirteen thousand of Nahapana’s silver coins, over two thirds of them struck over with Gautamiputra’s dies. Kings restruck captured treasuries rather than melt them down, so the hoard is a battlefield report in metal. No coin of any later Kshaharata exists, and no inscription names one; the family ends with Nahapana. The Satavahana court said so itself, in a queen’s inscription at Nasik that calls Gautamiputra the uprooter of the Kshaharata race and the restorer of the glory of the Satavahanas.

That could have been the end of Saka rule in the west. Instead, within a generation, a new Saka house rose at Ujjain, the sacred city of central India, and stayed for three centuries. Its founder Chastana was known as far away as Alexandria; the geographer Ptolemy, mapping India from sailors’ reports, records Ozene, the city of king Tiastenes. The house Chastana planted, the Western Kshatrapas as historians call them, ruled Gujarat, Kathiawar and Malwa for three hundred years. Their silver, small neat coins with the king’s head on one face and a hill and river sign on the other, is dated year by year in an unbroken series that numismatists treasure as the most complete chronological run in ancient India, and the era those dates count from, year one falling in 78 CE, became so identified with the dynasty that India simply calls it the Saka era. Keep the era in mind; it comes back at the end of this essay.

Chastana founded the line, and an inscription from Kutch, dated in the year 52 of the era, catches him in old age sharing the throne with his grandson; his own son had died too early to reign. The grandson was Rudradaman, whose rock at Junagadh gets its own section below. After Rudradaman the royal names begin to repeat themselves, Rudrasena, Rudrasimha, Rudradaman again, sons succeeding brothers succeeding cousins for a century and a half. The repetition is itself a confession. Rudra is a name of Shiva, and the royal house of the western Sakas was naming its heirs, generation after generation, for an Indian god. And because nearly every one of these kings dated his silver, the modern catalogues can follow the family year by year in a way that is possible for no other dynasty of ancient India. The record thins after 300. The satraps grow smaller, the coins grow lighter, and the last man in the line, Rudrasimha III, strikes his final dated silver in 388.

The dynasty was humiliated by Satavahana overlords for a stretch, shouldered aside twice by usurpers from the Abhira herdsmen clans, and reduced, in the late third century, to satellites of Sassanid Persia, whose emperors appointed a prince with the title Sakanshah, king of the Sakas, to mind the eastern marches. In the confusion one Saka officer near Sanchi, a general named Sridharavarman, declared himself an independent prince and left his own dated inscription by the great stupa, in effect a private Saka principality in the heart of Malwa. The dynasty came through all of it. The line of Chastana was still issuing its little dated silver at the end of the 380s CE, when the rest of the ancient world’s map had been redrawn twice over. It took the greatest empire India had produced in five hundred years to finish them: Chandragupta II of the Gupta line, who conquered the last great satrap, Rudrasimha III, around the year 400 and closed the series. From Maues at Taxila to Rudrasimha at Ujjain, Saka kings had reigned on Indian soil for very nearly five hundred years, longer than the Mughals, longer than the British would manage.

Out at the far western edge of this world, where Indian rule shaded into desert Baluchistan, one more Saka dynasty deserves a look before we go south. A small dynasty called the Paratarajas, kings of the Paratas, minted coins there for two centuries, a royal bust on one face and on the other a swastika, the old solar sign that Persian tradition read as the wheel of Mithra. The legend circling the swastika follows one formula, the king, his father, and the title, king of the Paratas, and the king list those legends preserve reads like a theological document in its own right. Watch the names walk south:

- **Yolamira**: Warrior Mithra. The founder, who also endowed a Buddhist
  water hall for the monks; the potsherds recording it were dug up by
  Aurel Stein.

- **Bagamira**: Lord Mithra.

- **Arjuna**: the third Pandava, hero of the Bhagavad Gita.

- **Hvaramira**: Glorious Mithra.

- **Mirahvara and Miratakhma**: Mithra’s Glory, Heroic Mithra.

- **Kozana**: the first of the line to add Kharoshthi legends to his
  coins.

- **Bhimarjuna**: Bhima and Arjuna, two Pandava heroes soldered into a
  single king.

- **Koziya and Datayola**: the late kings, minting to the end.

A family named ****for the old Iranian god of light, salting its line, generation by generation, with heroes out of the Mahabharata. The best guess of the numismatists is the obvious one, that Arjuna and Bhimarjuna were the sons of Indian wives. Somewhere on the desert rim of the Saka world, the epic that had listed the Sakas among the signs of the end of the world was being raided for baby names.

### **The gods on the coins**



A state can lie in its chronicles, but its money betrays it. The coins of the Sakas record a religious history that no chronicle bothered to write down.

The Greek kings of India had run a tidy system: the king’s portrait on the front of the coin, the king’s patron god on the back, Greek legend obverse, Prakrit reverse for the Indian subjects. The Sakas kept the machinery, the mints, even the engravers, and then, over about a century, changed every god in the pantheon without ever announcing a conversion. The scholar Percy Gardner said it best over a century ago: Maues and his successors had secured artists who had been instructed by Greeks but were not restricted by Greek traditions. What those artists produced was Greek outer clothing for gods who were no longer Greek underneath.

Some of the newcomers were the gods the Sakas had carried with them off the steppe, Iranian gods in borrowed dress. A radiate goddess on the coppers wears the look of Greek Artemis, but she had been worshipped in Bactria as Anahita, the Iranian lady of the waters. A smith god in Greek costume, Hephaistos to any Mediterranean eye, appears on a rare copper of the house of Azes; the numismatists read him as Atar, the Iranian genius of fire, son of the Wise Lord, the flame that carries a king’s royal glory. And glory itself, the khvarenah, the divine fire of legitimate kingship that Iranian tradition says settles on a rightful ruler, seems to flicker on these coins in the hands of goddesses who hold, instead of a cornucopia, a vessel of flame. Fire priests had ridden with the wagons, and the mints knew it.

But the traffic that mattered historically ran the other way, from India up into the dies. The humped zebu bull that walks across coppers of Maues and Azes was the badge of the city of Pushkalavati, and an old Greek lexicon preserves what everyone in Gandhara knew, that the bull was the god of the region; he is also, and was already becoming, the mount of Shiva. On one Maues copper stands a god with a club and a plough. No Greek carried a plough. He is Balarama, brother of Krishna, the farmer hero of the Yamuna country, and he had first been struck by a Greek king, Agathocles, whose mint had tested the Indian market a century earlier; the Saka engravers brought him back. A wheel appears beside a city goddess, and the wheel, the cakra, is the flag of Vishnu. The elephants pour their water over Lakshmi. God by god, the reverse dies of a Scythian state were turning into a gallery of the Indian pantheon before that pantheon had temples of carved stone anywhere in the land.

And then there is the trident, the story the coins tell better than any text in existence. It begins as Poseidon, the Greek sea lord with his three pronged spear, an odd guest in the landlocked Punjab. On coins of Maues and the house of Azes the trident god does something no Greek Poseidon does: he plants his foot on a small crouching figure beneath him. Scholars raised on Greek art saw a river god being subdued. The Indian art historians saw something else at once, the oldest formula of Shaiva sculpture, Shiva trampling the dwarf demon Apasmara, ignorance crushed underfoot. On the silver of Azes the god with the trident is by now, as the cautious numismatic phrasing has it, sometimes described as Zeus, sometimes as Poseidon, but perhaps intended as Shiva. The mints themselves settled the question in the following decades. When Gondophares the Parthian took over the Indus valley, the first reverse his engravers cut was the trident god, and when the Kushans built their imperial coinage a generation later they labelled the same figure in Greek letters: Oesho, who is Shiva by name in the Bactrian tongue, bull, trident and all. The god who had entered the dies as Poseidon was being worshipped off them as Shiva, and the change had happened on Saka silver.

The Kushans carried the process into gold. The emperor Kanishka put the whole religious world of his realm onto his dinars, Iranian gods, Greek gods, Indian gods, each labelled in Greek script. A few of those coins, only about six are known in gold, carry a figure in a monk’s robe with a halo, right hand raised, and the name beside him reads BODDO. Collectors have fought over those six coins for a century, and scholars over every detail of the image. What nobody disputes is the fact underneath: a dynasty two generations off the steppe chose to mint the Buddha. Patika had recorded the same instinct on copper at Taxila, two hundred years earlier

### **Who they fought, and why**



The Sakas fought through the whole of their five centuries in India. Reading the list of their wars, what strikes me most is how many of the enemies end up, sooner or later, as kin or overlord or neighbour inside one political world.

The first war, against the Greeks of the Punjab, made the kingdom. A displaced people with nothing behind them took walled cities from dynasties that had held them for a century; Maues took Taxila and Gandhara, the house of Azes took the rest, west and east of the Jhelum, until the last Greek kings vanish from their own coinage. Even this war of conquest was half a family matter by the time it ended. Maues had married into a Greek royal line, the engravers who cut his coins were Greeks, and the Greek population of Taxila went on living under a Scythian landlord, dating documents by Macedonian months.

Against Parthia the fighting was mostly over titles and border provinces. The Saka claim to King of Kings was a raid on Parthian prestige in the exact decade Parthia could not answer it, and the two Iranian powers passed Arachosia and the Kabul valley back and forth for a century. Against the Kushans, a hundred and fifty years later, there was not much of a fight at all. The Yuezhi arrived with the same momentum that had once carried the Sakas themselves through the passes, absorbed the northwest, and kept the Saka satraps in office. The displacement that had begun on the Chinese frontier ran itself out here, at the Indus.

The great feud was with the Satavahanas. Nahapana pushed the Saka border south into the Deccan; Gautamiputra Satakarni annihilated him and restruck his treasury, and his court remembered the deed in a queen’s inscription at Nasik with a phrase that still draws blood: he was the one

> who destroyed the Shakas, Yavanas and Pahlavas... who rooted out the
> Khakharata race and restored the glory of the Satavahana family.

Two generations later the pendulum swung back. Rudradaman, the great king at Ujjain, twice defeated the lord of the Deccan in open war, and then did a thing that tells you the era of extermination was over. In the words of his own inscription, he

> twice in fair fight completely defeated Satakarni, the lord of the
> Dakshinapatha, but on account of the nearness of their connection did
> not destroy him.

The nearness was marriage; a Saka princess of his house had been wed into the Satavahana line, and a cave inscription near Bombay survives in which a Satavahana queen names herself daughter of the great satrap. The same Rudradaman took back Sindhu Sauvira on the lower Indus from Kanishka’s Kushan successors, and Sircar counted six countries in his lists reconquered from the Satavahanas.

The republics fought hardest. The Yaudheyas, a warrior brotherhood of the Punjab plains who minted coins in the name of their war god and no king, resisted Rudradaman hard enough that his panegyrist could not resist naming them: the Yaudheyas, proud of their title of heroes among all kshatriyas, forcibly rooted out. The Malavas of Rajasthan rose against Saka officials and raised sacrificial pillars to celebrate what one of them calls the liberation of his country; the pillars still stand.

There were also usurpers and overlords. The Abhira herdsmen clans, once Saka auxiliaries, twice put their own men on the western throne, and Sassanid Persia, in the dynasty’s old age, briefly reduced it to a protectorate under a Persian prince titled Sakanshah, king of the Sakas.

The Guptas ended it. For the new imperial power of the Ganges, the conquest of the last great satrap around the year 400 was the closing act of a unification, and Gupta court tradition remembered Chandragupta II for centuries by the deed: the king who slew the Saka.

One kind of war is missing from that list. In five hundred years, no source I have found records the Sakas burning a temple, suppressing a sect, or fighting a battle over a god. Their wars were the ordinary wars of Indian statecraft, over ports and provinces, against neighbours who kept turning into relatives. The endowments to brahmanas and monks ran on through all of it. Even their enemies’ inscriptions, the ones celebrating Saka defeats, never accuse them of anything worse than being on the wrong side.

### **The marriages**



Conquerors marry into the conquered country. The Sakas started before the conquest was even finished; Maues took a wife from an Indo Greek royal house so his claim to the Punjab would wear a family face. Nahapana gave his daughter Dakshamitra to the officer Ushavadata, whose Nasik inscriptions ended up making the son in law more famous than the king. Rudradaman went furthest of all and married a daughter into the Satavahana line itself, the old enemy; that one alliance turned the Deccan war into a family argument and saved a beaten king his throne. By the second century, marriage to a Saka great satrap’s daughter had evidently become something an Indian dynasty could boast about, because the Satavahana queen’s cave inscription at Kanheri announces her descent from the great satrap with pride. And two queens of Ujjain went south, which carries the story two thousand kilometres from the Khyber, to a bend of the Krishna river. There, in the third century, ruled the Ikshvakus, a devoutly Hindu dynasty whose kings performed the horse sacrifice, the old Vedic ritual of paramount kingship, while their queens built Buddhist monasteries. The Sakas of Ujjain married into this court twice. King Virapurushadatta took as a queen Rudradhara bhattarika, whom his inscriptions call daughter of the king of Ujjain; the name Rudradhara sits unmistakably among the Rudrasenas and Rudrasimhas of the western Saka line. His son Ehuvala married Vammabhatta, daughter of a great satrap of Ujjain. And her son, in turn, reigned as Rudrapurushadatta, a name that fuses the dynasty’s southern god with the Saka royal house’s favourite element, as if the marriage had been written into his birth certificate. The last kings of the Ikshvaku line carried Saka blood.

They carried something subtler too. In exactly the generations of the Ujjain marriages, the Ikshvaku inscriptions begin dignifying their kings with a new title: svamin, lord. It is the signature style of the Western Kshatrapas, the word Nahapana and Rudrasena wore after their names, and at the Krishna river court it arrives with the brides and then spreads backwards, a memorial inscription of the last reign politely awarding svamin to all the dynasty’s ancestors, who had never used it. A title had travelled the length of India in a wedding party, and the southern house liked it so much it rewrote its own past to have always possessed it. The most intimate conquest in this essay was carried out entirely by queens.

### **The barefoot Scythian**



The place to see what the marriages meant is the place where the queens went: Vijayapuri, the Ikshvaku capital, whose ruins on the Krishna are known today as Nagarjunakonda. Before its valley was flooded by a modern dam, the Archaeological Survey of India lifted an entire buried city out of the ground there: palaces, bathing ghats, more than thirty Buddhist monasteries, and, of all things, an amphitheatre, a tiered stadium sunk around an arena, a building type with no other example in ancient India. Roman gold poured into the city through the coastal trade, and something of Rome’s idea of an afternoon evidently came ashore with it. Among the carvings, the excavators kept meeting a figure who had no business being in the tropics.

He stands on an ornate pillar from the royal palace, and A. H. Longhurst, who dug the site in the 1920s, described him exactly: a bearded soldier, apparently a Scythian, wearing a Roman like helmet, a quilted long sleeved tunic and trousers, and holding a heavy spear. The figure, he added, is quite un Indian. Everything on the man is winter kit from the steppe, the quilted coat, the tight trousers, the knobbed helmet of central Asia, carved by a southern sculptor in a country where nobody has ever needed a quilted coat. Trousers themselves, it is worth remembering, had entered India with the Sakas, riders’ clothing for a people who lived on horseback. His kind appear elsewhere in the reliefs, riding in battle panels, standing guard with that ornamented spear, and the study of the site’s costumes sorts its soldiers frankly into the local and the foreign by dress alone. A community of Sakas, guards, officers, grandees of the queens’ households, lived at Vijayapuri, and the sculptors knew exactly what they looked like. They knew his drinking companions too. The pillar that pairs with the soldier’s carries a plump, nearly nude figure clutching a drinking horn and a wine jar, whom Longhurst could only identify as a crude Dionysus, the Greek wine god, washed up on the Krishna with the Roman wine trade. A Scythian with a spear on one pillar, Dionysus with a wineskin on the next, in the palace of a Hindu king who endowed Buddhist nuns. Vijayapuri was that kind of town.

One detail on the palace pillar gives the whole game away, and it was the art historian K. Krishna Murthy who noticed it. At Sanchi and Mathura, in the north, Scythians are carved wearing their laced riding boots, as Scythians had dressed for six hundred years. The Vijayapuri soldier wears cap, coat, trousers, everything, and stands barefoot. Nobody wore shoes at Nagarjunakonda; it was not the custom of the country. The sculptor dressed him for the grasslands from the head down and then, at the ankles, let the south win. Murthy’s conclusion is the sober version: he was deliberately shown barefooted to be in tune with the prevailing custom of the Ikshvaku period. The unsober version is better. The man is mid dissolve. Two more generations and only the beard will be left; two more after that, nothing.

The small finds carry the same story into private life. Among the inscriptions Vogel edited from the site is a worn line of Prakrit along a slab carved with the Buddha’s footprints, recording, as he read it, the pious gift of Budhi, sister of Moda, the Saka. Vogel flagged his reading as an interpretation, and it should be repeated with his caution attached. But if he read it right, then here, on the far side of the subcontinent from the Khyber, an otherwise unknown Saka woman, no queen, no satrap’s daughter, spent her own money on the most ordinary act of Buddhist devotion there is, and bought herself seventeen centuries of remembrance. It was very likely this same modest slab that the epigraphist D. C. Sircar had in mind when he wrote that the site “records the pious gift of a Saka girl, which fact possibly shows that the Iksvakus were friendly towards the Sakas.” Even the city’s bookkeeping points the same way; gifts at Vijayapuri are valued in dinari masakas, a gold denarius unit that, in Sircar’s judgment, reached the south through the country of the Sakas. Nobody at Vijayapuri was writing polemics about mlecchas. They were logging donations.

The last dated carving of the whole city is the fitting one. In the eleventh year of the last king, Rudrapurushadatta, a memorial pillar was raised for his dead mother, Vammabhatta, the great satrap of Ujjain’s daughter, the Saka queen. A lady is sculptured at the top of the pillar, being attended by her women; the excavators believe she is the queen herself. The sculptural record of Vijayapuri, one of the great Buddhist cities of the ancient world, closes on the portrait of a Scythian’s daughter, commissioned by her half Saka son, in the deepest south of India

### **The rock at Junagadh**



In the province the Sakas held longest there stands a granite boulder that functions as a résumé of Indian kingship. At Junagadh in Gujarat, the emperor Ashoka had his edicts cut into the stone in the third century BCE. Seven hundred years later a Gupta emperor added his own record to the same rock. Between them, sharing the surface, stands the inscription of Rudradaman, grandson of Chastana, great satrap of Ujjain, and it earns its place in this story twice over.

The occasion was an engineering disaster. The Sudarshana reservoir, a great irrigation lake first built under Chandragupta Maurya and improved under Ashoka, burst its dam in a storm in the year 72 of the Saka era, 150 CE, leaving the farmers of Kathiawar staring at bare ground where the water had been. Rudradaman’s governor rebuilt the dam broader and stronger than before, and the king’s record is at pains to specify the financing: from his own treasury, without oppressing the people with taxes, forced labour or benevolences. The governor who carried out the work, the inscription notes, was Suvishakha, a Pahlava, a Parthian, administering Gujarat for a Saka king, repairing a Mauryan emperor’s lake. The inscription pauses to praise the king’s accomplishments in grammar, music, logic and the composition of prose and verse, and closes on his sworn vow to refrain from killing men except in battle.

The second claim on history is the language all this is written in. Every long royal inscription in India before it, Ashoka’s included, had spoken Prakrit, the common tongue. Rudradaman’s is composed in high literary Sanskrit, elaborate compounds, courtly cadences, the works, and it is the first long royal inscription in that language anywhere in India. Sanskrit had been the preserve of priests and poets; the fashion of kings proclaiming themselves in it, which would define Indian statecraft for a thousand years and export itself across Southeast Asia, begins, as far as surviving stone can show, with a Saka. Manu’s law code ranked men like him as fallen kshatriyas. This particular fallen kshatriya had just written the founding document of India’s imperial language.

His dynasty’s habit of dating things outlived everything else about it. The era its coins and inscriptions counted, year one in 78 CE, spread with the dynasty’s prestige, was picked up by astronomers as the standard reckoning for their tables, crossed the sea with Indian learning, and dated inscriptions from Cambodia to Java. The historian E. J. Rapson noted the irony with satisfaction: it was the long use of this count by the Saka princes of western India that fixed its everyday name, the Saka era, a name, he adds, which effectually disguises its origin. In 1957 the Republic of India, needing a national calendar clear of regional quarrels, adopted it. The official date on the Gazette of India, printed beside the Western one, counts the years of the Sakas. One caution, because the point is often muddled: this is the era of 78 CE, and the older Vikrama era of 58 BCE is a separate count with its own disputed story. It is the 78 count, the dynasty of Chastana’s own, that the modern state keeps.

### **What the texts remember**



All the while the Sakas were building, marrying and endowing, India’s writers were arguing about what these people were. The argument ran for centuries, and I know of no other ancient civilisation that ran one quite like it.

The register of alarm came first, and it was reserved, oddly, mostly for the Greeks. The Yavanas had invaded a century before the Sakas, striking deep into the Ganges heartland, and the one Hindu text that describes those campaigns in detail, the Yuga Purana, renders them as the end of the world written in the future tense, the way you write down a nightmare while you are still inside it. The Yavanas will reach Pataliputra, the capital. The social order will invert. And then the prophecy reaches its eeriest pitch:

> the earth will be desolate, terrible, and predominating in women.
> Women will do the ploughing, with hands the texture of the plough, and
> they will be warriors with bows in the fields, due to the scarcity of
> men.

The panic even got into literature. When Patanjali needed an everyday sentence to illustrate a past tense, he used the following example:

> aruṇad yavanaḥ sāketam. The Yavana was besieging Saketa.

For the first generation or two, that terror was easy enough to justify. The Sakas simply walked into a vocabulary the Greeks had earned, and the Kali age prophecy of the Mahabharata duly lists them among the wrong kings of the dark age; that is the verse standing at the head of this essay, and nothing about it is friendly.

The prophecy carries smaller insults too. Shudras, it says, will be addressed as arya, noble sir. Brahmanas will get bho, the curt syllable meant for servants. Two pronouns trade places, and the whole social order goes over with them.

The tradition even schedules the remedy: Kalki, Vishnu on the white horse, due at the blackest hour to sweep the mleccha kings from their thrones. But before leaving the verse, look at who stands beside the Sakas in it. Andhras and Pulindas are peoples of India, same line, same accusation. Foreigners are not really the subject. Thrones in the wrong hands are.

Manu’s code, the most influential social rulebook in Indian tradition, classifies the Sakas, Yavanas, Pahlavas and their kind not as demons or untouchables but as kshatriyas, warriors, who have slid to the bottom of society because they lost their rites and their priests. Patrick Olivelle draws out the implication: Manu is looking at people with real armies and real thrones and finding a category that keeps them inside the system, disreputable but demoted rather than alien. The oldest law manuals had already made the same move more bluntly, deriving the Yavana from a kshatriya father and a shudra mother, a mixed birth, half inside by definition. The epic itself states the doctrine most plainly, in the long teaching the dying Bhishma delivers from his bed of arrows. Here is Bibek Debroy’s rendering:

> It is because there are no brahmanas among them that Shakas, Yavanas,
> Kambojas and other similar kshatriya tribes are regarded as the equals
> of vrishalas.

A vrishala is a low man, a nobody. Read the verse again, though, and see what it concedes in the act of condemning: kshatriya tribes. Bhishma does not say the Sakas were never warriors. He says they fell, and he names the cause, no brahmanas among them, which is a curable condition. The next verse says the very same of the Dramilas, the Tamils.

There is a Pali version of this line drawing too, and to me it is the most revealing of the lot. Buddhaghosa, commenting on the monastic code in the fifth century, needed a working definition of barbarian speech for a point of law, and gave one: ariyaka, Aryan speech, means the Magadha language; milakkhaka is “whatever is non Aryan, such as Andhra, Tamil and so on.” Andhra and Tamil. The Buddhist commentator’s barbarians speak Indian languages, and a Saka with decent Prakrit walks past the definition untouched. Ritual and speech made the line on this subcontinent. Blood never did. And a line made of ritual and speech is a line a foreigner can cross.

The myths agree with the lawyers in their own crooked way. Vasishtha’s wish granting cow, in the Mahabharata’s first book, raises armies out of her own body to protect the sage, Pahlavas from her tail, Sakas from her dung, Yavanas from her urine. An insult, obviously; epic genealogy is rarely polite about anybody. Also a birth record, since it creates the foreign tribes inside the sacred order, on the sage’s side of the fight. King Sagara’s defeated Sakas in the Puranas get half shaved heads and a bar from Vedic study. Marked men, and kept.

One more fact belongs in this ledger, and I have argued it at full length elsewhere. The Sakas were Iranians, and Iranians and Indians started out as one people on the steppe, Avesta on one branch, Rigveda on the other, the Scythians a third branch off the same trunk. The Persians called one Saka tribe the haoma pressers; haoma is soma, the same sacrament under a sound shift, and the Veda grows its best soma on Mujavant, toward the Hindu Kush, Saka country. The Scythians’ own chief goddess, Tabiti of the hearth flame, is Tapati, the Burning One, kin to tapas. Wherever Mujavant actually stood, the lawyers who filed the Saka as fallen kin rather than as monster had it right. He was kin.

### **Converts, patrons, authors**



The converts did not wait for the theory. Two generations before Maues took Taxila, a Greek ambassador named Heliodoros put up a Garuda pillar at Besnagar and called himself, on the stone, a Bhagavata, a devotee of Vasudeva Krishna. The lawyers were still debating whether a fallen kshatriya could climb back; the column already stood.

The Buddhists, who had no varna ladder, no fourfold ranking of society to place anyone on, told a simpler story: the foreigner as convert, patron, and finally author.

Their monument to the Greeks is the Milindapanha, the Questions of Milinda, in which the historical king Menander, remembered as a debater whom no one in India could withstand, interrogates the monk Nagasena through hundreds of pages of philosophy and ends as a disciple; India remembers its Greek invader as a Buddhist sage. For the Sakas no such literary portrait was needed, because the ground itself was covered with their receipts, from Patika’s copper plate at Taxila to the reliquaries of Bajaur to Budhi’s footprint slab on the Krishna. The end of the arc lies far outside India. Up the Silk Road at Khotan, a Saka people kept their Iranian tongue for another thousand years, and Buddhist literature came to be written in Saka itself. One long Khotanese poem, the Jatakastava, has a court poet singing the praises of the Buddha’s former births in the speech of the horsemen. Scripture had cast these people as the omen of dharma’s death. At Khotan they ended up writing dharma’s poetry, and writing it in Saka.

### **The solvent**



Set the two ends of the story side by side one last time. At one end, the epic’s prophecy: when Shakas become kings, the age is dying. At the other end, the Gazette of India, dating the republic’s business, every day, in the era of the Sakas.

In between, this essay has walked past a copper plate at Taxila where a Saka officer buries the Buddha’s relics in a Greek month. A coin where the elephants of Lakshmi bathe a steppe king’s fortune. A trident that entered India in Poseidon’s hand and left it in Shiva’s. A customs house at Barygaza collecting Roman silver for a Saka king, and a Nasik cave where a Saka son in law feeds brahmanas and monks with the same purse. A rock at Junagadh where a Saka writes the first long royal Sanskrit inscription in the language’s history. Wedding parties carrying the title svamin down the length of the subcontinent. And a palace pillar on the Krishna where the last Scythian in the story stands in his quilted coat and his bare feet, halfway to being nobody in particular, which is to say, halfway to being Indian.

Other civilisations met the steppe and reached for stone. Rome answered it with a walled Danube, China with the long walls of the Ordos. Greece met the Scythian in the flesh and invented the barbarian, a permanent outsider to measure Greekness against. India built no wall against these particular strangers and coined no such word for them. It filed them as fallen kin instead, a door left on the latch, and then did what it has done to every conqueror before and since. It absorbed them so completely that they cannot be found today, not as a community, not even as a name on any living lip. Chattopadhyaya’s verdict needs only its emphasis shifted: India captured her captors, and the captives never noticed the moment the cell became the house.

Out on the Baluchistan rim, where this essay leaves them, the desert kings named for Mithra were naming their kings Arjuna and Bhimarjuna, after heroes of the epic that had once listed their people among the signs of the end of the world. The apocalypse, given four generations and a few good marriages, had started naming its children out of the Mahabharata. The end of days never came. The in laws did

  American Oriental Society 116.3, 1996), with **Stefan Baums’s** corpus
  of Gandharan reliquary inscriptions and **Jason Neelis, Early Buddhist
  Transmission and Trade Networks** (Brill): the Apraca dedications,
  including Rukhana’s triple dated reliquary, Indravarma’s re burials,
  the Kharahostes cup, and the line “All beings are brought to nirvana.”

- **Philostratus, Life of Apollonius of Tyana**: Taxila the size of
  Nineveh, walled like a Greek city.

- **Wilfred Schoff (trans.), The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea**
  (1912): Minnagara “subject to Parthian princes” (section 38) and the
  kingdom of Nambanus with Barygaza and Ozene (sections 41 to 48).

- **A. H. Longhurst, The Buddhist Antiquities of Nagarjunakonda**
  (Memoirs of the Archaeological Survey of India 54): the bearded
  Scythian soldier and the Dionysus pillar, with the plates reproduced
  here.

- **K. Krishna Murthy, Nagarjunakonda: A Cultural Study**: the barefoot
  detail and its explanation, the costume and helmet analysis, and the
  Ikshvaku adoption of the title svamin.

- **J. Ph. Vogel, Epigraphia Indica XX**: the Nagarjunakonda ayaka
  pillar inscriptions and the footprint slab of Budhi, sister of Moda
  the Saka, with Vogel’s own caution about the reading. **H. Sarkar
  and B. N. Misra, Nagarjunakonda** (ASI guide): the memorial pillar of
  queen Vammabhatta.

- **Buddhaghosa, Samantapasadika** (commentary on the Vinaya, Parajika
  I; Pali Text Society ed., vol. I p. 255): the definition of ariyaka as
  Magadha speech and milakkhaka as “whatever is non Aryan, such as
  Andhra, Tamil and so on.”

- **T. W. Rhys Davids (trans.), The Questions of King Milinda**:
  Menander and Nagasena.

- **Mark Dresden (ed. and trans.), The Jatakastava** (Transactions of
  the American Philosophical Society 45.5): Buddhist poetry in the
  Khotanese Saka language.

- **Pankaj Tandon, “Paratarajas,”** in Ancient Indian Coins: A
  Comprehensive Catalogue: the king list, the name etymologies, the
  swastika as Mithra’s wheel, Yolamira’s vihara on the Loralai
  potsherds, and the coin reproduced here.

- **Shapur I’s inscription at the Ka’ba ye Zartosht** (262 CE): Paradan
  among the provinces of the Sassanid empire.

- **Ptolemy, Geography**: Ozene, the royal city of Tiastenes.

- **Edith Hall, Inventing the Barbarian** (Oxford): the Greek
  construction of the absolute outsider.

- On soma and haoma, and Tabiti as Tapati: my earlier essays on the
  Scythians, where the argument is made in full.
