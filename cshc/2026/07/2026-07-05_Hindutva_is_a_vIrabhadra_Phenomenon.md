***What avails worshipping the gods in the sacrifice if[ Rudra](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/rudra#purana) is not worshipped by you?***

[Dadhīca asks Dakṣa](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/shiva-purana-english/d/doc226655.html) (when he learned that Śiva was not invited to his yajña)

We have been, since time immemorial, a land of storytellers. The stories of gods, seers, kings, animals and whatnot have been the most preferred medium for the transmission of knowledge in Indian traditions. We, as a culture, have always loved stories so much that the revered Satyanārāyaṇa Kathā/narrative (Revā Khaṅḍa, Skanda Purāṇa) comprises multiple stories that are eulogies of the meta-story, i.e., the Satyanārāyaṇa Kathā, which is not even narrated. Dr J. P. Singh points out, in his work *Nāradīya Sañcāra Nīti*, that it is a story whose components praise a story never told. The Purāṇas are one such corpus of Indian literature that has woven stories, rituals, peoples, geography, gods, flora and fauna, and transcendent realms into a single fabric of narratives and praxes.

The opening words are part of a Paurāṇika ākhyāna (narrative) – the Dakṣa Yajña Narrative – which defines many aspects of the lived cultural world of a billion Hindus. The sacred geography of Śaktipīṭha-s, festivals, iconography, performative traditions, and metaphysical implications, such as the continuation of Pravrtti Dharma into Nivrtti Dharma(1), are a few among many. It is a complex narrative to deal with. I may not be an adhikārī, but I will attempt to situate a cultural phenomenon that defines the current era of Indian politics within this ākhyāṇa for reasons discussed later.

### ***Hindutva***



Hindutva is a movement marked by the assertion of Indian cultural symbols, concepts, narratives, and interests in policy formulation and public discourse. It is informed by an idea of Bharat grounded in civilisational continuity rather than a purely statist conception. Hindutva politics involves aggressive rhetoric against the culturally alienating “secular” politics of India, which has been a dominant theme in Indian politics since Nehruvian times. However, it is noteworthy that despite the Hindutva political uprising, the vacuous construct of secularism(2) is part of the basic structure of the Indian Constitution (S. R. Bommai judgement, 1994). That is something to reflect upon.

**This essay is not a sad commentary on the stepmotherly treatment of the kaleidoscopic world of Hindu traditions by the Indian state.** The suggestive power of Paurāṇika narratives and allied praxes reverberates with the masses. It makes abstract knowledge and complex phenomena easier to grasp. **I try to position the Hindu resurgence, also known as Hindutva, by reading it through the Paurāṇika narrative of Vīrabhadra.**

### **What are Purāṇa-s?**



Purāṇa-s, literally, mean “stories of the past” and are a corpus of 18 major (and countless minor) texts. “One should augment and amplify the Veda with the help of the Itihasas and the Puranas,” says the Ādi-Parva of the Mahabharata (1.1.204). It can be called an education programme for the masses in which complex and abstract Vedic knowledge is transmitted through stories and narratives. According to tradition’s own classical self-definition, attested in the Amarakośa and the Purāṇas themselves, the Purāṇa-s are defined by their **Pañcalakṣaṇa**: five core themes covering the original creation of the universe, its cyclical dissolution and recreation, the genealogies of gods and sages, vast cosmic epochs (*Manvantaras*), and the histories of ancient royal dynasties. Written in accessible Sanskrit, they serve as expansive repositories of narratives, sacred geography, pilgrimage guides, and worldly knowledge. The meaning I derive from them, for this essay, is informed by this profound observation of Ananda K. Coomaraswamy (*Hinduism and Buddhism*):

> *Myth, the penultimate truth, of which all experience is the temporal
> reflection.*

Although the term ‘myth’ colloquially means an ‘untrue story’, we can say that Coomaraswamy meant the Itihāsa-Purāṇa tradition. He elaborates, “Myths are not distorted records of historical events. They are not periphrastic descriptions of natural phenomena, of ‘explanations’ of them; so far from that, events are demonstrations of the myths.” Without getting much into the semantics of it, let us jump into the “myth” that will demonstrate the phenomenon at hand, i.e., Hindutva.

### **The Origins of Vīrabhadra**



Śiva is one of the most beloved of the Hindu deities. He is also known as Bhole Bābā, an innocent elderly member of the family. He can be gratified very easily. Many *asura*-s did penance for evil purposes and got empowered by his grace. It had led to many problems in the sacred past. Bhasmāsura, empowered by Śiva’s grace, almost turned him to ashes (it was all his līla, though). This has been a common theme of many Paurāṇika narratives. What could he do about it? After all, he is Āśutoṣa, the easily pleased one.

Naive people tend to trust others blindly, out of ignorance of the ways of the world. But he is not naive. He loves his creation. Correction: He is the creation. He is love. There may come a Bhasmāsura, or two, but who cares? However, there have been times when his popular image of the ever-meditative yogi on the Kailāśa mountain has been shattered. One such episode is the myth we are dealing with here. I am not referring to *Tāṅḍava* or the destructive aspect of Śiva when samsāra dissolves.

If there is one Paurāṇika narrative which almost every Hindu must have heard, it is the myth of the dismemberment of Sati’s body. 51 Śaktipīṭha-s span the territory of the greater Indian region from Hiṅglāja in Baluchistan to Tripureśwarī in Tripura. The list may differ from 51 to 108 in different sources, but the story more or less remains the same.

*Dakṣa, a powerful Prajāpatī (creator deity) and the father of Sati, organised a grand Yajña (Vedic sacrifice). Daksha deeply despised his son-in-law, Śiva, viewing him as an uncivilised ascetic who frequented cremation grounds. Consequently, he deliberately excluded both Sati and Śiva from the invitation list.*

*Despite Śiva’s advice to stay away, Sati attended the Yajña uninvited, hoping to reconcile with her father. Instead, Dakṣa publicly humiliated her and mocked Śiva in front of all the assembled gods and sages. Unable to bear the insult to her husband and her own cosmic dignity, Sati severed her ties with her father and self-immolated.*

*When news of Sati’s death reached Śiva, his grief turned into catastrophic rage. He tore a lock of his matted hair and dashed it to the ground, creating the fierce warrior **Vīrabhadra** (and in some versions, the terrifying goddess Bhadrakāli). Vīrabhadra stormed the sacrificial arena, routed the assembled gods, destroyed the Yajña, and decapitated Dakṣa.* **(There is more to the story. We will come to it in the conclusion.)**

The story doesn’t end with iconoclasm but with creative destruction. I am aware that I am attempting a very adhibhautika interpretation.(3) Creative destruction seems indispensable if the form of institutionalisation is unjust or fails to serve its intended purpose. As I write this, I cannot help but picture the civilisational giants who toppled the dome of the Babri structure. It was a historic moment of creative destruction. Countless others have strived and sacrificed for the creative destruction represented by Hindutva. Throughout the history of this movement, some worked in silence, while others roared like lions. There have been no conspiracies, contrary to what some scholars argue. A hair lock of Bhagwān Śiva took the form of Vīrabhadra when the systemic injustice became unbearable.

“What is our mother-country?” Asking thus, Sri Aurobindo in *Bhawani Mandir* answers in his usual prophetic flair, “It is not a piece of earth, nor a figure of speech, nor a fiction of the mind. It is a mighty Shakti, composed of the Shaktis of all the millions of units that make up the nation.” Likewise, Hindutva is composed of all the hundreds of millions of Hindus who have had enough. They are Vīrabhadra personified, still calm and meditative at their source. The assertive and vigorous Hindutva is an organic unitary manifestation of pluralistic Indian traditions to deal with certain unprovoked onslaughts and forms of colonialism that have murdered millions, desecrated thousands of sacred spaces, extensively looted, and led to many cultural genocides in India.

### **The Yajña Metaphor and the Indian State**



The flaw in Dakṣa’s yajña isn’t that he excluded a minority. It’s that he excluded the deity whose presence the sacrifice structurally required, and the cosmos rejected the sacrifice as a result. Yajña is a ritualistic mechanism which is institutionalised to reap certain benefits. Those ends are pre-decided by the doers of the Yajña. In the same way, the Indian state was institutionalised with ends in sight. The constituent assembly debates comprised the discussions on those ends.

While those ends were being discussed, the killing of Gandhi by a Hindu nationalist was a big blow to the cultural resurgence of Bharat. The unabashed Hindu political elite, such as Sardar Patel and Purushottam Das Tandon, became defensive about native cultural organisations, narratives and their epistemic institutionalisation. The fact that an interpretation of Islam by a few Muslim political elites, represented by the Muslim League, led to the partition of India was a civilisational lesson of the millennium. The consequent exodus and killings of many millions of people were forgotten overnight by the English-educated political elite of India. Hence, the post-independence Indian state failed to create any institutions, grounded in the Indian cultural world, to check such separatist tendencies or maintain communal harmony.

I know this is a big fish to catch. I cannot justify all these arguments in this essay. What I want to point out is that the varied cultural world of Hindu traditions, which made traditional plurality of all sorts possible, was marked as the evil “majority” by the Indian state. On the other hand, monotheistic and imperialist faiths like Islam and Christianity were given special rights for being the so-called “minority”.

The benevolent Śiva was denied his rightful share in the sacrifice. Likewise, pluralist Hindus were denied their basic communal rights. The Indian state categorically patronised faiths that have historically persecuted minorities all over the world. The framework of minority-majority was imposed on a culture where distinction based on the deity you worship is not even a debate. (4) Amidst all this institutional lethargy and confusion, there is no systemic solution in sight within the secular paradigm. It has always been a colossal normative failure in India, marked by theoretical contradictions and communal favouritism.

### **Understanding Hindutva as a Vīrabhadra Phenomenon**



> *One of the grossest misconceptions about the Hindu movement, is that
> it is an artificial creation of political parties like the BJP and the
> Shiv Sena. In reality, there is a substratum of Hindu activist
> tendencies in many corners of Hindu society, often in unorganized form
> and mostly lacking in intellectual articulation.*

Koenraad Elst, in *Decolonizing the Hindu mind: Ideological development of Hindu Revivalism*, made these remarks to highlight the organic growth of the Hindutva movement since the freedom struggle. Hindutva became an expression against the legitimisation of rampant Hinduphobia in all walks of life by the modern Indian state apparatus. Its roots go much deeper. In another work of his, he writes: “Gandhi was one such bad parent who rewarded the ill-behaved and punished the well-behaved. He was harsh on the polemical but non-violent Swami Shraddhananda, and kind to the Swami’s murderer, about whom he stated in public: ‘Abdul Rashid is my brother.’” This culturally suicidal legacy was carried forward by the Republic of India’s first Prime Minister, Jawaharlal Nehru, as elucidated by Sitaram Goel in his work *Nehruvian Secularism*.

The impact of this anti-Hindu politico-cultural rhetoric was so deeply entrenched in Congress that it made V. D. Savarkar remark (in a speech on July 10, 1943),

> *The day Hindus get united, the Congress leaders will wear janeu over
> the coat.*

How prophetic have been his words! Popular politics has recently taken a serious turn toward caste rhetoric and freebie politics, but soft-Hindutva posturing has become a common theme in Indian politics today. The post-2014 consolidation of the Hindu vote bank has forced many political parties to move away from unabashed Muslim appeasement. Barring any value judgements, this has been a monumental change in Indian politics. Some scholars explain this change in conspiratorial terms.

For instance, Christophe Jaffrelot traces the origins of Hindutva to a “perceived threat” from the “Other”, initially Christian missionaries and British colonisers, and later, Muslims. He calls this mechanism **“stigmatisation and emulation”.** As if that stigmatisation had no theological and historical grounding in the fundamentals of the “other”. It is noteworthy that such scholars may call the emulation “Semitisation” of Hindu traditions but never criticise Semitic faiths for their violent and imperialistic fundamentals.

The problem of communal discord lies in the fundamentals of Semiticism, not in the Hindu resistance to it. What Jan Assman termed ‘mosaic distinction’. Nothing changes until the fundamentals of Semitic faiths change. So, the Babri demolition was not a hateful and vengeful act of a faceless mob which was ignited to spread anarchy. It was a renaissance of sorts. **Hammers of boundless love shattered the dome of unprovoked hatred.** It was a reawakening of the diverse Hindu peoples to reclaim what rightfully belonged to their gods. It was an act of creative destruction, and consequently Rāma Lalā resides there in his glorious temple. Hindus are reclaiming the spaces, narratives, epistemes, symbols and languages that were suppressed under the secular debris.

Former diplomat Pavan Varma, in his work *Becoming Indian,* beautifully narrates the cultural costs of not going through a ‘swaraj in ideas’, as enunciated by K. C. Bhattacharya. A swaraj in ideas, which is not possible without letting the flowers of Indian traditions bloom. The organic expression of Indian cultural genius was repressed for so long that it had to burst. The repressive structure had to collapse to be rebuilt. Creative destruction is necessary. There is no alternative.

## ***Hindutva is a Vīrabhadra phenomenon!***



Vīrabhadra is latent in Śiva’s hair until the insult draws him out. The Hindutva movement is latent in Hindu society until provocation articulates it. To quote the viral definition, “Hindutva is Hinduism that resists.” And it has historically resisted wherever injustices wreaked havoc on the Indian populace. How can one otherwise make sense of the rise of the Vijayanagara Empire or Chhatrapati Shivaji’s Maratha Kingdom? On top of that, even in post-Independence India, we have examples of Hindu resistance from people and organisations one could not imagine.

Romila Thapar, in her recently published memoir, writes: “Hindutva is not a religion and is not Hinduism. It was founded by Brahmanas belonging to the organisation called the RSS—Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh—in the early 1920s.” Saying that *Hindutva … is not Hinduism* is like saying Vīrabhadra has nothing to do with Śiva. It may be difficult for ideologues to understand, but complex entities/phenomena can, depending on context, contain multitudes.

Hindutva, an organic expression of Hindu culture, does not flourish because of Hindu organisations. Rather, many organisations grow because they espouse Hindutva. There are countless examples outside the Sangh Parivar to substantiate this claim. I will cite the one with the most fascinating coincidence.

Interestingly enough, there was a Hindutvaite Congress stalwart in Himachal Pradesh. Virbhadra Singh (1934–2021) was a prominent Congress politician, a five-time Member of Parliament, and the longest-serving Chief Minister of Himachal Pradesh with a 21-year tenure. Under his chief ministership, the state government in 2006 [banned forced conversions](https://www.etvbharat.com/hindi/himachal-pradesh/city/shimla/bill-against-conversion-was-first-introduced-in-himachal-pradesh-by-virbhadra-singh/hp20210708193536056) to check the Christian evangelical erasure of the native Hindu culture. In 2014, the Honourable High Court of Himachal Pradesh ordered a ban on animal sacrifice at Kullu Dussehra. Virabhadra Singh, the then chief minister, criticised the judgment, saying that the courts should first ban the animal slaughter on Eid, and then we may discuss our traditions.

I once visited the beautiful Parashar temple in Mandi, Himachal Pradesh. The pujārī of the temple narrated an anecdote. The state government decided to take management of another temple in Mandi under its control. The Pujārī-s went to ‘Rājājī’ (this is how he referred to him), meaning ‘king’, Virabhadra Singh, to complain about the same. He immediately directed his bureaucratic subordinates not to go ahead with the decision. Moreover, since day one, he had been speaking in favour of the reclamation of the Ramjanmabhoomi site in Ayodhya, deviating from his party’s official anti-Hindu stance. He famously [asked](https://www.livehindustan.com/national/story-virbhadra-singh-favour-having-ram-temple-at-same-spot-in-ayodhya-2482381.html) whether a temple dedicated to Lord Ram could not be built in Ayodhya, where else in the world could it be built?

He lived and died a Congress stalwart. He walked the Hindutva path without any organisation’s mediation. He was a Hindutvaite at the expense of earning the ire of his party’s supremo. He could not care less. He was a confident Hindu. Every scholar who explains away the Hindutva movement with a few organisations and people within conspiratorial terms not only fools his/her readers but also strengthens the very organisations they seem to oppose. The Indian masses flung themselves towards the organisations/leaders that are branded as Hindutvaites. People support Hindutva because it is the most normal thing for any aware and culturally rooted Indian to do.

### **Where Do We Go From Here?**



The erstwhile chief minister of Gujarat (and current Prime Minister) Narendra Modi, in a historic speech in 2003 at the first Pravasi Bharatiya Diwas event, stated:

> *Hindutva is a culture of thousands of years. The BJP may win or lose,
> but Hindutva can never be defeated.*

Hindutva is not a recent phenomenon. However, it became more prominent with the arrival of the aggressive unassimilative cult of Islam. It was there in the humiliation heaped on the treacherous Deccan Sultans by the de facto ruler of Vijayanagara, Rama Raya. It was witnessed when Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj demolished mosques built over the Shonachalapati (Shiva) and Samottir Perumal (Vishnu) temples in Tiruvannamalai, Tamil Nadu. Maharaja Ranjit Singh of Lahore demanded three strict conditions from Shah Shuja, the deposed Durrani Afghan monarch, for military aid:

- **Cow Slaughter Ban**: Complete prohibition of cow slaughter across
  Afghanistan.

- **Somnath Gates**: Return of the historic temple gates plundered by
  Mahmud of Ghazni.

- **Hostage Exchange**: Sending the Afghan heir to live at the Lahore
  court as a guarantee of loyalty.

Of course, Shah Shuja did not agree to these terms, and the agreement collapsed. Do the first two conditions make any geopolitical sense? He still did it. There can be innumerable such historical examples of Dharmic resistance to unprovoked Semitic aggression.(5)

I am not imposing present-day ideologies on historical figures and/or events. I am just pointing out a fact that Hindutva is as organic a movement as possible in a pluralistic culture facing the brunt of Semitic imperialism across centuries. Nevertheless, we cannot stop at reactive mechanisms.

From here, where we go is entirely up to us. Hindutva is an organic growth of Hinduism facing existential threats, but we need to build on it. The movement cannot stop with political rhetoric and empty slogans. Knee-jerk reactions won’t survive the test of time. As the Ram Mandir in Ayodhya was built over the cursed structure, we need to build institutions that last. Eminent scholar Vidyaniwas Mishra (*Bhāratīya Cimtanadhārā*) elucidates that, in the journey of Indian history, the term ‘Hindu’ became synonymous with the word ‘Indian’. Hence, if the Indian state is severed from its civilisational roots, it has no purpose, and the Hindu civilisation without a (or more) state(s) cannot endure.

We must build a prosperous civilisational state to further the civilisational cause. We must adopt and develop policy frameworks that further our civilisational cause. I won’t digress here. These may be the topics of many other essays. I am hopeful that the acceptance of Hindutva as the natural response to Semiticism and its variants, such as secularism, will dispel systemic confusion and pave the way for greater and greater achievements for the Indian people.

The Paurāṇika story will not conclude by itself. Let us conclude the story now for the everlasting cycles of new beginnings:

*Since Dakṣa was decapitated, Śiva restores him using the head of a sacrificial goat. The resurrected Dakṣa awakens entirely stripped of his arrogance, recognises Śiva’s supremacy, and pleads for forgiveness. Śiva pardons him, permitting the completion of the yajña with Śiva receiving his primary oblations.*

The assertive and uncompromising Vīrabhadra signifies the re-institutionalisation of Yajña, without an attempt at the marginalisation of Śiva, not its destruction. Śiva cannot be ignored or disrespected. For Śiva is the sacrifice itself (yajño), the Lord of the sacrifice (yajñapatir), the sacrificer (yajvā), and the all-powerful destroyer of sacrifices (yajñānāmantakṛd).
