# From Cohesion to Capture: Temples and the Fault‑Lines of Hindu Society

### The People’s Sanctum: The 100 Year Struggle To Democratize Temples in India, Manuraj Shunmugasundram.

What happens when a society excludes a group from a social institution central to its polity? *The People’s Sanctum* by Manuraj Shunmugasundaram takes up this question by tracing the temple‑entry movement in southern India, alongside the legislative and judicial developments that advanced it. The book is premised on the social reform tradition, beginning with the Vaikom Satyagraha and extending into modern Dravidian politics. Shunmugasundaram celebrates the Self‑Respect movement and Dravidian politics as forces that helped foster a secular culture in India. This review, however, turns to the social dimensions that produced the fault‑lines: how genuine demands for inclusion were at times appropriated by bad‑faith actors to deepen divisions within Hindu society; how the puritanical attitudes of dominant communities excluded others and made temple wealth vulnerable to state control, which has often been alleged to be misused. It also considers how a contemporary movement for temple autonomy might be imagined — one that strengthens cohesion within the Hindu fold, nurtures mutual respect, and preserves cultural continuity.

### **Significance of Temples in South India**



In Hindu contexts, temples are places where devotees go to worship their deity. Every religious institution is governed by codes of conduct, which often include prohibitions on entry into certain spaces. Such codes are generally respected unless they profoundly affect daily social life. For instance, we do not see mass movements by women across religions where they are expressly barred from entry — *Sabarimala* being the exception. Yet even there, the complaint did not arise organically from society but from a civil society group with little locus in the matter. Temples in southern India were never merely religious institutions; they carried social, educational and cultural significance. Rulers frequently legitimised their authority through the temple, and the deity occupied a place in South Indian society akin to sovereign power (p. 7); some scholars considered the deity to be ‘paradigmatic sovereign’ (p. 72). Temples played an important role in polity: the *mandapam*, or spacious hall, functioned as a kind of courthouse where rulers pronounced verdicts (p. 8). A place without a temple was deemed uninhabitable (p. 7), which explains why Chola royals such as *Sembiyan Mahadevi* embarked on temple‑building sprees to cultivate a ‘loyal cohort of devout communities’, thereby crystallizing the legitimacy of her order (p. 9). Donations in the form of extensive tracts of land further entrenched the temple’s importance, as the agrarian economy was organized around it. Massive irrigation facilities were constructed in proximity to temples during the Chola and Vijayanagar periods (p. 10), underscoring the temple’s role not only as a spiritual center but as a fulcrum of economic and political life. They thus enjoyed royal patronage, while people from all walks of life contributed donations to sustain religious functions (p. 9). Temples played an important role in bringing the tribal society into the Hindu fold, and due to the political and social relevance of the temples, it was able to put them into religious services (p. 11). Shunmugasundaram notes that the rulers’ patronage for temples brought the two into such close alignment that, for their subjects, the distinction between king and shrine often disappeared (p. 73). Even the Company administration recognized the importance of temples, for they were endowed with large tracts of land, and control over such land conferred considerable local influence (p. 77). The complications arise when religious puritanism — the *sine qua non* of any religious institution — enters the political realm, and that seems to have been the case with the temples of southern India.

### **Caste hierarchy and exclusion from the polity**



Caste hierarchies in southern India were highly nuanced; they were not reducible to the simple image of higher orders living off the lower. Shunmugasundaram notes that signboards were erected outside temples specifying the exact distance to be maintained from a high‑caste individual (p. 27). Even members of the higher orders, such as the Kshatriyas, were required to keep two feet away from the priest (p. 27). In this sense, a form of untouchability was created, sometimes appearing to stem from puritanical observance of ritual for the deity. Yet the social hierarchy extended beyond ritual, becoming inhumane and spilling outside the strictly religious domain. Shunmugasundaram cites Manu S. Pillai’s *The Ivory Throne*, which records how a person from the so‑called untouchable caste could, in effect, be rendered an unapproachable witness even in a court of law (p. 27). The government was not wholly indifferent to reform: Shunmugasundaram notes that the Kingdom of Travancore permitted individuals of all castes and backgrounds to use public roads. Yet such measures were resisted by influential high‑caste traditionalists (p. 26). This suggests that the problem was less religious than social, with traditionalists actively obstructing reforms aimed at curbing entrenched practices. The issue became so acute that it exposed the extent to which traditionalists dehumanize the so‑called untouchables. Roads barred to them were not reserved exclusively for Brahmins and other caste Hindus but were also used by beef‑eating Christians and Muslims (p. 32). Vellore Iyyasamy Munuswamy Pillai — a member of the Constituent Assembly — expressed his dismay at untouchability, noting how such practices had compelled many to abandon their own faith (p. 106). Hence, the puritanism of the traditionalists excluded large sections of the Hindu population from participation in the polity. Those who had once been drawn into the broader Hindu fold through the temple were now dehumanised by traditionalists who regarded themselves as superior to all others.

### **Social Reform Movement and later capture by bad faith actors.**



The greatest anti‑untouchability movement, the Vaikom agitation, was initiated by leaders such as T. K. Mahadevan with the support of Congress. It was not an anti‑Hindu agitation; rather, it sought to open all roads to all persons within the Hindu fold, which were reserved for certain castes. Untouchability was entrenched to such a degree that the agitation drew the support of every non‑Brahmin social organization (p. 31). Gandhi’s reluctance to join compelled the agitators to turn to Erode Venkata Ramaswamy, who entered the movement bringing with him both substantial support and the funds necessary to sustain it (p. 35). Consensus might have been reached had it not been for the traditionalist Raghavaiah, Dewan of Travancore, who opposed the Queen Regent, Sethu Lakshmi Bayi, in her willingness to open dialogue with the agitators. Raghavaiah insisted instead on dealing with them through the police machinery (p. 37). The success of Vaikom inspired new confidence among social reformers. Gandhi believed it would foster reform and cohesion within the Hindu fold, but Ramaswamy was indifferent to religion, directing his attack against Hinduism itself. For him, atheism was not merely opposition to dogma but a political stance (p. 52). One of his ambitions in furthering the temple‑entry movement was to demonstrate that nothing of substance occurred through ritual; once entry was permitted, he argued, devotees would themselves become disillusioned with divinity (p. 66). At the same time, the region witnessed a series of non‑Brahmin conferences that culminated in the formation of the Justice Party. Its central ideological plank was the Dravidian–Aryan Brahmin divide, first articulated by Bishop Robert Caldwell, which sharpened antagonism between Brahmins and non‑Brahmins and encouraged the latter to conceive of themselves as a distinct ethnic identity. The Justice Party, however, was never aligned with the nationalist cause; it consistently sided with the British on most matters. As R. Parthasarathy notes in *Builders of Modern India: S. Satyamurti*, the Justice Party government even banned nationalist poems and publications. Thus, while the nationalist movement and the broader Hindu fold focused less on caste reform within society, the British sympathizers of the Justice Party pressed for a distinct ethnic identity for the non‑Brahmin population — a desperate attempt to fracture the Hindu fold and secure political advantage through division. Later, the stirrings of nationalist sentiment among members of the Justice Party weakened it considerably. Successive electoral defeats forced the party to abandon its political ambitions and reconstitute itself as a social reform organization under the name *Dravida Kazhagam* (Dravidian Party).

### **Eyes on the temple wealth**



In pre‑colonial times, governance through temples was a distinctive feature of rule. The devotion local people had for the deity posited it to a place akin to a sovereign, and rulers, seeking to secure support, extended royal patronage and tax exemptions. Temple‑building sprees and endowments of wealth became instruments of popularity and legitimacy, so that temple administration and governance often appeared as two faces of the same coin (p. 73). As Shunmugasundaram observes, a formal temple economy was borne and sustained under the patronage and purview of the sovereign (p. 74). During colonial times, the large property endowed to the temples could not be overlooked by the company administration, and they actively got involved in the temple affairs; however, they ensured that the vast properties endowed to temples were put to the purpose intended by the donor, which led to the Madras Endowments and Escheats Regulation, 1817. This authorized appropriation of rents and produce from lands granted to religious institutions to the intended purpose of the donor. After 1857, British policy moved towards separation of religion and state. The Religious Endowments Act of 1863 marked a decisive severance: temple administration was placed under committees of local figures who were neither trustees nor involved in daily management. Temples ceased to function as sovereign institutions, though they retained immense socio‑cultural weight. The Act nevertheless provided for judicial review of temple administration, limited to those with a recognized right of attendance (p. 82). Between 1863 and 1920 repeated attempts were made to amend the Act. The Madras Hindu Religious Endowments Bill of 1922, introduced by the Justice Party, sought to secularize temple funds by permitting the government to divert ‘surplus’ endowments to non‑religious expenditure. This opened the way for the state to determine what was non‑essential and to fund purposes wholly outside the temple’s purview. Opposition came not only from Brahmins but also from non‑Brahmin communities, who resisted centralized control (p. 146). The Justice Party, however, remained determined: at its 1944 conference in Salem, it proposed redirecting temple wealth towards education, healthcare, science and industrial development (p. 148). That resolve was carried forward when M. Karunanidhi became Chief Minister in 1969 (p. 152). Temple wealth was no longer regarded as religious endowment. Proponents of the Dravidian movement advanced a narrative that recast such offerings as a form of taxation, in which donations were not confined to religious use but could be diverted — even embezzled — for sovereign purposes.

### **Suggestion for Temple Autonomy Movement**



Shunmugasundaram notes that in pre‑colonial times temples served a strategic purpose, drawing people together and nurturing the idea of a nation‑state (p. 74). Temples have always remained close to the people: when non‑Brahmins were permitted to be appointed as *archakas* in 2022, Dalit communities expressed elation at being able to draw nearer to their god. The southern social justice movement was premised on a setting in which every community experienced some form of exclusion from temples and public spaces. These exclusions seeded fault‑lines that vested interests later exploited, propagating theories of difference from Brahmins and other Hindu communities thereby deepening divisions within Hindu society. It is worth noting that despite the vicious propaganda from the Self Right Movement, the participants of the movement cannot fully disassociate them from the religion. The Dravidian Munnetra Kazhagam, itself born of the Dravid Kazhagam movement, eventually split into the Anna Dravidian Munnetra Kazhagam, precisely because the former had become detached from religious values. For many in the southern states, the nationalist movement remained more compelling than the Self Right Movement —which did not support the nationalist cause — a dynamic that weakened the Justice Party. It is also pertinent that state control of temples has repeatedly misappropriated funds away from their intended religious purposes, a grievance around which contemporary temple movements now revolve. Such movements, however, must be careful not to fuel further division. Temples, once sites of social unity, were fractured by traditionalist capture; any call for autonomy must reckon with that history while recognizing the deep faith that continues to reside in the hearts of the people. Hindu society has always shifted with changing values. While the purity of the deity must be protected, puritanism cannot be tied to a person’s family or place of birth. Temples have long been spaces that draw people together, their shared devotion creating a sense of kinship across the Hindu fold. Any movement for temple autonomy must therefore align itself with this idea of unity, rather than deepen existing divisions.

### **Conclusion**



Temples have always been close to the people’s heart, an intrinsic part of society and a source of legitimacy for rulers. They were once places of unity, but the exclusionary puritanism of traditionalists seeded division. What had been a site of cohesion became, through the self‑serving approach of certain interests, a site of exclusion. Yet despite decades of propaganda against Hinduism and its institutions, practicing Hindus continue to hold their deities in high regard. For every attempt to demonize the religion, counter‑movements have arisen from within the same fold. The vested interests that entered the temple‑entry movement were not solely concerned with social reform; they also regularized the sovereign misappropriation of funds endowed by devotees for religious purposes. Contemporary calls for temple autonomy revolve around this misappropriation, but they must be carefully shaped to serve the cause of unity rather than deepen division. Non‑Brahmin religious institutions, as Shunmugasundaram notes, also resisted the centralized diversion of temple funds. It is high time that the broader Hindu community confronts this sovereign theft together, ensuring that exclusion does not creep back under the guise of puritanism.
