> Hari plucked two hairs from his body. One was white and the other was
> black. These two hairs entered the wombs of two women from the Yadu

>
> *sa cāpi keśau harir udbabarha śuklam ekam aparaṃ cāpi kṛṣṇam \| tau
> cāpi keśau viśatāṃ yadūnāṃ kule sthirau rohiṇīṃ devakīṃ ca \| tayor
> eko baladevo babhūva kṛṣṇo dvitīyaḥ keśavaḥ saṃbabhūva \|\|*

### **1. Midnight**



Imagine an ancient prison in Mathurā, a guarded chamber somewhere inside a fortified palace. Devakī is in labour with her eighth child. Six sons are dead, killed by Kaṃsa one after another. Her seventh pregnancy mysteriously ended before birth, or so Mathurā believes. Vasudeva is beside her and, in the familiar telling of this scene, the two are being held under guard while Kaṃsa waits for the child he has been warned will one day kill him.

This is no ordinary birth. Kaṃsa has spent years waiting for the prophecy to come true. The eighth child will be his death. Devakī is giving birth knowing that the moment the baby is discovered, Kaṃsa will come for him too.

Even the sky becomes part of the story. In the Padma Purāṇa’s section on the Janmāṣṭamī, Sanatkumāra explains that when the dark-half eighth of Śrāvaṇa falls under Rohiṇī, the observance is called Jayantī. The same passage then tells the worshipper to recreate that sky on the shrine itself: Rohiṇī in gold and the Moon in silver.

Padma Purāṇa, Uttara Khaṇḍa 31.32 to 33 (Deshpande translation):

> If in the month of Śrāvaṇa on the eighth of the dark half Rohiṇī is
> seen in the sky then that day is named Jayantī.

So while Devakī labours inside the prison, the signs associated with Kṛṣṇa’s birth start to appear.

The Purāṇic stories describe what happens next in much more detail. The guards suddenly fall asleep. The locked doors open. Rain pours over Mathurā. Vasudeva steps out with the newborn Kṛṣṇa in his arms, heading towards the Yamunā.

### **2. The weight of the world**



The story does not begin in the prison cell. It begins with the Earth appearing before Brahmā as a cow. Her complaint is that she has become too heavy: kings, armies, kingdoms, villages upon villages. She can no longer bear the burden.

Brahmā takes the Earth and the gods to the Ocean of Milk. There they pray to Viṣṇu for help. Brahmā receives the answer: Viṣṇu will descend to earth and be born in Vasudeva’s family, while the gods themselves are to be born among the Yādavas.

Years later, standing between two armies at Kurukṣetra, Kṛṣṇa explains why he comes:

Bhagavad Gītā 4.7 to 8 (Sargeant translation):

> Whenever a decrease of righteousness exists, Arjuna, and there is a
> rising up of unrighteousness, then I manifest Myself. For the
> protection of the good and the destruction of evil doers, for the sake
> of establishing righteousness, I am born in every age.
>
> *yadā yadā hi dharmasya glānir bhavati bhārata \| abhyutthānam
> adharmasya tadātmānaṃ sṛjāmy aham \|\| paritrāṇāya sādhūnāṃ vināśāya
> ca duṣkṛtām \| dharma-saṃsthāpanārthāya sambhavāmi yuge yuge \|\|*

“Whenever dharma declines, I come.” It is one of Kṛṣṇa’s most famous promises. But when the old books turn to this particular birth, they do not give just one reason

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HNyB!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2dfea2af-b026-4152-95bf-294509ccbbbf_1024x1024.heic)





.  
The Mahābhārata, Harivaṃśa, and the later Purāṇas all answer the question a little differently. Sometimes Kṛṣṇa comes to restore dharma. Sometimes he comes because the Earth is crushed under the weight of kings and armies. Sometimes he comes so that people trapped in ordinary suffering will have his deeds to hear and remember. The explanations overlap rather than cancel one another. One of them even concerns a demon:

Harivaṃśa 91.21 (Brodbeck translation):

> It was for the sake of Naraka’s death that strong-armed Janārdana, the
> bearer of conch, discus, mace, and sword, was born to Vasudeva and
> Devakī among the Vṛṣṇis.

The Bhāgavata Purāṇa puts two of them almost side by side. Kuntī first remembers the complaint with which this story began and, in the next verse, gives another reason for Kṛṣṇa’s birth:

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 1.8.34 to 35 (Tagare translation):

> As per prayer of god Brahmā you were born to lessen the burden of the
> earth which, like an overloaded boat in the sea, suffered from
> overpressure. Some others regard that you are born for performing
> deeds worth hearing and remembering by persons suffering in this
> worldly existence on account of nescience, desires and actions.
>
> *bhārāvatāraṇāyānye bhuvo nāva ivodadhau \| sīdantyā bhūri-bhāreṇa
> jāto hy ātma-bhuvārthitaḥ \|\| bhave ‘smin kliśyamānānām
> avidyā-kāma-karmabhiḥ \| śravaṇa-smaraṇārhāṇi kariṣyann iti kecana
> \|\|*

The Harivaṃśa, however, gives a much stranger reason for his birth: Nārāyaṇa wanted fame.

Harivaṃśa 43.77 (Brodbeck translation):

> Since Lord Nārāyaṇa wanted to be famous, he was born into the
> honourable family of Vasudeva the wise, who hailed from the lineage of
> Yayāti.
>
> *vasudevasya dhīmataḥ \| kule pūjye yaśaskāmo jajñe nārāyaṇaḥ prabhuḥ
> \|\|*

One might ask, why Vasudeva’s family?

The Harivaṃśa answers that question as well. At one of Varuṇa’s great sacrifices, the sage Kaśyapa took Varuṇa’s divine milking cows and would not return them. Varuṇa went to Brahmā to complain. These were no ordinary cattle: they are described as ageless, divine cows whose milk never spoiled. Kaśyapa had taken them at the urging of his wives Aditi and Surabhi.

Brahmā’s punishment was as follows… Since Kaśyapa had taken the cows, the part of him responsible for the theft would have to go down to earth and live among cows.

Harivaṃśa 45.32 (Brodbeck translation):

> With the part of himself which he used to steal the cows, the great
> Kaśyapa will go to earth and become a cowherd.
>
> *yenāṃśena hṛtā gāvaḥ kaśyapena mahātmanā \| sa tenāṃśena jagatīṃ
> gatvā gopatvameṣyati \|\|*

Aditi and Surabhi would follow him to earth as well. The Harivaṃśa then tells us who they have become: Kaśyapa’s earthly portion is Vasudeva, Aditi is Devakī, and Surabhi is Rohiṇī. It even places Vasudeva near Govardhana, living among cattle and paying taxes to Kaṃsa.

Harivaṃśa 45.35 (Brodbeck translation):

> Not far from Mathurā there’s a mountain called Govardhana, and he’s
> busy there with his cows, paying his taxes to Kaṃsa.

Two stories sit on top of each other here. Vasudeva belongs to the Yādava ruling line; he is not simply a poor cowherd. But the Harivaṃśa also makes his life among cattle the consequence of an old theft. The man chosen to become Kṛṣṇa’s father is, in another lifetime, paying off a curse.

Then, in the middle of all this, it gives Vasudeva one small promise. Vasudeva’s life will be blessed: Kṛṣṇa will call him father, and Vasudeva will call Kṛṣṇa his son.

The family tree above him carries an old scar. Ages back, King Yayāti lost his youth and asked his eldest son, Yadu, to hand over his own. The answer he got was that of a young man who had thought it through.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.18.40 to 41 (Tagare translation):

> I dare not accept and live with the old age which overtook you in the
> middle of life, for a man cannot attain to desirelessness without an
> actual experience of the worldly pleasures first.

Nobody gets to renunciation without having lived first. It was a good answer but it cost his line everything.

> O son! You were born from my heart, but you will not give your youth
> to me. Therefore, your offspring will have no share in the kingdom.

The Harivaṃśa keeps the father’s temper in it too:

> You imbecile! Your descendants won’t get the kingdom.

From Yadu, the Yādavas. From Puru, the Pauravas.

Generations later the curse was still good enough to throw as an insult in a hall full of kings. Śiśupāla, objecting when Kṛṣṇa was given the highest honour:

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.74.36 (Tagare translation):

> His family has been cursed by Yayāti and is excommunicated by all good
> people; he is always indulged in drinking; how is he eligible for this
> worship?

And the whole family hangs off that refusal in a few lines of genealogy. Through Devayānī, Yayāti had an eldest son named Yadu. In Yadu’s line came Devamīḍha; from that line came Śūra; and from Śūra’s house came the immensely illustrious Vasudeva.

From the cursed son to the man waiting in the prison cell. Keep the curse in mind. We will visit it again.

### **3. The tyrant**



Kaṃsa’s story becomes stranger when the Harivaṃśa talks about his birth.

Ugrasena was king of Mathurā. One day his queen was approached by Drumila, who took on Ugrasena’s appearance to deceive her. By the time she realized this, it was too late. Kaṃsa was born from that encounter.

The story matters here because Kaṃsa grows up aware that something about his birth sets him apart from the rest of Ugrasena’s family. Much later, when Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma have come to Mathurā and Kaṃsa is preparing to kill them, he says as much to the keeper of his war elephant.

Harivaṃśa 73.36 to 37 (Brodbeck translation):

> So I’m the son who was sown in Ugrasena’s field, elephant warden.
> Neglected by my mother and father, I was raised by my own fiery
> energy. I’m despised by both of them, and most especially by my
> relatives, and after I’ve killed the two cowherds, I’m going to kill
> them too.
>
> *viditvā sarvam ātmānaṃ vacanaṃ śraddadhāmy aham \| kṣetrajo ‘haṃ
> sutas tasya ugrasenasya hastipa \|\| mātāpitṛbhyāṃ saṃtyaktaḥ
> sthāpitaḥ svena tejasā \| ubhābhyām api vidviṣṭo bāndhavaiś ca
> viśeṣataḥ \|\| etān api haniṣyāmi yādavān kṛṣṇapakṣiṇaḥ \| tad imau
> ghātayitvā tu hastinā gopakilbiṣau \|\|*

“Sown in Ugrasena’s field” is the phrase to note here. Ugrasena is still his father in society, and Kaṃsa belongs to his household, but Kaṃsa knows the story of his conception. He also believes that his parents and relatives have rejected him. By the end of the speech, the people he wants dead are not strangers. They are his own Yādava relatives.

Kaṃsa eventually takes the throne from Ugrasena but does not kill him. He also marries two daughters of Jarāsandha, the powerful king of Magadha, giving him an important ally outside Mathurā. At the same time, the families of Ugrasena and Vasudeva are already tied together by marriage. So by the time Kṛṣṇa returns to Mathurā, this is not simply a hero coming to overthrow a distant tyrant. It is a family conflict.

Note that Mathurā itself is not presented as a wasteland. Kaṃsa comes off as a tyrant ruling a functioning city.

More interestingly, Kaṃsa does not think of himself as helpless before prophecy. He has a theory about how fate can be beaten. He reasons that since the danger will arrive in human form, human planning should be enough to stop it.

Harivaṃśa 47.6 to 7 (Brodbeck translation):

> This is a plan for dealing with a human, so we can accomplish it as
> mere humans. Listen to how people like me can foil the will of the
> gods. When many secret details have been properly arranged, when the
> medicines have been properly administered, and when the effort is
> fitted to the facts, then even the will of the gods falls into line.
>
> *eṣa mānuṣyako yatno mānuṣair eva sādhyate \| śrūyatāṃ yena daivaṃ hi
> madvidhaiḥ pratihanyate \|\| mantrāgrāmaiḥ suvihitair auṣadhaiś ca
> suyojitaiḥ \| yatnena cānukūlena daivam apy anulomyate \|\|*

Kaṃsa believes enough careful planning can beat a prophecy.

Years later, we get a glimpse of what it feels like to live under him. Akrūra, one of Kaṃsa’s own men, is travelling to Mathurā with Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma. On the way, he stops at the Yamunā and has a vision of Kṛṣṇa’s divine form in the water. When he returns to the chariot, Kṛṣṇa is still sitting there in front of him.

Akrūra has just seen the man beside him revealed as God, yet his next thought is about Kaṃsa:

The marvel that I have seen in the stream of the Yamunā I behold before me, even here, in a bodily shape; but enough of this, let us proceed to Mathurā: I am afraid Kaṃsa will be angry at our delay: such is the wretched consequence of eating the bread of another.

Akrūra has just witnessed a divine revelation, but he is still worried about being late because Kaṃsa might be angry.

That tells us something about Kaṃsa’s Mathurā. His power is not only in prisons and executions, but it also impacts ordinary calculations people make about their jobs.

There is more to Kaṃsa. The Harivaṃśa identifies him with Kālanemi, an older enemy of Viṣṇu who had already been defeated once. So Kṛṣṇa and Kaṃsa are not meeting for the first time in Mathurā. The Harivaṃśa places their conflict inside an older cycle of hostility between Viṣṇu and his enemy.

### **4. A voice at a wedding**



The story proper starts at a wedding. Devakī was Kaṃsa’s young cousin, though the books often simply call her his sister. The Mahābhārata mentions a version of her marriage with not much drama surrounding it. A Yādava named Śini wins Devakī for Vasudeva, places her on his chariot, and the genealogy moves on

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Hq3R!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd39b1dbe-444c-40b9-b4c7-6f2d52ed409a_1254x1254.heic)





.

The Purāṇic versions turn the same wedding into something much more dramatic. Kaṃsa himself is driving the newly married Devakī and Vasudeva away when a voice suddenly comes from the sky.

Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.1 (Wilson translation):

> As they were going along, a voice in the sky, sounding aloud and deep
> as thunder, addressed Kaṃsa and said, “Fool that you are, the eighth
> child of the damsel whom you are driving in the car shall take away
> your life!”
>
> *athāntarikṣe vāg uccaiḥ kaṃsam ābhāṣya sādaram \|
> meghagambhīranirghoṣaṃ samābhāṣyedam abravīt \|\| yām enāṃ vahase
> mūḍha saha bhartrā rathe sthitām \| asyās tavāṣṭamo garbhaḥ prāṇān
> apahariṣyati \|\|*

Kaṃsa’s first reaction is visceral: he reaches for his sword and turns on Devakī. Vasudeva has nothing to defend her with, so he tries to reason with Kaṃsa.

Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.1 (Wilson translation):

> On hearing this, Kaṃsa drew his sword and was about to put Devakī to
> death; but Vasudeva interposed, saying, “Kill not Devakī, great
> warrior; spare her life and I will deliver to you every child that she
> may bring forth.”
>
> *ity ākarṇya samādāya khaḍgaṃ kaṃso mahābalaḥ \| devakīṃ hantum
> ārabdho vasudevo ‘bravīd idam \|\| na hantavyā mahābhāga devakī
> bhavatā tava \| samarpayiṣye sakalān garbhān asyāudarodbhavān \|\|*
>
> [](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pULF!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8e6add07-98e7-432e-970d-0622ae4babb3_1254x1254.heic)
>
> ![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pULF!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8e6add07-98e7-432e-970d-0622ae4babb3_1254x1254.heic)
>
> 
>
> 

First, he begs Kaṃsa to spare Devakī. Devakī herself is not the danger, he argues, since the prophecy concerns her future child.

Then Vasudeva shifts to the subject of death. Everyone who is born must die someday, whether that happens now or a hundred years from now. A person should try to avoid death while he can, but some things cannot be escaped forever.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.1.38 (Tagare translation):

> Death is born along with the body for those who have taken birth, O
> hero. The death of all living beings is fixed, whether it occurs today
> or after a hundred years.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.1.48 (Tagare translation):

> Death should be warded off by an intelligent person for as long as
> strength and intelligence are vigorous, but if it cannot be prevented,
> then there is no fault on the part of the embodied soul.

Kaṃsa is not convinced, so Vasudeva finally offers him something more practical: spare Devakī, and I will bring you every child she gives birth to.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.1.54 (Tagare translation):

> This fear of yours which the unembodied voice spoke about is not on
> account of this woman, O gentle one; I will offer up her sons; it is
> on account of them that your fear has arisen.

It is a terrible bargain, but in that moment it saves her life.

And Vasudeva keeps his word.

When their first son is born, Vasudeva does exactly what he promised. He takes the baby to Kaṃsa and strangely, Kaṃsa gives the child back.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.1.60 (Tagare translation):

> Let this boy return to his mother as there is no danger from him to
> me, as my death has been ordained from your eighth child.
>
> *pratiyātu kumāro ‘yaṃ na hy asmād asti me bhayam \| aṣṭamād yuvayor
> garbhān mṛtyur me vihitaḥ kila \|\|*

For a moment, it looks as though the worst might be avoided.

Then Nārada arrives.

The Harivaṃśa introduces him almost mischievously. Nārada is a great sage, but also someone who carries secrets from one place to another and seems to enjoy the quarrels that follow.

Harivaṃśa 44.10 (Brodbeck translation):

> He’s a learned great seer who knows the lore of the light-elves and
> divulges secrets on earth, and he takes pleasure in quarrels, like a
> planet whose appearance portends quarrels.

He comes to Kaṃsa with information that makes an already frightened man even more dangerous.

Harivaṃśa 46.15 to 19 (Brodbeck translation):

> This Devakī, your cousin here in Mathurā, her eighth child will be
> your death, Kaṃsa. … But you should respect him, for he’s been your
> death before. Remember him! If he can be dealt with while he’s still
> an infant, Kaṃsa, then you should try to arrange that. This is a
> favour from me to you, and it’s the reason why I came. Good luck to
> you.
>
> *tatraiṣā devakī yā te mathurāyāṃ pitṛṣvasā \| asyā garbho’ṣṭamaḥ
> kaṃsa sa te mṛtyurbhaviṣyati \|\| ślāghyaśca sa hi te
> mṛtyurbhūtapūrvaśca taṃ smara \| yatnaśca kriyatāṃ kaṃsa garbhasthe
> yadi śakyate \|\| eṣā me tvadgatā prītirityarthaṃ cāhamāgataḥ \|
> bhujyantāṃ sarvakāmārthāḥ svasti te’stu vrajāmyaham \|\|*

The warning is already bad enough: your killer is coming, you have faced him before, and if you want to survive, deal with him while he is still a baby.

Some later versions make Kaṃsa’s fear even worse by playing with the number eight. What exactly counts as the eighth child? Count from which direction? Why assume the child destined to kill him will arrive in the neat order he expects?

The important part is what happens inside Kaṃsa after Nārada leaves. The Harivaṃśa says his mind is burning. The old Kālanemi, the enemy of Viṣṇu with whom the text identifies Kaṃsa, seems to rise inside him again.

And then the story becomes brutally short.

Harivaṃśa 48.2 (Brodbeck translation):

> The Sixkids were born, and Kaṃsa smashed them on the stone floor.
>
> *ṣaḍgarbhānniḥsṛtān kaṃsastāñjaghāna śilātale \|*
>
> One line. Six dead children.

Later books at least give those children names, although the names change from text to text. One tradition gives Kīrtimat, Suṣeṇa, Bhadrasena, Ṛju, Sammardana and Bhadra. Another remembers them as Smara, Udgītha, Parisvaṅga, Pataṅga, Kṣudrabhṛt and Ghṛṇin.

And that is almost all we are told about those years.

Devakī becomes pregnant.

A child is born.

Kaṃsa kills him.

Then it happens again (six times).

The story moves on. Devakī clearly does not.

Much later, after Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma have grown up and Kaṃsa is dead, Devakī asks them for something: she wants to see her murdered sons again.

I desire to see, brought to life, my sons who have been killed by Kaṃsa, the King of Bhojas.

Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma bring them back to her.

For one evening, Devakī gets to be their mother again.

Devakī had her breasts filled with milk. Hugging them and placing them on her lap, she smelt their crowns repeatedly.

The Bhāgavata then adds one final theological turn. The sons drink the milk that remains after Kṛṣṇa has once drunk from her breast, and through that contact with Nārāyaṇa they recover knowledge of who they truly are.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.85.55 (Tagare translation):

> Having sucked the nectar of her milk which was left over there after
> Lord Kṛṣṇa’s sucking, and due to the contact of the person of Lord
> Nārāyaṇa, they regained their self-knowledge.
>
> *pītvāmṛtaṃ payas tasyāḥ pīta-śeṣaṃ gadā-bhṛtaḥ \|
> nārāyaṇāṅga-saṃsparśa-pratilabdhātma-darśanāḥ \|\|*

Then they leave her again.

### **5. The plan**



While Kaṃsa made his plans in Mathurā, Viṣṇu made his own.

Before the killings begin, the Harivaṃśa takes us somewhere unexpected: into the underworld, where six beings called the Ṣaḍgarbhas lie asleep in what the text describes as a womb of water.

Harivaṃśa 47.23 to 25 (Brodbeck translation):

> Viṣṇu went to the underworld, where the Sixkid demons were tucked up
> resting in their home, a womb of water, entered their bodies in the
> form of a dream, extracted their souls and gave them to Sleep.

Their story goes back to an older curse connected with Kālanemi, the earlier enemy of Viṣṇu with whom Kaṃsa is identified.

Harivaṃśa 47.21 to 22 (Brodbeck translation):

> Because this name of yours, the Sixkids, was given to you by your
> father, that same father will kill all of you himself, when you’ve
> become kids, and the great Sixkid demons will become exactly six kids
> of Devakī, and Kaṃsa will kill you when you’re kids.

Viṣṇu has already decided that these six beings will become Devakī’s first six children.

But Viṣṇu does not place them in Devakī himself. He gives that job to the goddess of Sleep.

Harivaṃśa 47.27 to 28 (Brodbeck translation):

> These living souls are called the Sixkids: take them and put them, one
> after another, into Devakī’s six embryos. When they’ve been born as
> infants and led to the house of Yama, and Kaṃsa’s plan has proved to
> be fruitless, and Devakī’s labours have borne fruit,
>
> *imān prāṇeśvarān gṛhya ṣaḍgarbhān dānavottamān \| ṣaḍgarbhān
> devakīgarbhe yojayasva yathākramam \|\| jāteṣv eteṣu garbheṣu nīteṣu
> ca yamakṣayam \| kaṃsasya viphale yatne devakyāḥ saphale śrame \|\|*

So the first six pregnancies are already part of a larger plan. Kaṃsa thinks he is preventing the prophecy by killing the babies. The text tells us that even these deaths have already been folded into the story that will eventually defeat Kaṃsa.

Viṣṇu then turns to the seventh pregnancy.

This child will not die. He is to be removed from Devakī’s womb during the seventh month and placed inside Rohiṇī, another wife of Vasudeva who is living safely among the cowherds.

Harivaṃśa 47.30 to 32 (Brodbeck translation):

> Devakī’s seventh foetus, an auspicious portion, will be my elder
> brother; in the seventh month, you must transfer him to Rohiṇī;
> because the foetus was extracted, that youngster will be called
> Saṅkarṣaṇa the Extraction.

The name explains the event. Saṅkarṣaṇa is the child who was “drawn away” or transferred. Vraja will know him better as Balarāma, Kṛṣṇa’s elder brother.

Where is he being hidden? In the household of the very man who keeps Kaṃsa’s own cattle.

Then Viṣṇu gives the goddess of Sleep another role. She too will be born on earth.

Her mother will be Yaśodā, Nanda’s wife in the cowherd settlement. She will be born on the same night as Kṛṣṇa, but on the other side of the river.

Harivaṃśa 47.33 to 35 (Brodbeck translation):

> There’s a woman, brought up in a cowherding family, who’s the beloved
> wife of cowherd Nanda, the keeper of Kaṃsa’s cows. She’s named Yaśodā,
> the Giver of Glory, if you please. You’ll be her child, the ninth in
> our group, born on the ninth day, of the dark fortnight, naturally,
> during the moon’s conjunction with the constellation Abhijit the
> Victorious, at midnight when the night is no longer young. I’ll make
> the delivery go smoothly.
>
> *yā tu sā nandagopasya dayitā bhuvi viśrutā \| yaśodā nāma bhadraṃ te
> bhāryā gopakulodvahā \|\| tasyās tvaṃ navamo garbhaḥ kule ‘smākaṃ
> bhaviṣyasi \| navamyām eva saṃjātā kṛṣṇapakṣasya vai tithau \|\| ahaṃ
> tv abhijito yoge niśāyāṃ yauvane sthite \| ardharātre kariṣyāmi
> garbhamokṣaṃ yathāsukham \|\|*

Sleep is not just an idea here. She is a goddess who is also promised a future of her own.

Harivaṃśa 47.29 (Brodbeck translation):

> I’ll do you a favour on earth that matches the favour you’ve done me,
> and as a result, goddess, you’ll become the goddess of the whole
> world.

Harivaṃśa 47.51 (Brodbeck translation):

> With a retinue of sprites and a fixed preference for flesh offerings,
> you’ll be worshipped on the ninth lunar day, with animal sacrifices.

Viṣṇu tells her that people will worship her when they are lost in dangerous places, trapped, threatened by death, deprived of children or wealth, or afraid of sickness. The goddess who begins this story as Sleep will later be known by many names: Durgā, Bhadrakālī, Vijayā, Vaiṣṇavī, Caṇḍikā, Māyā, Nārāyaṇī, Ambikā and others.

Then the transfer of the seventh child actually happens. According to the Bhāgavata, people believe Devakī has miscarried.

The Harivaṃśa tells the scene differently. The woman it shows in distress is Rohiṇī. She experiences the transfer almost like a dream:

As if in a dream, she saw the foetus that had come out of her body. A short while later she couldn’t find the child, and she became distressed.

The child has been drawn out of Devakī and placed inside Rohiṇī.

Later, the text makes the relationship between the two brothers explicit:

Whatever you are for the sake of the worlds, I am too; the two of us have a single body, but we’ve been divided in two for the earth’s sake.

Then comes the eighth pregnancy. And this is where Kaṃsa’s earlier theory about defeating prophecy becomes practical.

According to the Harivaṃśa, Kaṃsa wants Devakī watched without her knowing it. He orders his people to monitor the house, count the months of her pregnancy and keep Vasudeva under surveillance.

Harivaṃśa 47.3 to 5 (Brodbeck translation):

> Monitored when she’s at home, watched over by secret agents, Devakī
> must go about as she pleases without suspecting anything, while my
> forces stand at the ready. My women must count the months since her
> last period, so that we’ll know how much time remains before any child
> is due; wherever they may be, Vasudeva and his wife must be watched,
> absolutely night and day, by vigilant people loyal to me, without any
> women or eunuchs mentioning the reason for this.
>
> *devakī ca gṛhe guptā pracchannair abhirakṣitā \| svairaṃ caratu
> viśrabdhā garbhakāle tu rakṣyatām \|\| māsān vai puṣpamāsādīn
> gaṇayantu mama striyaḥ \| pariṇāme tu garbhasya śeṣaṃ jñāsyāmahe vayam
> \|\| vasudevas tu saṃrakṣyaḥ strīsanāthāsu bhūmiṣu \| apramattair mama
> hite rātrāv ahani caiva hi \| strībhir varṣavaraiś caiva vaktavyaṃ na
> tu kāraṇam \|\|*

Every month matters because Kaṃsa believes that if he knows exactly when the child is coming, he can still control what happens next. This is the theory from section 3 put into practice.

The child he is watching for has already taken over his thoughts. The Bhāgavata says that whether Kaṃsa is sitting, lying down, walking or eating, Kṛṣṇa is constantly in his mind.

Meanwhile, something very different is happening around Devakī.

At night the gods come to her.

The Viṣṇu Purāṇa describes them praising not simply the child inside her, but Devakī herself:

Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.2 (Wilson translation):

> You are sacrifice, whence all fruit proceeds: you are the wood, whose
> attrition engenders fire. As Aditi, you are the parent of the gods; as
> Diti, you are the mother of the Daityas, their foes.

The hymn keeps widening. Devakī becomes the altar, the heavens, patience, creation itself:

You are patience, the parent of fortitude: you are the heavens and your children are the stars: and from you does all that exists proceed.

The gods have come because Devakī is carrying Viṣṇu.

### **6. The night**



Śrāvaṇa. Monsoon. The dark half of the month. The eighth night.

By now the story has brought everyone into position. Devakī is in Mathurā. Yaśodā is across the Yamunā in the cowherd settlement. Rohiṇī is already carrying the child taken from Devakī’s seventh pregnancy. Kaṃsa is watching and waiting.

Then midnight comes.

The Harivaṃśa mentions two births happening at the same time. Devakī and Yaśodā have been pregnant for the same length of time, and in the same midnight hour one gives birth to Viṣṇu and the other to a girl.

Harivaṃśa 48.11 to 13 (Brodbeck translation):

> Yaśodā and Devakī had both been pregnant for the same length of time,
> and when the bejewelled midnight hour had come, under the
> constellation of Abhijit the Victorious, Devakī gave birth to Viṣṇu,
> and Yaśodā gave birth to a little girl.
>
> *tulyakālaṃ hi garbhiṇyau yaśodā devakī tathā \| aṣṭamyāṃ śrāvaṇe māse
> kṛṣṇapakṣe mahātithau \| rohiṇyāmardharātre ca sudhāṃśorudaye tathā \|
> devakyajanayadviṣṇuṃ yaśodā tāṃ tu kanyakām \| muhūrte’bhijite prāpte
> sārdharātre vibhūṣite \|\|*

Two women, on two sides of a river, giving birth in the same hour. And this is one of those moments where the old books tell the same night differently.

The Bhāgavata Purāṇa gives us darkness and the moon:

At midnight, when deep darkness had fallen, Janārdana was born; Viṣṇu, who dwells in the heart of everyone, appeared in Devakī, who resembled a goddess, like the full moon appearing in the eastern direction.

The Harivaṃśa makes the whole world react:

When Janārdana was born the oceans trembled, the mountains shook, and fires that had gone out blazed up again; benign breezes blew, the dust was cleared away, and the stars shone.

The Viṣṇu Purāṇa makes the same moment gentler:

At midnight, when the supporter of all was about to be born, the clouds emitted low pleasing sounds and poured down rain of flowers.

Different books, different skies. But inside the room, Vasudeva is looking at the same child.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.3.9 to 10 (Bryant translation):

> Vasudeva saw that amazing, lotus-eyed child, his four arms wielding
> the weapons of the conch, club, lotus and disc. He bore the mark of
> śrīvatsa, and the Kaustubha jewel was radiant on his neck. Clad in a
> yellow garment, he appeared as beautiful as a dark rain-cloud. He was
> resplendent with a magnificent belt, and arm and wrist bracelets, and
> his profuse locks were encircled with a lustrous helmet and earrings
> made of valuable vaidūrya gems.
>
> *tam adbhutaṃ bālakam ambujekṣaṇaṃ catur-bhujaṃ
> śaṅkha-gadādy-udāyudham \| śrīvatsa-lakṣmaṃ gala-śobhi-kaustubhaṃ
> pītāmbaraṃ sāndra-payoda-saubhagam \|\|
> mahārha-vaidūrya-kirīṭa-kuṇḍala-tviṣā pariṣvakta-sahasra-kuntalam \|
> uddāma-kāñcy-aṅgada-kaṅkaṇādibhir virocamānaṃ vasudeva aikṣata \|\|*

Four arms. A conch, discus, mace and lotus. The Kaustubha jewel from the churning of the ocean. A crown.

All of this on a child who has just been born.

Vasudeva immediately understands who lies before him. But after the amazement comes a much more practical thought: Kaṃsa.

You are born, O sovereign god of gods, bearer of the shell, the discus and the mace; but now in mercy withhold this your celestial form, for Kaṃsa will assuredly put me to death when he knows that you have descended in my dwelling.

In other words: put it away or I am a dead man.

Devakī is frightened for the same reason. She knows she is looking at the divine being the gods have been waiting for, but she is also looking at her newborn son and she knows Kaṃsa is somewhere above them.

God of gods, who are all things, who comprise all the regions of the world in your person and who by your illusion have assumed the condition of an infant, have compassion upon us and forego this your four-armed shape, nor let Kaṃsa, the impious son of Diti, know of your descent.

She is speaking to the soul of the universe and to her baby in the same breath.

Kṛṣṇa’s answer is surprising because he does not begin with Kaṃsa at all. He begins with something that happened long before this night.

Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.3 (Wilson translation):

> Princess, in former times I was prayed to by you and adored in the
> hope of progeny: your prayers have been granted, for I am born your
> son.

You asked for a child. Here I am.

Only then does Kṛṣṇa explain the four arms. He says he showed them so the two people in the room would recognize who had come back to them.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.3.44 (Tagare translation):

> I have revealed this form of Mine to you in order to remind you of my
> previous births from you; otherwise the knowledge about my real being
> is not possible through the semblance of human form.

This is not their first birth together.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.3.32 (Tagare translation):

> Oh chaste lady, in your previous birth in the age of Svāyambhuva Manu,
> you were Pṛśni and this sinless Vasudeva your husband, was then a
> Prajāpati named Sutapas.

Once, Devakī was Pṛśni and Vasudeva was Sutapas. They wanted a child and performed severe austerities for one. Viṣṇu came as their son. Later they were Aditi and Kaśyapa, and he came again.

Now the names are Devakī and Vasudeva, and the same child is before them for a third time.

The four arms are therefore doing more than announcing divinity. They are a reminder between parents and a son who, according to the story, have done this before.

And then comes the sentence I find hardest to read in this scene:

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.3.39 (Tagare translation):

> Though both of you had not tasted the conjugal and other pleasures of
> ordinary sensual objects and were childless, you did not seek for
> Emancipation from the world as you were deluded by My divine Māyā.

This part lands differently after everything Devakī has already lost. The text says the couple could have asked for liberation. They did not. They wanted a son.

Devakī has now lost six sons. Mathurā believes her seventh pregnancy ended before birth. The eighth child is finally before her, telling her that this desire for him goes back across lifetimes.

Devakī asks him for one last thing.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.3.30 (Tagare translation):

> Oh soul of the universe! May you withdraw this Superhuman form with
> four arms furnished with the superb splendour of conch, discus, mace
> and lotus.

Devakī does not ask him to go away. She asks him to stop looking like Viṣṇu.

He does.

The four arms disappear. So do the weapons, crown and jewels. What remains looks like a newborn child. Only after that does the escape begin.

In the Bhāgavata, Yogamāyā opens the prison doors and the guards fall asleep.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.3.48 to 49 (Tagare translation):

> All the entrances, which had been securely shut with huge doors and
> iron bolts and chains, opened of their own accord through Yogamāyā’s
> influence, just as darkness disappears before the sun.
>
> All of the doorkeepers and citizens slept, their consciousness and all
> their functions overcome by sleep; the clouds, rumbling mildly,
> showered rain; Śeṣa followed, warding off the water with his hoods.

The storm is still going outside. Vasudeva lifts the child and walks out. Śeṣa follows, spreading his hoods over the baby against the rain.

The prophecy is now outside the prison.

And Vasudeva still has a river to cross.

### **7. The crossing**



The Yamunā in Śrāvaṇa, at midnight, in a storm, is not a river a man wades.

While Indra was pouring down rain incessantly, the river Yamunā, younger sister of Yama, the lord of death, foaming with the force of waves from its mass of deep water, became agitated with hundreds of fearful whirlpools. But she gave passage, as the ocean did to Rāma, the husband of Sītā.

That is all one book will say about it. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa is more precise, and gives us the picture everybody paints.

Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.3 (Wilson translation):

> To protect the infant from the heavy rain that fell from the clouds of
> night, Śeṣa, the many-headed serpent, followed Vasudeva and spread his
> hoods above their heads; and when the prince, with the child in his
> arms, crossed the Yamunā river, deep as it was and dangerous with
> numerous whirlpools the waters were stilled and rose not above his
> knee.
>
> *varṣatāṃ jaladānāṃ ca toyam atyulbaṇaṃ niśi \| saṃcchādyānuyayau
> śeṣaḥ phaṇair ānakadundubhim \|\| yamunāṃ cātigambhīrāṃ
> nānāvartaśatākulām \| vasudevo vahan viṣṇuṃ jānumātravahāṃ yayau \|\|*

The line everybody remembers is the last one: the water comes only to his knees.

The Padma Purāṇa adds a scene I had never seen in the paintings. The baby slips into the river.

O lord of the worlds, protect my son.

Vasudeva says that to the person he has just dropped.

The Harivaṃśa tells the exchange with almost no scenery:

Harivaṃśa 48.18 to 19 (Brodbeck translation):

> As quickly as he could, Vasudeva, frightened but full of love for his
> son, picked up the child and went to Yaśodā’s house. There, without
> anyone noticing, he set the boy down for Yaśodā, and then he took the
> girl away and put her on Devakī’s bed.
>
> *vasudevastu saṃgṛhya dārakaṃ kṣiprameva tu \| śayānamaṅke devakyā
> rātrāvādāya yādavaḥ \| yaśodāyā gṛhaṃ bhīto viveśa sutavatsalaḥ \|\|
> yaśodāyāstvavijñātaṃ tatra nikṣipya dārakam \| gṛhya tāṃ dārikāṃ cāpi
> devakīśayane’nyasat \|\|*

No serpent. No doors. What survives is the man’s state of mind: frightened, full of love, quick.

He crosses back with a daughter who is not his.

Harivaṃśa 48.20 to 21 (Brodbeck translation):

> He was giddy with fear for both infants, but he’d completed his task.
> Ānakadundubhi then announced to Ugrasena’s son Kaṃsa that a beautiful
> baby girl had been born.

Ānakadundubhi is another name for Vasudeva. He has done the impossible part, but he still has to finish the deception. In the Harivaṃśa, he reports the girl’s birth himself. In the Bhāgavata, the doors close again and the bonds return to their old state, as though nobody had ever left.

Across the river, Yaśodā wakes to a different reality. She remembers giving birth, but Sleep has taken the details from her. She cannot even say whether the child born to her was a boy or a girl.

Now there is a boy beside her.

### **8. The girl who could not be killed**



The word went up the stairs before the sun did, and the king came down himself. Perhaps a girl did not count. Perhaps a prophecy is a prophecy. He took the baby by the heels, the way he had taken the other six, and swung her at the stone.

She never touched it.

She went out of his fingers and up, and she did not come down. Wearing clothes of blue and yellow, she had breasts like the globes on an elephant’s head, a bottom as broad as a chariot, a face like the moon, and four arms. She shone with a colour as clear as lightning; her eyes were like newly risen suns; her voice like a thundercloud. She was like a twilight with breasts instead of clouds.

Armed, garlanded, laughing in the air, she says his name twice.

Harivaṃśa 48.34 to 35 (Brodbeck translation):

> Kaṃsa, Kaṃsa. It was for the sake of your own ruin that you destroyed
> me, lifting me up suddenly and smacking me down onto the stone.
> Because you did that, at the time of your death, when you’re being
> dragged around by your enemy, I will tear your body open with my bare
> hands and drink your warm blood.
>
> *kaṃsa kaṃsa vināśāya yadahaṃ ghātitā tvayā \| sahasā ca samutkṣipya
> śilāyāṃ vinipātitā \|\| tasmāttavāntakāle’haṃ kṛṣyamāṇasya śatruṇā \|
> pāṭayitvā karairdehamuṣṇaṃ pāsyāmi śoṇitam \|\|*

The threat is not a literal preview of Kaṃsa’s death. Kṛṣṇa kills him in the arena years later.

The girl has one more thing to say before she leaves:

You dunce. What have you gained by killing me? Your destroyer, your adversary in the former birth, is born somewhere. Do not unnecessarily kill helpless, innocent children.

Somebody finally says it. Stop killing the children.

Everything she had been promised under the water came out of that cell with her. They who address you morning and afternoon with reverence and praise and call you Āryā, Durgā, Vedagarbha, Ambikā, Bhadrā, Bhadrakālī, Kṣemī or Kṣemaṅkarī, shall receive from my bounty whatever they desire. Propitiated with offerings of wine and flesh and various viands, you shall bestow upon mankind all their prayers. The Harivaṃśa moves her worship into hill country and dangerous passes, among people carrying goods through them and among the people who rob them. The traveller’s goddess and the robber’s goddess are the same, and the books host both without noticing. One of them even paints her shrine the way the robbers would want it painted: she’s had her consecration, and she answers prayers; attended by gangs of sprites, she’s praised by violent brigands and delighted when large animals are offered; she’s blessed with a pair of jugs, one full of liquor, one full of meat, and she’s decorated with peacock-tails and bright peacock-feather armbands. All of it promised, under the water, to a goddess who entered this story as somebody’s baby daughter.

Years later, in Vraja, a crowd of village girls fasted and prayed to her, and what they asked her for was him. O goddess Kātyāyanī, great Māyā, great yoginī, supreme Lord; honour to you! Please make the son of Nanda, the gopa, my husband, O Goddess. The goddess of the ninth night, petitioned for the boy of the eighth.

And she stayed in the family. They called her Ekānaṃśā and said that this invincible yogi girl was unique in being born to protect Keśava; years later, in a crowd, everyone gazed at that dear sister between Rāma and Kṛṣṇa, looking like Lakṣmī sitting on the lotus, holding golden lotuses in her hands. The sister survives outside the story too. Some early Mathurā sculptures show Balarāma and Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa with a goddess between them, often identified as Ekānaṃśā. The dating and identification of individual pieces are debated, so I would not make the sculpture prove more than it can. But the family picture is worth noticing: the sister is still there in the middle.

Back in the cell, Kaṃsa bows to Devakī.

I bow my head down to your two feet, Devakī, as a son of yours would. Don’t be resentful towards me. I myself know that I did wrong.

She calls him what she might have called him as a child:

Get up, get up, my calf.

And then this:

When you bow your head down to my two feet, disgusted by your own behaviour, I can forgive what you did in destroying my babies.

It does not reform him. The fear returns. The search widens.

One more correction to the picture most of us know: in the Harivaṃśa, the chains belong later. When Kaṃsa cannot capture Bala and Keśava, he punishes the family left in Mathurā. The irons are traditional. They are not part of this early scene on the night of the birth.

### **9. Morning in Gokula**



Morning comes to Gokula with no knowledge of what happened in Mathurā. Yaśodā has a son. Everyone around her can see that.

The house fills with people. The cowherds bring gifts, curd and butter, and the birth becomes a celebration. Later tradition remembers that morning as Nandotsava, Nanda’s festival.

But Nanda is not outside Kaṃsa’s world. The Harivaṃśa calls him the keeper of Kaṃsa’s cattle. The child Kaṃsa wants dead is growing up in the household of a man who still owes the king taxes.

The same text makes that dependence uncomfortable rather than comic. When Nanda goes to Mathurā to pay the year’s taxes, he speaks like a father who knows exactly what kind of king he serves.

Harivaṃśa 49.5 to 6 (Brodbeck translation):

> Of all fathers I’ll be the one who deserves to be criticised by the
> ancestors, because I’m not going to see the face of my only baby son.
> For even though I’m honest and wise, my wisdom has been removed
> against my will, because I’m afraid of Kaṃsa. It’s truly merciless
> when a child is killed.
>
> *ahaṃ vācyo bhaviṣyāmi pitṛpakṣeṣu putriṇām \| yo’hamekasya putrasya
> na paśyāmi śiśormukham \|\| hriyate hi balātprajñā prājñasyāpi sato
> mama \| asmāddhi me bhayaṃ kaṃsānnirghṛṇādvai śiśorvadhe \|\|*

It does not stay safe for long. A nurse comes out of Mathurā with poison on her breast.

Harivaṃśa 50.22 (Brodbeck translation):

> That night, while everyone was asleep, she offered Kṛṣṇa her breast.
> Kṛṣṇa drained her breast, and with it her life, and he roared. The
> bird fell to the ground immediately, her breast in tatters.
>
> *dadau stanaṃ ca kṛṣṇāya tasmin supte jane niśi \| tasyāḥ stanaṃ papau
> kṛṣṇaḥ prāṇaiḥ saha vinadya ca \| chinnastanī tu sahasā papāta śakunī
> bhuvi \|\|*

The Bhāgavata does something the earlier story does not. It refuses to leave Pūtanā as only an assassin.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.6.35 (Bryant translation):

> Pūtanā, the devourer of people’s children, was a demoness whose
> sustenance consisted of blood. But she offered her breast to Hari and
> so, despite intending to kill him, she attained sadgati, the
> destination of saints.

The Bhāgavata refuses to stop at “she came to murder him.” Pūtanā’s intention is poison, but the gesture is nursing. The book lets the gesture win. She put Kṛṣṇa to her breast, however falsely, and that imitation of motherhood is enough for salvation.

After her come the stories everybody already knows: the cart, the whirlwind, Kāliya, the butter, the mortar, Govardhana, the universe in Kṛṣṇa’s mouth.

And then the book says plainly who got those years, and who did not.

His mother and father did not fulfil their desire of experiencing the wonderful childhood activities of Kṛṣṇa. These can eradicate the sins of the world. Even to this day, sages sing of them.

All of it belonged to Yaśodā. A king listening to the story ages later asks the obvious question about her, and it is the question this whole festival turns on.

O supreme brahman, what did Nanda do, to obtain such great fortune? And what did the greatly fortunate Yaśodā do, that Hari drank from her breast?

The birth was all Devakī and Vasudeva ever got of his childhood. Those years went to a woman across the river with no notion of what was asleep in her house. And when the grown man was asked to choose between his two sets of parents, he refused, in the kindest sentence in the argument: he is the real father and she is the de facto mother who nourishes and caresses like their own sons, other babes who are abandoned by their relatives, due to their incapacity to feed and protect them.

She saw his face for one hour one night, and then years went by. She was waiting eagerly for the day he came back, and the line about her there is the saddest in the book, because she is mourning a son who is alive and standing in front of her.

Amongst the wives of the citizens appeared Devakī, mourning for her son, whose lovely face she longed to behold even in the hour of his destruction.

Then the fight went the other way. The tyrant fell, the prison opened, and the oldest book watched the two faces in the doorway.

Devakī gazed at Kṛṣṇa, trembling and weeping tears of joy from her eyes, milk welling up in her breasts. Vasudeva’s eyes too were clouded with tears at the sight of Kṛṣṇa, and his love made him throw off his old age and become young again.

The milk comes years late. Vasudeva is young for one breath.

The throne is empty, and Kṛṣṇa refuses it. He puts Ugrasena back on it and reaches all the way back to Yayāti:

The curse of Yayāti has pronounced our race unworthy of dominion: but with me, for your servant, you may issue your orders to the gods. How should kings disobey them?

There is the curse again. A sentence meant to keep Yadu’s descendants off thrones becomes Kṛṣṇa’s reason not to take one.

One text even lets Yayāti soften it:

When the great god will take birth with his portion in your family, then your family will be purified.

Kṛṣṇa apologizes for the one thing he could not give them:

We could not, at any time, give you the pleasure of witnessing the infancy, childhood and boyhood of your sons. It behoves you, Oh father and mother, to forgive us.

Vasudeva answers with a sentence that almost gives the boy back to heaven:

Blinded by delusion, I thought you my son; and for you, who are beyond all fear, I dreaded the anger of Kaṃsa, and therefore did I take you in my terror to Gokula, where you have grown up; but I no longer claim you as mine own.

You are no son of mine.

Vasudeva has just withdrawn the word “son.” The Bhāgavata immediately puts it back. Kṛṣṇa draws Māyā over his parents so that knowing what he is will not keep them from loving him as theirs.

For a moment, theology gets out of the way. They have their child again.

Before leaving the story, one much older name is worth setting beside it. The Chāndogya Upaniṣad mentions a Kṛṣṇa Devakīputra: Kṛṣṇa, son of Devakī.

Chāndogya Upaniṣad 3.17.6 (Radhakrishnan translation):

> When Ghora Āṅgirasa, after having communicated this to Kṛṣṇa, the son
> of Devakī, he also said, as he had become free from desire, in the
> final hour one should take refuge in these three thoughts: thou art
> the indestructible, thou art the unshaken, thou art the very essence
> of life.
>
> *taddhaitad ghora āṅgirasaḥ kṛṣṇāya devakīputrāyoktvovāca apipāsa eva
> sa babhūva \| so ‘ntavelāyām etat trayaṃ pratipadyeta akṣitam asy
> acyutam asi prāṇasaṃśitam asīti \|\|*

Is this the Kṛṣṇa of the later birth stories? Scholars have argued both ways, and the verse cannot settle the question on its own. I would not use it as proof. What it gives us is something smaller and firmer: an early Kṛṣṇa already attached to the name Devakī.

A Mathurā relief creates a similar temptation. It shows a man in water with a seven-hooded nāga beside him and has often been read as Vasudeva carrying the infant Kṛṣṇa across the Yamunā. The identification and date are debated. If that reading is right, the crossing reached stone very early. If it is wrong, the relief should not be made to carry the argument.

Even the calendar refuses to settle neatly. The sources do not always place the boy and the girl on the same lunar date. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa splits the two nights inside a single speech:

Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.1 (Wilson translation):

> In the night of the eighth lunation of the dark half of the month
> Nabhas, in the season of the rains, I shall be born. You shall receive
> birth on the ninth. Impelled and aided by my power, Vasudeva shall
> bear me to the bed of Yaśodā and you to that of Devakī.

Kṛṣṇa on the eighth, the goddess on the ninth. Later ritual schools had to decide which civil night should carry the fast, and they followed different rules.

Padma Purāṇa, Uttara Khaṇḍa 31.62 to 67 (Deshpande translation):

> O king, what is the use of observing crores of vows if Aṣṭamī with
> Rohiṇī falls on a Wednesday or with the Moon. … That Aṣṭamī is
> acceptable on the ninth day, but not on that eighth day which is
> united with the seventh day.

The disagreement did not begin because later families forgot the calendar. It is already present in the sources.

After all these explanations, the Mahābhārata gives the shortest answer to the question that started the essay: why was Kṛṣṇa born?

Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva 57 (Debroy translation):

> Viṣṇu himself, worshipped by all the worlds, appeared in Devakī
> through Vasudeva, for the welfare of the world. This prime being, with
> infinite wealth and the lord and grandfather of all beings, took his
> birth in the lineage of the Andhakas and the Vṛṣṇis in order to
> increase righteousness in the world.
>
> *anugrahārthaṃ lokānāṃ viṣṇur lokanamaskṛtaḥ \| vasudevāt tu devakyāṃ
> prādurbhūto mahāyaśaḥ \|\| … puruṣaḥ sa vibhuḥ kartā
> sarvabhūtapitāmahaḥ \| dharmasaṃvardhanārthāya prajajñe ‘ndhakavṛṣṇiṣu
> \|\|*

The Bhāgavata is less restrained:

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 1.3.28 (Gita Press translation):

> All these, however, are either part manifestations or rays of the
> Supreme Person; while Sri Krsna is the Lord Himself.
>
> *ete cāṃśa-kalāḥ puṃsaḥ kṛṣṇas tu bhagavān svayam \| indrāri-vyākulaṃ
> lokaṃ mṛḍayanti yuge yuge \|\|*

That is its answer. Not simply an avatāra among avatāras, but Bhagavān himself.

And every Janmāṣṭamī the enormous story is reduced again to small things: a fast, a lamp, a cradle, a midnight bell. The Padma Purāṇa makes that reduction almost literal by putting the sky itself on the shrine:

Padma Purāṇa, Uttara Khaṇḍa 31.41 to 45 (Deshpande translation):

> He should fashion a golden image of Rohiṇī and a silver one of the
> Moon; the image of the Moon should be of the size of a thumb only, and
> that of Rohiṇī should be of the size of four fingers.

A gold Rohiṇī. A silver Moon. The whole sky small enough for a shrine.

Below it is the scene that refuses to become small: a father in floodwater, holding the child above him, trying to reach the far bank.
