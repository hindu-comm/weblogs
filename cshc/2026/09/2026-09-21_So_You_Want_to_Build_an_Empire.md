Imagine you are a commander of a Rashtrakuta army division, assisting **Rashtrakuta Govinda III (793-814 AD),** who is encamped at Alampur (in the Raichur Doab, on the banks of Tungabhadra, also known as Dakshina Kashi) in Nov 803 AD and is conducting operations to subdue the southern kings, especially the **Pallava Dantivarman of Kanchi (795-846 AD)**. He has come here in haste after residing at the court of Sarva at the foot of the Vindhyas at Sribhavana (modern Sarbhon) around Bharuch during the “chaturmasa” (rainy season).

He is in very good spirits, as he has just conquered the whole earth (the Indian subcontinent, the traditional **“Chakravarti-kshetra”**, the area extending from the Himalayas in the north to the sea in the south, and a thousand yojanas wide from east to west, as described by Kautilya) & has earned the epithet **“Jagattunga”**, the head of (kings), just as Meru is jagattunga (lofty on earth) of mountains.

You have fought and bled, marched thousands of kilometres, braced mud, grime, hunger, pain and all the perils of campaigning, but you feel proud, and more importantly, you see the end of the campaign approaching (it's been hard, but you won't tell a soul about this; you value your reputation more than comfort, thank you very much).

Your master's victory is especially sweet as his campaign against the hereditary enemies of the Deccan empires, the **Pallavas of Kanchi,** is going swimmingly & the 2 contenders of the “Chakravarti” title, the **Gurjar-Pratiharas of Kannauj** & **Palas of Bengal**, lie prostrate. His other enemies, the**Chalukyas of Vengi**(controlling the fertile Krishna-Godavari delta & a perennial thorn in the flesh of the Rashtrakuta Empire) & the**Gangas of Talakad**(traditional vassals but recently turned rebellious by supporting Govinda’s brother Stambha in the succession crisis) have also been defeated. All the other kings have submitted & to put the cherry on top, his son & future Emperor Amoghavarsa I is born during the stay at Sribhavana. He will wrap up his digvijay by 805, issue grants at Raameshvaram, accept the gift (re:ad tribute) of the Lanka king & see the Rashtrakuta power reach its zenith.

His northern expedition is the stuff of legends. The Pratihara Nagabhatta II, having defeated Dharampala & his vassal of Kannauj, Chakrāyudha, became**Uttarapatha-svamin**(Lord of Uttarapatha/Northern India, an epithet of Dharampala) and conquered many regions, Malwa being the most important, as it was the frontier between the Pratiharas and the Rashtrakutas. He became a threat & had to be humbled.

Govinda first secured his other frontiers and sent his brother Indra to conquer Lāta (Bharuch region) and attack Malwa, where he achieved total success by pinning down the Pratiharas' core territory and strongholds. Meanwhile, Govinda went straight for the jugular.

Moving via the **Vidarbha (Nagpur)-Dāhala (Jabalpur)-Rewa-Satna** route, he defeated the ruler of Chitrakoot & entered **Antarvedi** (Ganga-Yamuna doab, where Kannauj is situated). There he defeated Nagabhatta, who fled west towards the Rajputana; “in fear vanished, nobody knew whither, so that even in a dream he might not see battle”, boasts a Rashtrakuta inscription. Dharampala, seeing the Emperor's might, submitted. Sanjan plate inscription of Amoghavarsa boasts:

**^(“The water of the springs of the Himalaya mountains was drunk by whose horses and plunged into by whose elephants, the thunder was redoubled in (its) caverns by the turya musical instruments of (whose) ablutions, (and) to whom, the great one, those (kings) Dharma and Chakrayudha surrendered of themselves. He(Govinda) thus bore resemblance to the fame of Himalaya, and was consequently Kirti-Narayana.”)**

Govinda III etched his name into history; the command of the whole earth (Indian Subcontinent) lay at his feet. He could recreate the glory of Mauryas and rival the fame of the Guptas. He could stay at Kannauj, that glittering crown jewel, and proclaim himself**Ekrat, Tribhuvan-Chakravatin, Samrat, Samastabhuvanāsraya, Sārvabhauma**, epithets symbolising Primacy and emperorhood, and nobody would oppose. Nobody could. But at that critical juncture where greed (entirely justified) should have won, **he returned**(All your efforts, down the drain!).

This was not an inevitable choice. About 880 years ago, Aurangzeb, having destroyed the Bijapur and Golconda sultanates and the Maratha swarajya, couldn't stop. He stayed there, hell-bent on finishing the Swarajya and becoming Ekrat. We all know what happened to him and his dynasty. When the invincible juggernaut of your army makes you the master of the world, it is very easy to keep going and never stop, like Alexander of Macedon. So why, why did Govinda return? Does he not care about the lost lives of your fellow comrades-at-arms? All the hard work and suffering, for what?

It wasn't as if the prize wasn't worth the trouble. Kannauj, also calledAntarvedi or **Madhyadesa**, was the most important city in India at that time. It was the seat of **Harshavardhan**, the **“Sakalottarāpathanatha”**, whose shadow loomed large in the minds of all the kings of that time. It was also the land of sacred sites like Mathura, Ayodhya, Varanasi, Gaya, etc along with the holy Ganga. The famous**“Uttarapatha”**, the Grand Trunk Road, running from Balkh via Peshawar-Lahore-Bhatinda-Delhi-Mathura-Kannauj-Lucknow-Kaushambi-Varanasi-Patna-Bhagalpur to Tamralipti in Bengal, was the route that connected the subcontinent to the Silk Road.

From Harsha till 1200, Kannauj was THE place to capture. From Yashovarman to Lalitaditya Muktapida & from Govinda's own father **Dhruva Dhārāvarsha**, the “Kali-vallabha” to his Great-great-grandson **Indra lll** (914-29 AD) and his grandson **Krishna lll** (939-67 AD), the Various Pratihara and Pala Emperors (yes, emperors, you recognize the immensity of Govinda's achievement, he defeated his Peers, not some puny kings, and yet he returned!) like Mahipala, Nagabhatta and Dharampala etc, all tried to hold it.

Even later, during the 11th and 12th centuries, Kannauj was the geopolitical nerve-centre whose reputation as the capital of India prompted **Mahmud of Ghazni** to attack and sack it in 1018 AD. The successors and vassals of the Pratiharas, Palas, and Rashtrakutas, such as the Paramaras of Malwa, the Kalachuris of Tripuri, the Chandellas of Jejabhukti, the Gahadavalas of Kannauj, and the Chahamanas of Ajmer, struggled with one another and viewed Kannauj with the same regard as Delhi was viewed after 1200 AD. So, it was not a trivial thing to abandon the prize when it was literally in your lap. But Govinda did exactly that.

After returning via the same route, he defeated **Kosala, Kalinga, Malava, Dāhala,** etc. The Rewa-Jabalpur area is centrally located to do all this. Afterwards, he went to Sribhavana, where he spent the rainy season and, from there, created a viceroyalty in Lāta, called “Lātēsvara-mandala”, and appointed his brother Indra there, along with his nephew Karaka as military governor in Malwa, to act as a “door-bolt" to protect Malwa against the aggressive expansionist attacks of the Gurjara-Pratihāra rulers (they were defeated, not destroyed; why, that's the question). Govinda turned south.

He hastily went to Alampur to crush the southern kings, namely the**Pallavas**,**Cholas**,**Pandyas, Keralas,**and**Vengi Chalukyas,**whose king, Vijayaditya II, was made to sweep the floors of Govinda's camp. Honestly, at this point, after fighting for almost a decade, you are questioning the purpose of it all.

This paradoxical decision to abandon the most important prize is rooted in sound calculations that every ruler must make while expanding his domain. We will explore these factors in this series.

### Force, Power and Paradox.



It will come as a surprise to you, but rulers, either the pre-modern (we'll take a blanket 1800 AD dividing line; afterwards, new technology and the Industrial Revolution changed just about every calculation and fact that was true for millennia before) Kings/Lords/Emperors, or modern-day Prime Ministers/Presidents don’t give a hoot about you.

It's not a bad thing, rather inevitable. The rulers see **“population blocks, regions, nodes and networks”**. They want to exercise sovereignty and control over the territory and population; thus, they think big.

Now, if you are a pre-modern Indian ruler, trying to be a good little follower of tradition espoused by Kautilya in his Arthashastra to:

**“अलब्धलाभाथा, लब्धपररक्षणी, रक्षतववधनी, वृद्धस्य तीथषु प्रतपादनी च।”**

***which effectively says that, "The philosophy of the state is to*****acquire*****what is not possessed, to*****preserve*****what is acquired, to*****increase*****what is preserved, and to*****bestow*****what is increased on worthy recipients.”***

You'll try to acquire what is not yours first; that means snatching either common property or somebody's property. When we talk about states, we mean annexing territory. This usually happens by forcibly acquiring what you want, or in other words, WAR.

So, if you're a customer (of territory, or even better, of **POWER**), trying to get a bargain deal, the currency (**FORCE**) in your pocket is the Army and Treasury of your kingdom. You'll naturally want the biggest Bang for the buck (pun intended). That fundamentally means that you want to spend as little force to generate as much power.

Cantankerous strategic genius Edward Luttwak discusses force deployment and power generation. His argument, boiled down, is that “Political power is generated by the perceived capacity to use force (and to confer or withhold benefits), rather than by the actual application of force itself.” The more you use force, the less it converts into power, as power is “psychological” in nature, whereas force is physical. Therefore, you are more likely to submit to a king who has a big army and big treasury because you can imagine what will happen when he invades you, than to a king who has already invaded and is actively destroying your territory.

Then you can see the force he has brought with him, devise strategies to defeat the enemy, or evade him and make the invasion a logistical nightmare (we'll see how this is more effective than actually engaging in battle). If he has brought 20,000 troops with him, and 10,000 are deployed to guard the supply lines, man the garrisons, block routes, forage for supplies, etc., you know he has only 10,000 troops now. If you open a second front and harass his supply lines, foraging parties, garrisons, checkposts, etc., maybe he needs to employ another 4,000 to manage the situation. Now he has only 6,000 troops to catch you. It's a little less intimidating than a 20,000-troop juggernaut.

So force is a finite commodity (your currency) that diminishes with use, whereas power is potentially infinite and doesn't depreciate with use. When your 5 neighbours see that you have an army of 20,000, they all think that you're strong and thus entertain talks of cooperation or surrender. They don't think about the collective power of all 5 neighbours, only their individual relationships with you. **This allows you to generate 5× power from your force**, because of what Mancur Olson calls the “Collective action problem”: individuals failing to act in a coordinated method because coordinating is itself costly and uncertain, so individually rational actors default to separate, weaker responses even when a unified response would clearly serve them better. The moment your forces are deployed, your 4 remaining neighbours will KNOW that your other fronts are vulnerable and will act accordingly.

Therefore, it's Power you want. Because it is**the ability to bend other people's will to your whims,**or**to exercise sovereignty over others**, it can be achieved in various ways.

Sociologist Michael Mann identifies 4 types of power: I**deological, Military, Economic,** and **Political**. To establish power, that is to “influence other people's behaviour”, you have to employ all these.

All other forms of power are important, but everything in the end depends on how much force you can deploy to fulfil your objectives. As Mao said, "Political power grows out of the barrel of a gun”, military force is the ultimate trump card and the lynchpin around which everything is developed. Force is the necessary reserve that makes every other form of power credible, without needing to be the thing most frequently deployed. To employ this extremely expensive and finite currency to buy power is what matters when every other form of control shatters. Let's look at how a pre-modern king fulfils the first task, that is, acquiring a kingdom, usually through military force.

### Logistics, एक अंतहीन सड़क।



Do you remember that you're a commander of the Rashtrakuta army? Now you have to take an army of 40,000 (a major force for large-scale campaigns) from the Antarvedi to Alampur via the Chitrakoot-Rewa-Jabalpur-Sarbhon route, and then most probably to Paithan (on the Godavari) and from there to Alampur, on the banks of the Tungabhadra. From there, Govinda's army went right up to Rameswaram. This route is almost 4,000 km long and passes through mountain ranges, plains, and river crossings.

The first priority is to ensure a steady and adequate supply of food. Adult men need anywhere from 2,000 to 3,200 calories per day to support their activity; soldiers marching under a heavy load will naturally tend toward the higher end of this range. This requirement is met primarily by a **staple grain** (e.g., rice) along with additional foods such as fruits, vegetables, and meat. Various armies throughout history have focused on ensuring a staple grain supply, with daily rations of around **0.8-1.36 kg**.

Kautilya assigns 800g (1 prastha) of rice, 200g of pulses, 50g of ghee, 10g of salt, plus vegetables, totalling 1 ling of staple meat and vegetables. According to Jonathan P. Roth, the Roman army was issued a daily ration of 1.1- 1.327 kg (of which 0.85kg was grain). Donald W. Engels takes the American Civil War ration of 1.36 kg for Alexander's army. Geoffrey Parker tells us that the Spanish army of Flanders was issued a minimum ration of 0.68 kg/day.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!AcDx!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffd0a8fe9-3961-482b-ae31-3a9f1e8f4c42_782x460.png)





Taking 1.25 kg/person/day as the standard, the daily food requirement of the 40,000-strong army is **50,000 kg** or **50 tonnes**. Our journey of about 4,000 km, covered at a marching speed of 20km/day (a very respectable number; usually such large armies managed 12-15 km/day), means 200 marching days, which is a staggering **10,000 tonnes**.

Now, a soldier can carry 30-50 kg of load by himself, but this includes his armour, weapons and other things which usually weigh about 20 kg. That means **he can realistically carry about 10 days' rations with him**; the maximum is 15 days. This assumes marching every day; if the army stops to fight o, to besiege, or because something has broken down, the reserves deplete quickly

This is a very simplistic calculation because an army never marches without a significant number of non-combatants, like carpenters (in Akbar’s time, according to official regulations in usage in the imperial stables, each group of 10 carts must be accompanied by a carpenter), merchants selling goods, men managing the animals, sweepers (to keep the camp, which was like a moving city clean), laborers, pioneers (for clearing and maintaining roads), prostitutes, pilgrims who attach themselves with the moving army for protection from the bandits, cobblers, tailors, servants and slaves of officers, nobles and kings (remember, the camp was also an administrative center or if the King was travelling, the capital itself) and other camp followers (people who wanted to find a job in the moving city, or those whose produce was destroyed or requisitioned and thus have no source of food) along with family members of soldiers and officers. **Usually there are about as many** **non-combatants as soldiers**. This means our food requirement has just doubled to 100 tonnes a day and 20,000 tonnes for the campaign, as there are now 80,000 people moving and we haven't even considered the animals.

And don't forget the cavalry (5-7 kg grain+dry and green fodder) and elephant (150-300 kg food/day) corps.

You can see how all this is a little problematic. It's almost as difficult as managing a joint-family trip or a big, fat Indian wedding. You must also take into account the lack of modern paved roads and the **campaigning season** (October-May, traditionally from Dussehra onwards till the arrival of Monsoon), which made long-distance travel very difficult; the tradition of Chaturmās, i.e., 4 months of monsoon when nobody does anything, and even monks take shelter)

This means you have a “**7-8 month window**” to mobilise, sally from your base, go into enemy territory, capture strategic nodes (forts, cities, river crossings, mountain passes, major routes, etc.), find the enemy, give battle, win, see that war booty is delivered; if you want to capture territory, then make arrangements and finally go back all the way.

Oh, and we haven't looked into water requirements (about **3 litres/day for a human, 25-40 litres for a horse, 30-70 litres for an ox, and 100-200 litres for an elephant**). And don't forget the fuel for cooking: firewood (about 30 tonnes/day to prepare 1 meal for 80,000 people).

I think it is now clear that carrying this much food, water and fodder is impossible for any serious journey. **You have to look for resources along your route**. As the march is divided between your territory (where you can establish mechanisms like supply depots, setting up markets, requisitioning in the form of taxes, etc.) and the enemy territory (hostile surroundings, so people will not part with their food and the enemy will actively try to deny you vital rations), you must carefully decide your route of advance. Because pre-modern agriculture was less productive and thus produced much less surplus, **you could choose only those routes with plenty of food, fodder, and water available**.

If the army is moving at 20km/day, then foraging parties (generally on horseback) can probably cover about 15 km on either side of the marching column (going, foraging, and returning in a single day, so they couldn't go far from the main body). This means they can cover about 600 square kilometres in a day. To **forage for rations (grain, about 1kg/person) only for humans (soldiers + camp followers)** means they must find 80 tonnes of food.

A general rule of thumb is that foraging parties find only 10-15% of total grain in an area (due to hostile territory reasons we discussed earlier). This means the area must contain about 800 tonnes of food. If we are marching right after Dussehra, when the harvest is done, and each person has their full-year supply stocked (usually 200 kg), this means the area must contain 4,000 civilians, or a population density of 6.67 people/km² (17 people/mile²).

If we add animal food requirements (let's take 40,000 horses and oxen; no, it is not too much; actually, it is a very conservative number), the total food requirement jumps to 300 tonnes (5.5 kg/animal/day = 220 tonnes + 80 tonnes of human food) and pushes the population density to **25 people/km² or 65 people/mile²**.

If we take Shireen Moosvi’s estimate of the population of India in 1601 (the underlying factors of production remained broadly the same, so the basics hold true even for our period) = 145 million people and about 100 million in Akbar's empire, we arrive at an average density of **33 people/km²** or about 85 people/mile². This figure contains wide fluctuations. Agra, Delhi, Lahore, Allahabad, and Awadh subahs account for 33.1 million, or 33%, of the total population. This reinforces the point that the army could move only in densely populated areas.

All these calculations assume that the army is moving daily at a constant speed of 20 km/day. If the army halts for even a day (it was necessary to rest the animals, particularly horses, for 1 day after 6-7 days of work; otherwise they become lame or unfit for work, or might die), the whole calculation doubles, and the pressure on the area suddenly becomes unsustainable. If the army is bogged down because of enemy action or decides to plunk down for a siege (which can last for weeks or even months), the supply situation becomes extremely problematic because, as the year passes, the total availability of food in an area also decreases, and before harvest, there might be no food available in an area.

That's why, if the enemy strategy is to just harass your supply lines and foraging parties, block the choke points, not engage in a battle and move here and there (practice manoeuvre warfare) so that you are forced to follow him deeper into enemy territory, and to hold the forts and river crossing, all aimed at prolonging the campaign, he can count on your army either mutinying or starving to death from disease or committing to an unfavourable (read: chosen by him) time and place for the battle and eventually getting destroyed. **Actually, he doesn't even need to give battle; you will lose in a Gandhian way, doing a fast unto death.**

So, given all these pesky complications, how do you solve the logistical constraints and actually go around conquering? We'll talk about the solutions next time. Till then, think about why Chandragupta Vikramaditya launched an invasion into Balkh and then destroyed the Saka Kshatrapas of Gujarat.
