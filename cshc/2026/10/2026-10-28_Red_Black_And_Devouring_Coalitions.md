The Persian region and its civilization have fascinated the world this year. This is primarily because Iran, with its decentralized military command and significant, capable, ideologically dedicated IRGC, is fighting for its very survival. The conflict is not simple, direct combat; it is asymmetrical warfare, something which Iran has been doing for decades but has become an object of world attention now. This article, however, examines Iran’s revolutionary history that brought it here, along with what the revolution teaches us.

The Iranian Revolution of 1979 brought an end to the rule of Mohammad Reza Pahlavi, allegedly his final statement before departing for Egypt, to the Prime Minister Bakhtiar being, ‘I give Iran into your care, yours and God’s’\[1\]. Mr. Bakhtiar would lose the care first, and then God would too, at the hands of the fundamentalist backlash of this Red and Black revolution. The 1979 Iranian Revolution was, in essence, a temporary alliance of incompatible actors: one end of the Alliance (Red Tudeh Communists) assuming they had real control and a say, and the other end actually holding the required power, united only by opposition to the Shah.\[2\] The post-revolution trajectory was characterized best by the systematic purge of Tudeh communists and the Mojahedin-e-Khalq (MEK), and the consolidation of velayat-e faqih\[3\]. This holds, and continues to hold, a lesson: the spectrum on the left remains ignorant of the obdurate belief in sustaining the world post-revolution built on the ‘Red-Black Alliance’. The Iranian revolution demonstrated the core mechanism of coalitions the left builds with fundamentalist units, where the shared negation collapses once ‘what comes after’ must be decided.\[4\]

To identify the source of this ignorance, we have to look at how left-wing movements define ideas about ‘what comes after’. As Roger Scruton argues, left-wing movements’ ideas consistently fail to provide a specific positive end-state. The elements and ideas of ‘what comes after’ are engulfed deep into the abstract depths of terms like ‘liberation’ from the projected ‘tyrant’ who is projecting ‘tyranny’ on the various ‘victim’ groups (one of any number of compatible victim groups). The ‘tyrant’ is drenched in the ‘blood’ of people, starving them of ‘equality’, when they actually imply equity. The region or nation has to be emancipated from the ‘tyrant’. However, these ideas of liberation, tyranny, victimhood, and equality rest on negating the existing order, arguing for anarchical movements, and none of them provides a tangible description of an alternative. This is idealistic, strategic, and virtually doomed, since specifying the alternative concretely would expose it to the same critique levelled at the status quo. This also allows the bastions of free will and ideas to align with elements, fundamentalists, who are diametrically and radically opposite to the elements they preach.

**Pre-1979 Coalition\[5\]**

The coalition of forces that acted in unison to ensure the fall of the Shah of Persia was varied in their political nature, something we could almost map to the present uncomfortable alliance that persists between political parties on the spectrum in India. The Reds, the Tudeh Party (*Hezb-e Tudeh-ye Iran)*, formed in 1941, with Soleiman Mirza Eskandari elected leader. The party initially aligned more with communism, but it quickly shifted toward the Soviet Union rather than Iran. In 1966, several party members, including Ali Khavari and Parviz Hekmatjoo of the Central Committee, and Asef Razmdideh and Sabre Mohammadzadeh, were arrested and sentenced to death. This sparked an international outcry and hunger strikes in Europe, which forced the government to reduce the sentences to life imprisonment. These events generated international sympathy for the struggle in Iran and helped unify the party after its previous splits. From this point on, the Tudeh Party became one of the strongest underground movements. Members of the Tudeh Party also maintained contact with Afghan Commando Forces personnel in the People’s Democratic Party of Afghanistan during the late 1960s and early 1970s, helping them distribute leftist ‘progressive’ magazines among the commando battalions. The next element of this alliance was the National Front (Secular Liberal). Before we discuss this alliance, we need to acknowledge the irony of having Khomeinist clergy and Seculars on the same side. The presence of proclaimed secularists and liberals allied with Khomeinist clergy may seem surprising to many of foreign origin; however, it is not uncommon in India. The example can be drawn from the long list of popular progressive intellectuals and their writings. It would be an immense disservice not to acknowledge the leading proponents of this class, who have authored ‘And His Life Should Become a Meaningful Story’ (2006) alongside commentary published after Afzal Guru’s execution in 2013, profiles and op-eds following the elimination of Hizbul Mujahideen commander Burhan Wani in 2016, as well as commentary on other terrorist figures in Jammu & Kashmir. All of this with a dash of spice: members of the Revolutionary Writers’ Association (Vishwa Viplava Rachayitala Sangham). My kind readers may forgive the lack of surprise in my prose at the formation of this alliance.

Coming back to the alliance. Founded in 1949 by Mohammad Mosaddegh, the National Front, Jebhe-ye Melli-ye Irân, was torn by strife between secular and religious elements before its consolidation. Over time, the National Front gradually emerged as the leading organization of secular liberals, with nationalist members adhering to liberal democracy and social democracy. The National Front was revived in late 1977 by Karim Sanjabi (former minister of education under Mossadegh and now the leader of the Front), Shapour Bakhtiar (former deputy minister of labor under Mosaddegh and now the leader of the Iran Party) and Dâryuš Foruhar (head of the Iran Nation Party). The three signed an open letter which politely criticized the Shah and called on him to re-establish the constitutional monarchy, free political prisoners, respect freedom of speech, and hold free and fair elections. Furthermore, after the violence in Qom, Sanjabi, as representative of the Front, came to Paris and emerged from his meeting with Khomeini with a short declaration that spoke of both Islam and democracy as basic principles, and committed the National Front to the twin goals of abolishing the monarchy and establishing a democratic and Islamic government in its place.\[6\]

**Bazaar**

Everything requires economic muscle to function. The ‘Bazaar’ built this muscle on the protein of money*.* Merchants had always been the traditional commercial backbone of Iranian cities. The merchants were a force to be reckoned with; with their guild-like organization, they were a community that included not only simple traders and merchants but also guild-organized artisans and moneylenders. This section maintained a centuries-old alliance with the region’s clergy. This alliance was older than any other, with clergy members often emerging from these *Bazaari* families. The functionality and sustenance of the mosques were intrinsically linked with the Bazar districts and their economy. The program Shah engaged in threatened the ‘*Bazaari’* economy regularly. Shah’s embrace of ‘reforms’ included state-backed department stores and import-substitution policies that favored large industrial conglomerates within the ‘crony-capitalist’ structure. All of this was piled on by the anti-profiteering campaigns in the mid-1970s, during which the Shah ensured the arrest of and fines for thousands of small shopkeepers for hoarding and overpricing\[7\]. This campaign alienated b*azaars* from the Shah and captured the shift toward a simple alliance to cement support for the clergy in the revolution. This stratum provided the alliance with finance and a nationwide organizational network that survived state repression because it was economic, not overtly political. This ensured the alliance had funded mosques for radicalization, strike funds to survive economic attack of the state and overall funding of the actual revolution itself.\[8\]

**Clergy - Apolitical to Militant**

Now, let us discuss the part of the alliance designed to take over post-revolution. Clerical positions had shifted over the decades; more recently, they leaned toward politics and limited the monarchy’s power over clerical rule. The clerical positions had moved firmly towards militant factionalism around Khomeini, who by the 1970s was arguing for direct clerical governance or ‘*Velayat - e - faqih’.* Khomeini rose to dominance with an uprising against the Shah’s reforms, which also led to his exile to Iraq. Earlier, Iran’s clergy were influenced by traditional Grand Ayatollahs like Abol-Ghasem Khoei and Mohammad Kazem Shariatmadari. They argued that the clergy should focus on jurisprudence, seminary education, and moral guidance while refraining from direct political governance until the return of the Hidden Imam. These proponents of a more apolitical approach were systematically curtailed, with leaders like Shariatmadari stripped of their titles and placed under house arrest and *‘Velayat - e -Faqih’* was written into the Constitution\[9\], effectively absorbing state administration and subordinating all other Shi’a traditions to the ruling doctrine\[10\]. Now, India’s Sunni Muslims (The Majority of Muslims in India) are also divided across distinct Sunni *‘maslaks’* (doctrinal orientations). Be it Deobandi, Barelvi, or Ahl-e-Hadith, all are at present highly atomized, engaging in localized bargaining with secular or regional political parties. However, each has a radical manifestation, with only something like the lack of a Khomeinistic leader.

**Shariati, Mujahideen and Discursive Bridge - Made In Paris, Bloomed in Tehran\[11\]**

With the alliance’s composition laid bare, let us look at the individuals within it. Ali Shariati, born in 1933, was a sociologist trained in Paris. This individual was exposed extensively to the ideas of Frantz Fanon’s anti-colonial theory and existentialism of the left. He returned to Iran to lecture at Husseinieh-e Ershad, Tehran. He began lecturing in 1967, re-reading Shi’aism’s foundational tales as active revolutionary resistance against oppression. He spoke of Imam Hussein’s martyrdom at Karbala to inspire a spirit of revolution rather than mourning. He called the earlier reading of this tale ‘Black Shi’aism’ for its quietist, ritualistic, clergy-controlled version and his revolutionary ideal as ‘Red Shi’aism’\[12\]. He focused on layering Fanon’s or Marxist vocabulary onto Islamic terms in the form of terms such as ‘*Mostazafin* ’*,* a layering of Fanon’s ‘Wretched of the Earth ’ and ‘*Nezam-e-Towhid* ’, a monotheistic order for a classless society. His lectures were essential in building the connecting wire between Tudeh and the mosque, as the clergy in this section of youth he appealed to found Marxism too foreign and atheistic, with traditional clergy too passive. As Khomeini’s order moved toward the red with ideals of militancy, Ali Shariati took a step toward the clergy for the reds. His death in 1977, believed by his followers to be an act of the Shah’s secret SAVAK(Bureau for Intelligence and Security of the State), meant he became a martyr around whom the revolution could build momentum; his absence allowed his followers to build around him without answering the question about the coalition’s end game. The idealist sociological frameworks Ali had built provided the intellectual defence and rallying cry that slowly co-opted the educated class into the alliance of devouring alliance.

There was another moment building the ‘discursive bridge’ independently of Ali Shariati: ‘*Mujahedin-e Khalq (MEK)’\[14\]*. Recent graduates of Tehran University founded it, and they were former members of a religious nationalist party called the ‘Liberation Movement’ under Mehdi Bazargan. These members were all from clerical or bazaari families. Their first major theoretical text, ‘*Nahzat-e-Husseini’* (Hussein’s movement), argued that the monotheistic order the prophet sought was a classless society and that Imam Hussein’s uprising targeted feudal landlords as well as illegitimate religious authority. This meant that Shariati and the Mujahedin independently and simultaneously arrived at nearly identical Marxist-Islamic syntheses. This bridge was the movement’s structural pull, allowing ‘pious’, educated Iranians to find a vocabulary that let them be both religious and revolutionary. The Mujaheedin operated with violence, with one of its first instances being the 1971 bombing of Tehran’s electrical grid during the Shah’s 2500 year monarchy celebration, assassinating a U.S. military advisor and the Tehran police chief. This was the more armed, more capable wing of the devouring Red-Black coalition. They did lose their founding leadership to executions by the mid-1970s, yet remained active.

**Ambiguity to Capture\[15\]**

The key feature of the clergy during their march towards institutional capture and sustaining their alliance with the Red was ambiguity. During his exile, Khomeini spent almost fifteen years avoiding topics of land reform, the scope of future clerical power, and women’s legal status or rights. He focused his campaign against the Shah primarily by highlighting grievances shared by members of the Red-Black Alliance. This took the form of highlighting the foreign oil interest, the Israel relationship, arms spending, inequality, press and party suppression and violation of the existing constitution. His imprecision and vagueness about the post-revolution goal were a measured strategy. His private seminary lectures provided proof that this vagueness was a strategy. These lectures were published in 1970 as ‘*Velayat-e-Faqih: Hukumat-e-Islami ’.*He laid out a doctrine, with a detailed plan for how clerical jurists alone should hold state authority.

Khomeini ensured that not a word of his doctrine was preached outside the circles that most desired it, among the ‘pious’ followers. He created and circulated the doctrine of how power would exist, be sustained, and move among his followers years before the alliance existed, and kept it out of the alliance’s sight. The end goal was clear to everyone, but the revolutionary Red section of the alliance, the tale of their devouring, was written even before they became part of it.

**The Hug and Choke**

In late 1978, September of 1978 to be precise, 47 years ago, troops fired on protestors in Tehran’s Jaleh Square. Khomeini took the opportunity to speak to the ‘People of Iran ’ and warned them that the regime of the Shah was against the people, not just the clergy. He spoke of secular ‘allies’ as fellow victims, bringing into the alliance the intelligentsia and ‘honest politicians’ of Iran. A month later, in November, he told the press conference that the future government would be ‘Democratic’ and ‘Islamic’ and solicited help from ‘all organizations’. In December of the same year, he declared that in an Islamic society, women would vote and have the same rights as men. Finally, in January, he proclaimed that a ‘freely elected constituent assembly’ would draft the future constitution, even though he had already decided what it would be: *‘Velayat-e-Faqih: Huqumat-e-Islami’.\[16\]*

Shah refused to negotiate with secular opposition, and by December 1978 revolutionary momentum had become too large for any politician to attach themselves to the regime. This, coupled with Khomeini’s reassurances stated above, created a sense that a theocracy wouldn’t replace the autocracy, and Khomeini also leveraged Shariati’s posthumous popularity among educated youth. Shariati was never named, letting Shariati’s own followers assume Khomeini agreed with their revolutionary reading of Islam, despite him being silent on the subject the entire time. The ‘Red-Black’ coalition wasn’t a case of mutual misunderstanding, but of one partner running a communications strategy the other partner wasn’t positioned to see.

**Who Fought The Battle in 1979\[17\]**

The Tudeh central committee was weakened, almost beyond recovery in terms of their street strength. This happened because, after the 1953 coup, the Tudeh was hit far harder than other opposition groups, with forty Tudeh militants executed and fourteen tortured to death, versus the National Front’s leadership mostly receiving five-year sentences with early amnesty. Shah’s SAVAK kept targeting Tudeh members even decades later, keeping the cost of Tudeh association visibly high. By the late 1950s, the party was, by its own historians’ account, a shadow of its 1940s mass-movement self. The actual insurrection of 9^(th) to 11^(th) February\[18\], 1979, that included assaults on police armories, Evin prison and army garrisons, was carried out by the Mujahedin, the Feda’i (Marxist guerrillas), whatever was left of Tudeh members, along with military defectors, and thousands of armed civilian volunteers. All of these networks were, in actuality, filling the gap in organisational and armed capacity that the Tudeh hadn’t maintained. The devouring of Red was written even before the revolution had ended. The disciplined, armed factions had already shown superior organisational capacity in the final days of the revolution, before any purge began.

**The Devouring**

Between 1979 and 1980, Khomeini was tolerant of Tudeh; however, a year later, their leadership would be arrested, forced on television to confess, and promptly executed. The MEK, excluded from power, launched armed resistance, leading again to the mass execution of its members. Finally, in 1983, the systematic eradication of both was complete.\[19\]

Coalitions built only on agreement about what to remove have low coordination costs, likely borne by the secular partners, as in Iran’s case. The coalition partner here agreed on what they wanted to replace: the Shah. None, except the clergy, had planned what they wanted post-revolution. There was no negotiated agreement on what came after; the grievance vocabulary was portable and not fixed. Words like ‘liberation’, ‘oppression’, ‘the dispossessed ’ carry no inherent metaphysical content. Shariati’s *‘Mostazafin’* was co-opted Shariati’s ‘Mostazafin’; most alliance partners used the same words with different meanings, letting incompatible actors believe they agreed. Actors with mobilization infrastructure, i.e., clergy with mosque networks, bazaar funding, and armed organization, could convert state control once the target regime fell; narrative legitimacy producers on the Red end could not. Khomeini used the Red’s words to seize power and ensured no Red was left to use them against him.

**Parallel Structures in India**

Now, when we look at the examples discussed later, we have to understand and state it plainly: the demographics and size of the Indian population today make it difficult for such an alliance to rise to power. Difficult does not inherently mean it is impossible, improbable or unlikely.

The difficulty lies only in the current lack of power due to the population mismatch. The key ingredients, a common adversary, allow incompatible actors to align. Furthermore, the Subaltern or post-colonial academic vocabulary present in abundance provides the same kind of language as Shariati’s synthesis. The ‘structural violence’, ‘epistemic injustice’, ‘majoritarian hegemony ’, filled with Dalit rights, feminist or minority personal rights, easily creates a booklet of injustice.

The oldest case and the case in India can be inferred as the most tragic one, primarily due to the way the events panned out for the ‘Jai Bhim’ end of the alliance. The recent ‘Jai Bhim-Jai Meem’ alliance has origins much older than most would know, and most in the alliance would rather not admit it. Jogendra Nath Mandal was a Namasudra Leader from Bengal whose association with the Muslim League was based on fighting landlords and moneylenders, finding common cause with poor Bengali Muslims. The logic of shared grievance was coherent with both groups being ‘discriminated against’ and the INC unable to reasonably resolve the grievance with immediate effect; Mandal’s support and camaraderie were rewarded with aiding Ambedkar’s move into the Constituent Assembly, and he himself gained a position in Jinnah’s cabinet.\[20\]

The shared negation was ‘*Savarna-Hindu Congress rule,’* and the Muslim League’s public vocabulary of protecting minorities gave Mandal what he needed to hear. As in Iran’s case, what came after was never negotiated in specific terms. Pakistan’s famous newspaper Dawn’s report of the March 1949 debate talks about how a Dalit member moved to amend the Objectives Resolution so that it named Scheduled Castes among the minorities to be safeguarded. Abdur Rab Nishtar defended the original wording, saying specificity wasn’t needed because it would cover any marginalized community. The amendment was rejected.\[21\]

For the man who had actively pointed out that the partition anti-Hindu riots were purely political, the breaking point arrived when, during the Khulna riots of February 1950, his own Namasudra community was brutally attacked by Muslim mobs, often with the complicity of the police. Mandal came back to India, was criticized and reviled as a ‘Pakistani,’ and spent his remaining years rehabilitating the mostly Namasudra refugees arriving in West Bengal. In India, both his attempts at elections ended in a loss, and he died suddenly in October 1968 while gearing up for a third electoral attempt with the Republican Party of India.

The alliance, however, can sometimes be built without the presence of the traditional Black ally of Islamists, mutating and metastasizing without them into a violent revolutionary alliance against a target group, whom they deem as the ultimate harbinger of all evil. The Indian version of this violent ‘revolutionary’ mutated version of alliance was the Dalit Panther and Naxalite one. The Dalit Panthers were formed on July 9, 1972, by Namdeo Dhasal, J. V. Pawar and Raja Dhale, inspired by the Black Panther movement\[22\]. The primary item from the booklet of injustice that Dalit Panthers adopted was caste atrocity, which they said existed despite Article 17 and other laws concerning preventive and affirmative actions. The Naxal movement, by contrast, originated around 1967 over sharecroppers’ rights in Naxalbari. D. N. Dhanagare’s overview notes that it later spread across many states, mobilising Dalits, bonded labourers and agricultural workers ([abstract](https://serialsjournals.com/abstract/94199_1.pdf)). The ideological convergence of the Dalit Panthers and the Naxalite movement was explicitly synthesized and articulated in the 1973 Dalit Panthers Manifesto, drafted largely under the influence of poet-activist Namdeo Dhasal. The manifesto expanded the definition of ‘Dalit’ beyond an exclusive caste label to encompass landless agricultural laborers, exploited workers, and all oppressed masses, directly mirroring the socio-economic revolution sought by Naxalite groups. Both movements discarded reliance on parliamentary reform and state enforcement. They viewed constitutional provisions like Article 17 (the abolition of ‘untouchability’) as toothless without radical land redistribution and militant self-defence against landlord cartels (despite a total alliance). By 1977, ideological friction between Dhasal’s Marxist-oriented faction and Dhale and Pawar’s Ambedkarite identity-focused faction led to a permanent rupture, offering a critical case study in the challenges of how identity politics and class politics can diverge when institutional power or strategic priorities clash.\[23\]

In India, the Muslim clerical hierarchy is incomparable to the scale of consolidation Khomeini achieved; however, it was achieved and is replicable. The current clerical hierarchy is fragmented across Deobandi, Barelvi, Ahl-e-Hadith, Shia, and regional political lines (AIMIM, Jamaat-e-Islami-Hind, state-level parties), with no single actor positioned to absorb the others as Khomeini’s faction eventually did. However, each is a force to reckon with wherever its population can gain a foothold. They become the default choice when numbers outweigh differences and people vote en masse. A prime example is the long-running victory in the metropolitan city of Hyderabad. Despite changes in the ruling party in the state, one party has consistently won because of sheer population asymmetry. Khomeini had a specific constitutional theory of clerical rule worked out and written down years before 1979, something to rally his followers towards, a clear end goal. India had its own versions too: *‘Hukumat-i-Ilahi ’,* authored by Sayyid Abul A’la Maududi, who was born in Aurangabad, Maharashtra, and founded Jamaat-e-Islami in Pathankot, Punjab, in 1941. He argued that human legislation is invalid and that political authority belongs solely to God, to be interpreted and administered by righteous Islamic scholars. Both insisted that Sharia must be the supreme law of government and that jurists/scholars must guide political authority. In fact, Khomeini’s early writings drew on translated works by Maududi. Dar-ul-Qaza Network, under the All India Muslim Personal Law Board, was established to ‘safeguard’ Muslim personal law from State interference. Through this system, trained Qazis (Islamic jurists) issue binding moral and religious verdicts called ‘*Fatawa’* and settle civil disputes for Muslims who voluntarily submit to their jurisdiction, maintaining juristic authority over community affairs alongside the secular Indian legal system. So the structures for a potential end goal already exist. The caveat, as previously discussed, is the population for now, and that is where the alliance comes in.

As with Iran’s Red-Black alliance, numerous alliance options exist; the obvious Red is the left, or the Red of traditionally landowning castes. There are also Blue, catering to the traditionally ‘marginalized’. India’s federal, multiparty system may look like a fundamentally different opportunity landscape than a unitary monarchy with a single institutional choke point, but the common-adversary dynamics still play out the same way. A common adversary exists, and it is colored saffron. The mechanism of enemy-of-enemy coalition formation, portable grievance vocabulary, and infrastructure-vs-narrative asymmetry is already at work, though still some distance from absolute clerical capture. The call of *‘Gazwa-e-Hind ’* captures the potential target already mapped, so the mechanism, end-goal, and alliance are all present, with population demography and population spread as the only things preventing their tryst with destiny. And this demography is changing. The example of this is Assam, where the number of Muslim-majority districts rose from six (2001) to nine (2011) out of Assam’s total districts, concentrated in the Bangladesh border belt (Dhubri, Goalpara, Barpeta, Karimganj, Hailakandi, etc.) with some districts showing Muslim population shares above 70–95%.

The question now remains whether the ‘secular’ Indian civil-society partner retains independent institutional standing in its courts, its press and its party structure or gets absorbed gradually with its changing population and when- not if any Islamic section can produce and move towards a unifying capture.

**Tale of Two Civilizations**

The lesson of Jogendranath Mandal and the Dalit Panthers from our history, and the lesson from 1979’s Iran along with all others, has one output for us. The arithmetic of a coalition built on negation, on the common-enemy dynamic, is that partners in the Red-Black Alliance may agree overtly to tear down; but what follows is often expressed in language that is, at best, ambiguous, covert in doctrine, and masked under layers that lie hidden until the time is ripe.

One partner spends years discussing in covert doctrines and teachings, working out the logistics of organization and how to fund them when the common enemy is torn down. That part has also spent time building this machinery of takeover while the other reveled in its sharp arguments. That very partner who built the machinery, in most cases, the Black of fundamentalism, has the mechanism of succession, and their machinery beats vocabulary every time the State actually falls. The faction equipped with superior institutional infrastructure, ideological discipline, and grassroots financial networks inevitably fills the vacuum, consolidating power while dismantling the partner that failed to prepare for the day after victory.

Shariati’s poetry, Tudeh’s street fighters, the Mujahedin’s weapons—all fought in the revolution with zeal, and none got a seat at the table. This mechanism of Black deserves attention and recall, not any inherent treachery of clerics or naivety of the left. Wherever ideologically incompatible actors unite only around a shared adversary, the question of ‘what comes after’ is answered by whichever partner showed up with an institution instead of a slogan: the clergy. India’s political landscape has no shortage of enemy-of-enemy alignments and borrowed grievance vocabularies, and it would be complacent to assume that India’s federal structure, its multiplicity of competing power centres, and the absence of any single institution positioned to absorb the rest would make the Iranian outcome only a warning to study, not a prophecy.
