## **Abstract**



> Much has been written about Lee Kuan Yew. The body of literature
> produced from his speech notes suggests that the art of his statecraft
> is vast enough to fill an entire library. Yet, despite the diversity
> of perspectives on him —whether sympathetic or critical of his body of
> work—a remarkable consensus persists among world leaders: he is almost
> invariably cited as an archetype of the hard-headed pragmatist whose
> political imagination was shaped less by an uncompromising concern for
> survival. He has a Machiavellian understanding of the political nature
> of the world, as evidenced by his rejection of any popular decision;
> if I may say, his foremost responsibility was to secure the survival
> and stability of the polity, even if that meant being feared. A
> “foster state,” as many said, to which he added, *“If Singapore is a
> nanny state, then I am proud to have fostered one.”* If the dominant
> portrayal of him is accepted at face value, then any attempt to
> uncover an idealistic dimension within Lee’s political thought appears
> almost paradoxical. It seems like a search destined to fail. Yet it’s
> the assumption I scrutinise most closely. Lee’s memoir is less
> interesting as a political autobiography than as a catalogue of
> recurring governing principles. In it, every chapter begins with a
> recall of a historical episode and an explanation of the constraints
> and dilemmas surrounding it, then offers his own reflections on why
> certain choices were made before connecting them to broader political
> lessons from the experience. The episodes that follow are therefore
> treated as variations on a common problem rather than as disconnected
> moments in Singapore’s history.

## Structure of the Book



The book “From Third World to First: The Singapore Story: 1965-2000” mixes Lee’s notes with his commentary, including information about Singapore and its relationships with other countries, but it is not presented as a single, cohesive view. Lee goes through each country one by one; he merges his notes, taken before and after the meetings, then creates a narrative about them—explaining the dynamics of Indonesia, Thailand, Brunei, Vietnam, and Cambodia, as well as ASEAN’s evolution. It is rather shocking that this kind of history is largely buried and less known today by the dominant IR students, where Russia was supporting Vietnam, and the latter was seen as an aggressor of communism, only to be stopped by China. The chapters provide precise details, names, and a narrative of Lee’s days and what they were like while dealing with these countries; at times, it can feel too dense and boring, but he always ends up with lessons. One must observe a pattern running underneath his foreign policy. It was strategically far more systematic than we could read. Three objectives recur throughout these chapters: prevent any single power from dominating the region, embed Singapore inside overlapping networks of cooperation, be backed by a superpower umbrella and let economic interdependence do the work of political stability. We must remember he envisioned Singapore as a force of modernity.

# **The Crucible of History (1299–1965)**



While the book does not detail Singapore’s history, elements of it are scattered throughout. Presenting it linearly will build the argument more effectively.

Singapore’s origin story has two beginnings. The first is 1299, when the Malay Annals record a kingdom at the mouth of the strait already embedded in the Indo-Sanskrit world of kingship and maritime trade. The second is 1819, when Stamford Raffles planted a British flag and turned the island into a trading post. The British built Singapore into one of the world’s busiest entrepôts by doing very little except getting out of the way. Free port status, a strategic location at the junction of global shipping routes and a willingness to let commerce organise itself turned a mangrove swamp into a city. Prosperity was dependent on the colonial masters. Singapore’s economy wasn’t a self-contained system that could flourish on its own; it was seen as a node in a network that required it to function.

Japan’s seven-day war with British forces dismantled this network in 1942 during World War II. Koreans accompanied them in Japanese uniforms; they were one of two groups of auxiliaries the Japanese brought to Singapore, the other being Taiwanese. Koreans were distinguishable as being of Mongolian stock, with facial and physical features that set them apart from the Japanese or the Chinese. Singapore was renamed Shonan-to, meaning “Light of the South Island”, and was also included as part of the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere. The lesson for the natives was that the British, who had sold themselves to the local population as natural rulers and permanent protectors, surrendered the island in one of the most complete military collapses. The myth of British invulnerability broke, and so did the chains of trust that had prevented a nationalist uprising. The population that had organised its political imagination around colonial permanence now watched its rulers march into captivity. Japan introduced a currency called banana money; it was paper backed by nothing.

The trade networks that had made the island prosperous were of no use to the locals, but rather to a global order that the war shattered. The Allies (the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, the United States, and China) blockaded one of the world’s busiest ports, turning it into an isolated garrison serving someone else’s war. When Japan surrendered in August 1945, the island entered a period that colonial histories wouldn’t hesitate to punch into a paragraph. Looting, revenge killings, food shortages, disease and crime. The British returned in September under Mountbatten to accept the formal surrender of the Japanese forces, and the old administration resumed. Still, everyone present understood it was no longer the same administration, as the confidence to rule the locals in British style was gone. But as it was a trading site, recovery was bound to happen, with tin and rubber here the unexpected heroes of Singapore’s Boom. Global demand pulled the economy upward despite ongoing infrastructure damage and labour strikes, driven by several factors that already signalled the political energies building beneath the colonial surface. The British, reading the room accurately if belatedly, began dismantling the institutional architecture of direct rule. They decided to dissolve the Straits Settlements and grant Singapore the status of a separate Crown Colony, masking this with limited elections in 1948. However, the franchise remained narrow enough to keep participation performative.

David Marshall, Singapore’s first chief minister from April 1955 to June 1956, pushed for full self-government, and when the British refused, he resigned—an honest response to an impossible position. His successor, Lim Yew Hock, negotiated more carefully with the British and extracted more by earning Britain’s trust; they were granted internal self-government in 1959 while retaining defence and foreign affairs—a standard colonial formula for decolonisation that kept the strategic assets while offloading the administrative costs. The general election under universal suffrage produced a decisive PAP victory, and Lee Kuan Yew became Prime Minister of a city that was technically self-governing but still economically fragile, still within a British defence perimeter, and still uncertain about what exactly it was and to whom it belonged. The answer to those questions would take another six years and a separation no one planned for.

# **The Survival Problem**



Economically, Singapore’s prospects appeared bleak. The newly independent republic had a population of barely 1.9 million people and a per capita income of less than US\$500–600, placing it firmly within the developing world. Unemployment hovered between 10 and 14 per cent, with approximately 200,000 people either unemployed or underemployed. Manufacturing remained underdeveloped and accounted for only a small proportion of national output. Singapore possessed neither a significant domestic market nor natural resources that could provide the foundations for industrialisation. Its economic dependence on external actors further deepened these vulnerabilities. The British military presence constituted one of the principal pillars of the economy. British bases directly and indirectly supported nearly 70,000 jobs and contributed approximately one-fifth of Singapore’s GDP. Any reduction in British military expenditure therefore threatened severe economic dislocation and mass unemployment. The situation became even more precarious when Britain announced its intention to withdraw from the East of Suez.

Singapore also lacked the basic conditions usually associated with viable statehood. It imported nearly all its food, fuel and raw materials and remained dependent upon Malaysia for water supplies. Lee repeatedly referred to Singapore as a country with “no hinterland.” Economic survival, therefore, depended entirely on maintaining access to international trade and external markets. As Henry Kissinger later observed, Singapore appeared to many contemporaries as a state “located on a sandbar with nary a natural resource” and seemed destined to become a client state of larger neighbours, if indeed it could preserve its independence at all. The domestic social situation was equally troubling. Much of Singapore still resembled a colonial trading outpost rather than a modern state. A significant proportion of the population lived in overcrowded housing or squatter settlements lacking adequate sanitation and infrastructure. Communal tensions remained acute following the racial riots of 1964, while communist networks retained considerable organisational influence among students, trade unions and Chinese-speaking workers.

Singapore’s independence in August 1965 was less an achievement than an unexpected crisis. Unlike many post-colonial states that fought for sovereignty, Singapore achieved independence through its expulsion from Malaysia. Lee himself admitted that separation was not the PAP leadership’s original objective. Their political project had been a merger with Malaya, which they considered economically and strategically indispensable. Independence, therefore, presented Singapore with a series of problems for which neither its leaders nor its institutions were fully prepared. The central question was not how Singapore would prosper, but whether it could survive at all.

The structural circumstances appeared profoundly unfavourable. As Henry Kissinger later observed, Singapore was “located on a sandbar with nary a natural resource”. It seemed “destined to become a client state of more powerful neighbours, if indeed it could preserve its independence at all.” Singapore possessed no mineral resources, no agricultural hinterland, and no domestic market of meaningful size. Its economy had developed as an entrepôt of the British Empire and remained heavily dependent upon external trade and imperial military expenditure.

More fundamentally, Singapore had never been intended to become an independent nation-state. As Kissinger notes:

> ***“As the main British naval base in the Far East, it had neither
> prospect nor aspiration for nationhood until the collapse of European
> power in the aftermath of the Second World War, which redrew the
> political map of Southeast Asia.”***

The island’s economic viability had long depended upon integration with a wider regional economy. Separation therefore immediately raised fears regarding access to markets, labour, water and trade routes. Singapore also faced an extraordinarily difficult geopolitical environment. To the north lay Malaysia. To the south stood Indonesia, which until recently had pursued Konfrontasi and sponsored acts of sabotage against Singapore. Lee repeatedly wrote that Singapore existed *“in the midst of larger, newly independent nations pursuing nationalistic policies.”* Many contemporaries regarded the island as too small and vulnerable to maintain genuine independence. The demographic situation added another layer of insecurity. Singapore’s population was overwhelmingly Chinese in a Malay-majority region. Communal riots in 1964 remained fresh in public memory, while questions of loyalty and identity remained unresolved. The new state had to forge a common civic identity among Chinese, Malays and Indians while simultaneously preventing communal politics from tearing the country apart. Security conditions were equally precarious. Singapore inherited almost no independent military capability. Lee later recalled that “We had to have a credible force to protect ourselves.”

The situation was particularly dangerous because the army and police remained overwhelmingly Malay. Lee openly worried whether these forces would remain loyal to a government now perceived as predominantly Chinese:

> ***“The question was whether the army and police would be as loyal to
> a government no longer British or Malay, but one the Malays perceived
> as Chinese.”***

The possibility that hostile elements in Malaysia might exploit these divisions was not entirely implausible. Lee even feared that Singapore’s leadership could be overthrown before it had constructed its own defence institutions. Economically, the prospects were similarly bleak. British military expenditure constituted a major source of employment and economic activity, while unemployment remained high and industrialisation limited. Development economists widely believed that small states without resources or domestic markets had little chance of independent development. Lee and Goh Keng Swee therefore faced the challenge of creating livelihoods for nearly two million people in a country that appeared to lack the conventional foundations of economic success. Singaporean statecraft has its distinctive character. Survival became the organising principle around which policy was constructed. Lee later summarised this ethos succinctly:

> *“We had one simple guiding principle for survival, that Singapore had
> to be more rugged, better organised, and more efficient than others in
> the region.”*

The developmental state, the emphasis upon meritocracy, military conscription, racial integration policies, economic openness and institutional discipline all emerged from this fundamental predicament. The problem confronting Singapore in 1965 was not merely one of development but one of existence itself. State-building was therefore inseparable from national survival. As Lee himself reflected, his generation’s experiences were shaped by “the difficulties Singapore faced then in its struggle to survive.” Singapore’s subsequent success cannot be understood without recognising the depth of insecurity surrounding its birth. The republic began its existence not with confidence but with a pervasive sense of vulnerability, uncertainty and impermanence. The question that haunted its leaders was simple: could this accidental state endure in a hostile world? Lee Kuan Yew’s political project may be understood as an answer to that question.

# **The Roots of The Great**



The man who inherited this fractured, vulnerable island in 1959 was shaped by forces just as complex as the nation itself. Lee Kuan Yew’s pragmatism, which many might read as shaped by the political landscape, was in fact a byproduct of his family and home. He was born on September 16, 1923, into an English-educated Peranakan Chinese family. This kind of person occupied an unusual position in colonial Singapore—neither fully of the coloniser’s world nor of the world being colonised, but fluent in both. His grandfather, Lee Hoon Leong, had westernised himself by trade and temperament, working on British ships and giving his grandson an English name, Harry, before Lee had any say in the matter. English became the language of the household, and with it came early, unforced exposure to the administrative culture of the Empire — its habits of order, its instinct for hierarchy, its confidence that systems, properly run, produce results. His mother ran the house by including him. She brought him into daily decisions the way other parents bring a child into a game, and the child who is asked to decide eventually becomes a decision-maker. Lee said as much himself: the qualities that mattered, he attributed to her. His father, he describes more sparingly:

> ***“My father was carefree by nature and early in my teens my mother
> had co-opted me as the substitute head of the family”***

Even his cousins, by his own account, treated him as older than his years, as though authority were something he wore before he had earned it. It is tempting, reading the standard accounts, to locate the source of Lee’s pragmatism in the crucible of governing— the riots, the merger with and expulsion from Malaysia, the resource-starved island with no business surviving on its own. But my reading of his memoir suggests otherwise. The instincts were already there before Singapore needed them. Cambridge and the Middle Temple gave him vocabulary and credentials. They did not give him the temperament. That came earlier, in a household that let him make decisions, and in a family position that taught him to move fluently between two worlds without fully belonging to either. He studied law because the British Empire ran on law, and Lee, even as a young man, understood which instruments carried power. He founded Lee & Lee not as a deviation from politics but as a rehearsal for it.

While his upbringing provided him with a pragmatic temperament and his legal training gave him his vocabulary, his time in Britain also exposed him to the prevailing ideological currents of his generation: anti-colonialism and socialism. Lee Kuan Yew did not arrive at his anti-communist stance directly, as we are made to believe. He first embraced socialism in his youth and later worked his way out of it.

At the London School of Economics, he studied under Harold Laski for a brief time. He absorbed a vision of equal opportunity and reduced wealth disparity that fit his generation’s temperament perfectly. Every anti-colonial student of that era treated socialism as the obvious vocabulary of liberation. It was not a fringe position for them, as it was a consensus among those about to inherit the post-colonial world. Lee admired Nehru specifically and saw secular, multiracial, democratic India as a model worth betting on against communist China.

He also accepted the Marxist diagnosis of exploitation. Ironically, he rejected the Leninist prescription for this vision and the bag of black-magic actions it carries. Intolerance, coercion, and violence to sustain its state structure were problematic. Now, whether it was its overt violence or what was too visibly deplorable to the public and the world, he disagreed with it to its core. I cannot make any certain remarks on it. What he retained from socialism was therefore its appreciation of organisation and collective purpose. Socialism, at its most persuasive, appeared to him less as a theory of redistribution and more as a method of mobilising societies toward common ends. It promised discipline, sacrifice for social solidarity and a capacity to subordinate narrow interests to national objectives. These were qualities that deeply impressed Lee, particularly in contrast to the factionalism and short-termism he increasingly associated with post-colonial democracies. Economically, he had deep disagreements with socialism as he watched post-colonial Africa run the socialist playbook and watch it fail. What he used from the socialist playbook was the instinct toward social cohesion.

# **Birth of PAP**



The People’s Action Party (PAP) was born in 1954 out of a coalition of very different groups united by a single immediate objective: ending British colonial rule. However, it was not initially a purely moderate party; rather, it was an uneasy alliance between competing factions at opposite ends of the spectrum.

1.  English-educated anti-colonial intellectuals and professionals (Lee
    Kuan Yew, Goh Keng Swee, Rajaratnam).

2.  Chinese-educated trade unionists and student leaders.

3.  Underground communist cadres and their sympathisers.

Lee needed the support of the Chinese-speaking masses and trade unions, largely influenced by communist China, to build a viable anti-colonial movement. The English-educated leaders and colonial government workers brought organisational ability and legal expertise, but little mass support for mobilisation. The leftists had the masses, unions and Chinese schools behind them. Thus, both sides entered into a tactical alliance.

As the influence of the party started growing, communists were becoming uncontrollable and needed to be disciplined, which led to the great split of 1961. This had many interlayered factors, but the immediate trigger was the Anson by-election in July 1961. PAP candidate Mahmud Awang lost to former Chief Minister David Marshall. Following the defeat, several left-wing PAP leaders publicly criticised Lee Kuan Yew’s leadership and demanded policy changes. This exposed deep divisions that had existed since the party’s founding. Lee increasingly believed that many of these leaders were linked to the underground Malayan Communist Party (MCP). He argued that while both factions wanted independence, the communists sought to use constitutional politics merely as a stepping stone towards revolutionary power.

The discipline came through expulsion. Lee pushed his leftist faction out of the party, and it reconstituted itself as Barisan Sosialis, the first of several moments in which ideology was traded away the instant it threatened power. Before Lee could manufacture cohesion among the populace, he first had to enforce it within his own political vehicle. In all this, the biggest substantive disagreement concerned the merger. Lee regarded the merger with the Federation of Malaya as both economically and politically essential to the island’s survival. The lack of resources and hinterland was eating away at Lee, leaving him sleepless. The left wing opposed the terms of the merger. They feared that the merger would strengthen anti-communist measures and weaken their political position, as it would create a common market and, importantly, allow communist organisations to be checked through the internal security arrangements shared with Kuala Lumpur.

They demanded full citizenship rights and different constitutional arrangements. All of this culminated in the split and expulsion of the PAP's left-wing faction.

This merger was indispensable for Singapore’s survival. To Lee, the merger with Malaysia was a question of political economy and ultimately a civilisational survival. The prosperity of the Island had always rested upon its role as an intermediary between the Malay Peninsula and global markets. On 27 May 1961, Tunku Abdul Rahman publicly proposed creating a new Malaysian federation incorporating Malaya, Singapore, Sarawak, North Borneo and Brunei. For Lee, this appeared to provide the political and economic framework Singapore needed for its future.

But for better or worse, it made Lee emotional on national television, followed by a short-term breakdown in his health and mental equilibrium when the merger with Malaysia didn’t happen and Singapore was expelled from the union. Lee Kuan Yew and the PAP’s advocacy of civic nationalism explain this action by Malays. They argued that citizenship, not ethnicity, should determine political rights. Singapore’s leaders repeatedly argued for a “Malaysian Malaysia”, where Chinese, Malays, Indians and others would possess equal political standing. This directly challenged the ideological foundations of post-colonial Malaya. For Lee, the constitution’s special provisions for Malays could perhaps be justified temporarily due to socio-economic backwardness. Still, he rejected the idea that political sovereignty should permanently belong to a single ethnic community. This is what Lee meant when he responded to UMNO (a Malay nationalist political party in Malaysia) leaders that Malaysia was agreed upon as a state for Malaysians, not Malay rule.

The betrayal which Singapore faced was pretty evident, while Malaysia might have thought it was over for this island; they did not expect this comeback. Upon closer reading, one can see that the seeds of the merger’s failure were ever-present. The Tunku and the Malay rulers had instructed the Reid Commission to ensure that all citizens of an independent Malaya would enjoy equal rights and opportunities irrespective of race or creed. At the same time, however, the Commission incorporated special provisions protecting Malay interests, including quotas in education and public employment and the recognition of Islam and the Malay language as foundational elements of the federation. These protections were expected to be phased out gradually. But as history has it, no enfranchised group will give up its special rights. So, leaders within UMNO increasingly interpreted these constitutional provisions as recognition of the historical and political primacy of the Malay-Muslim community. The constitutional position of Malays came to be viewed as an acknowledgement that Malaya was fundamentally the homeland of the Malays (*Tanah Melayu*) and the racial superiority of Malays over Chinese and Indians. Singapore’s entry into Malaysia intensified these fears. The incorporation of a Chinese-majority and economically dynamic Singapore raised concerns that Chinese economic power might eventually translate into political dominance. For many Malay leaders, constitutional protections under Articles 3 and 153 became essential mechanisms preventing the marginalisation of Malays within their own homeland. Since Malay identity was constitutionally tied to Islam, any challenge to Malay political primacy also acquired civilisational and religious dimensions.

The failure of this merger amounted to the disintegration of the economic order upon which Singapore had been built. Malaysia’s finance minister moved rapidly to redirect trade flows and restrict Singaporean access to the common market, even blocking ventures such as the Guinness investment, which was subsequently established in Kuala Lumpur instead. Singapore was losing not merely a trade agreement but the wider British-Malayan economic network that had sustained its entrepôt function for over a century. Winsemius expressed the danger in stark quantitative terms. Without restoring a common market with Malaysia and reopening trade with Indonesia following the confrontation, unemployment was projected to exceed 14% by the end of 1966. Malaysia was simultaneously attempting to establish direct trade routes that bypassed Singapore altogether. For Lee, separation in 1965 therefore produced a profound existential crisis. Singapore appeared to be an artificial city-state suddenly deprived of conditions that had made its existence possible. The island was walking a razor’s edge, facing economic isolation, mass unemployment and political vulnerability simultaneously.

The tragedy of Malaysia lay in the fact that both sides could legitimately claim fidelity to the constitutional settlement. The PAP emphasised the Reid Commission’s insistence on equal citizenship and non-discrimination. In contrast, UMNO emphasised the constitutional entrenchment of special rights for Malays and the primacy of Islam and Malay identity. The merger failed because these two principles, temporarily reconciled within the Constitution, became politically irreconcilable once questions of power, demographics, and electoral competition emerged. Singapore’s separation in 1965 therefore resolved a longstanding constitutional contradiction dating back to the founding of independent Malaya itself.

# **Riots and Violence**



The “great lie” of nation-building is the foundational myth that a state originates from a peaceful, voluntary social contract. In reality, political philosophers such as Charles Tilly argue that new nations are born from violence and coercion, relying on ideological myths to unify diverse populations. The fragile foundations on which Singapore was built were never going to make governing easy. The island emerged at a historical moment dominated by universalist ideological currents, chief among them communism, whose transnational appeal threatened to override older social, ethnic and political loyalties.

The year 1949 was particularly significant because it coincided with the Chinese Communist Party’s victory in China. The establishment of the People’s Republic of China inspired many Chinese-educated workers and students in Singapore, bolstering left-wing movements and intensifying labour militancy. Lee notes that the rise of communist China generated immense patriotic enthusiasm among sections of the Chinese-educated community and strengthened communist influence in local politics. The 1950s were a period of ambiguity and violence as Communist influence grew in trade unions and Chinese schools, producing major riots. These were the byproducts of Lee’s ban on the Singapore Chinese Middle School Students’ Union in 1956, which was closed due to its communist activities. The government also arrested four student leaders and expelled 142 students and 2 teachers. In this protest, students gathered and camped at Chung Cheng High School and the Chinese High School, where they sat, organised meetings, and held demonstrations. The government intervened and issued an ultimatum that the schools be vacated by 8 pm the following evening. These ideologically inflamed students, as the deadline approached, rioted at Chung Cheng High School and the unrest spread to other parts of the island. The state answered with its own monopoly on legitimate force and threw tear gas at them. Forced out of the schools, the students headed for the city to overturn cars, damage public infrastructure and throw stones at vehicles. Violence continued for 5 days, and 13 people were killed, and more than 100 were injured. There were many riots that I can list, but the point of the problem was that the communists’ growing influence was the danger the government moved to curb, and the act of curbing it was what produced the violence against the state. They mobilised students and union workers, both of whom are vulnerable groups.

One must take a glance at the merger: political and communal effects between Singapore and Malaysia increasingly became entangled with communal politics. Beneath disputes over taxation, representation and constitutional arrangements lay a deeper anxiety within sections of Malay nationalism. The fear was that Chinese economic strength and demographic weight might ultimately challenge Malay political primacy in their *Tanah Melayu*, the historical homeland of the Malays.

Lee regarded communal media as one of the greatest threats to nation-building. The Malay daily *Utusan Melayu*, printed in Jawi script and closely aligned with UMNO politics, became an important vehicle for pan-Malay nationalism. It frequently portrayed Singapore’s Chinese-led government as discriminatory towards Malays. It framed PAP’s advocacy of a “Malaysian Malaysia” as an assault on Malay rights by the “Chinese” government of Singapore. Such narratives increasingly transformed political disagreements into communal grievances. For Lee, this sustained communal mobilisation demonstrated that freedom of expression could not be divorced from its social consequences in a fragile multiethnic society. The experience would later profoundly shape Singapore’s restrictive approach towards the press and political speech. Figures such as Syed Jaafar Albar increasingly accused Lee Kuan Yew of pursuing Chinese domination under the guise of multiracialism. Sections of UMNO portrayed PAP’s calls for equal citizenship as attempts to dismantle the constitutional position of Malays and ultimately displace Malay political leadership. Political competition thus became increasingly ethnicised, with each side viewing the other as an existential threat.

The fragility of Singapore’s social fabric had already been exposed during the Maria Hertogh riots of 1950, which demonstrated how religion and ethnicity could rapidly become vehicles for mass mobilisation. The anti-Chinese riots of July and September 1964 permanently reinforced these fears in Lee. During celebrations marking the Prophet Muhammad’s birthday, nearly 20,000 Malay-Muslims had gathered amid an atmosphere already inflamed by communal rhetoric and political tension. The immediate spark may have been relatively minor. Still, leaders’ inflammatory speeches were linked with the distribution of leaflets calling for a united front to “wipe out” the Chinese and accusing them of plotting against Malays. Four people died and 178 were injured across 220 recorded incidents. Fire took some twenty Chinese-owned shophouses in Geylang and Eunos. The trauma of these riots left a permanent mark on Lee’s political psyche. Chinese conspiracies against Malays rapidly transformed isolated clashes into widespread communal violence. That day he learned a lesson about communal passions: once politically mobilised, they acquire a momentum that neither the constitutional arrangement nor policing can contain.

One might now understand the reason for his government’s reluctance to place Malay soldiers in sensitive military positions, such as the air force or armoured units; it wasn’t cited as racial superiority but as divided loyalties during a conflict with Malaysia. Lee and later military officer Lee Hsien Loong openly acknowledged that emotional and religious ties could create operational dilemmas if Singapore ever fought a Malay-Muslim neighbour.

The origins of this concern lay in the fact that, at independence, Singapore inherited a security apparatus that was disproportionately Malay because British recruitment practices had traditionally favoured Malays for military service while many Chinese avoided soldiering altogether. Lee openly acknowledged the dilemma: *“The question was whether the army and police would be as loyal to a government no longer British or Malay, but one the Malays perceived as Chinese.”* This fear was not entirely hypothetical. Singapore’s leaders had genuine concerns that sections of the old military establishment could potentially be persuaded to reverse separation. Lee wrote that figures such as Syed Ja’afar Albar might convince Brigadier Alsagoff that it was his *“patriotic duty”* to undo Singapore’s independence, noting that the brigade stationed in Singapore *“could have captured all my ministers and me without difficulty.”* The May 1969 riots further reinforced these anxieties. Lee recalled being deeply unsettled by the atmosphere among Malay soldiers deployed in Geylang Serai, observing their *“sullen, unfriendly looks”* and concluding that race relations had become dangerously polarised. He writes, *“Even Malays who had served in our police and armed forces for many years had become very race conscious, easily swayed by racial pulls during the race riots in Malaysia.”* He had to gradually rebalance the ethnic composition of the security forces by finding and recruiting *“more Chinese and Indians into the police and armed forces to reflect the population mix.”* It was a problematic posture for Islamic countries because it reflected Lee’s willingness to privilege strategic calculation over liberal ideals of equal representation, which Malaysia wanted Lee to uphold but never reciprocate. When he publicly aired the matter, Malaysia acknowledged: *“Chinese were represented only to a small extent in the armed forces and in the top echelons of the civil service.”* Even here, however, the policy was presented as temporary, expected to diminish as a distinct Singaporean national identity matured. Even after all things, Lee concludes in the book about Singapore’s relationship with Malaysia:

> ***“The dynamics of Singapore-Malaysia relations have not changed
> fundamentally since our separation on 9 August 1965. Malaysia asked us
> to leave because we stood for a Malaysian Malaysia and they for a
> Malay-dominated Malaysia. A multiracial society of equal citizens was
> unacceptable to the UMNO leaders of Malaysia in 1965 and remained
> unacceptable in 1999.”***

By now, the pattern should be familiar. Faced with a problem, Lee instinctively sought the most direct and administratively efficient solution, often with little regard for liberal conventions or procedural sensitivities. What is more striking, however, is his repeated reliance on minority interlocutors when dealing with minority communities. He preferred to negotiate through Malay PAP MPs, consult Muslim institutions and work through recognised communal leaders.

As Singapore rapidly urbanised, redevelopment required clearing old settlements and acquiring land for housing, infrastructure, and industrial expansion. This process frequently encountered resistance from communities reluctant to leave ancestral neighbourhoods or from groups that framed relocation in religious terms. The redevelopment of Malay kampungs and the demolition or relocation of mosques could easily have been portrayed as an assault on Islam and Malay identity, particularly in the politically charged atmosphere of the 1960s. Rather than relying solely on coercive state power, Lee pursued a strategy of managed accommodation. The government negotiated through Malay PAP MPs, consulted religious authorities and MUIS, guaranteed the construction of replacement mosques, and later introduced a CPF-funded mosque-building program. While the state retained ultimate authority over land use and urban planning, it simultaneously invested in strengthening Muslim religious institutions. Lee used Community leaders, who possessed an emotional legitimacy and cultural proximity the bureaucracy lacked, especially on matters of religion and family aspirations. Lasting change was most effective when it “appeared” to emerge from within the community rather than being imposed externally by the state.

The lessons of the communal riots reinforced this conviction. He understood the dangers of repeatedly relying solely upon coercion or policing. Instead, he pursued a strategy of institutional engineering by introducing ethnic housing quotas, strengthening minority representation and consulting Malay MPs. Working through MUIS and embedded ethnic integration within HDB estates. This deliberately dispersed communities and prevented the emergence of ethnic enclaves; the state effectively redesigned everyday social life, making segregation progressively more difficult and intercommunal interaction increasingly routine. The apparent success of this model encouraged other communities to establish comparable organisations. Chinese and Indian leaders later developed their own self-help institutions to address educational and social disparities within their respective communities. Yet these organisations remained firmly embedded within a state-defined framework, illustrating Lee’s preference for managed pluralism rather than unrestricted multiculturalism.

### **GRC**



Singapore’s Group Representation Constituency (GRC) system, introduced in 1988, was designed primarily to manage the political consequences of Singapore’s multi-ethnic society and to address how such representation could be guaranteed in a political system where electoral incentives might naturally encourage majoritarian outcomes. Lee’s answer was characteristically Singaporean in its simplicity and directness. If electoral behaviour could not guarantee minority representation, then the institutional rules themselves had to be altered. The introduction of the Group Representation Constituency (GRC) system in 1988 therefore required that several MPs contest a constituency as a team, with each team required to include at least one candidate from a minority ethnic group. The central objective was to prevent the under-representation of Malays, Indians and other minorities that could occur under a conventional single-member, first-past-the-post system.

> ***“To end up with a Parliament without Malay, Indian, and other
> minority MPs would be damaging. We had to change the rules.”***

It institutionalised multiracialism within the electoral system by requiring political parties to build multi-ethnic teams rather than mobilise voters purely along ethnic lines. The system therefore helped reduce ethnic voting, encouraged parties to appeal across communi,ties and ensured a relatively stable presence of minority MPs in Parliament, while initially arresting the decline in minority representa,tion as minority MPs remained slighunderrepresentednted in 1988 and only reached 21% in 1991, broadly corresponding to their share of the population. The real surplus emerged from 2001, when minority representation rose to 26.2% against a minority population share of roughly 23.2%. Even then, the GRC system itself guaranteed only a minimum minority presence. Nine Malay MPs were elected through GRCs in both 2001 and 2006, meaning that the eventual over-representation of Malays reflected PAP’s decision to nominate more minority candidates beyond the institutional minimum. Thus, the GRC should be understood by the readers less as an automatic mechanism producing minority over-representation than as an institutional floor upon which PAP recruitment and nomination choices could build

Thus, the GRC embodies a trade-off: it strengthens ethnic inclusion and political stability by embedding multiracial representation within the electoral system, while narrowing the space for electoral competition for opposition parties. By transforming ethnic diversity from a potential source of political fragmentation into a mechanism for producing multi-ethnic political representation, the GRC contributed to Singapore’s social cohesion while constraining political participation and opposition competition. The system consequently reveals a deeper tension in Singapore’s model of governance: the pursuit of ethnic inclusiveness and political stability could conflict with the ideals of open competition, participation, and electoral accountability.

The cumulative lesson Lee drew from these experiences was that multiculturalism could not survive on goodwill alone. It requires active state management, restrictions on communal mobilisation and the deliberate construction of a civic national identity that superseded ethnic and religious loyalties. Singapore’s later policies are not abstract authoritarian impulses but are drawn from the memory of how rapidly communal politics could unravel a fragile society. Religion would be protected and facilitated, but it would not be permitted to veto state policy or become an autonomous centre of political authority. In Lee’s view, the survival of a multiethnic Singapore required a secular state strong enough to manage religious diversity while preventing communal identities from determining national policy. He was willing to employ highly interventionist institutional engineering to pursue social objectives that liberal democracies generally leave to electoral competition and civil society. He used political arrangements as instruments to be modified whenever national resilience required it. Lee kept the riots present rather than letting them recede, using memory rather than amnesia as the adhesive holding the myth of multiracial consent together. What follows is less a case of nation-building through violence than nation-building through the refusal to let violence be forgotten, which India did.

# **Resistance to Communism**



If communalism represented one threat to Singapore’s survival, communism represented another, arguably more formidable, threat. Whereas Malay nationalism threatened to divide Singapore along ethnic lines, communism offered a universal ideological alternative capable of transcending communal boundaries and challenging the legitimacy of the state itself. In the decades preceding independence, communist organisations had successfully mobilised Chinese schools, labour unions, and student groups, creating a parallel source of legitimacy that rivalled the colonial state and, later, the PAP itself.

China occupied a unique place in Singapore’s political imagination. No foreign country, apart from Britaiexertedd a greater influenonver Singapore’s political development than China. As the ancestral homeland of nearly three-quarters of Singapore’s population, China represented both a source of civilisational pride and an enduring security concern. This security concern had deep historical roots. Beginning in the late nineteenth century, successive Chinese governments sought to cultivate the loyalty of overseas Chinese communities through educational institutions, cultural associations, and consular networks. The rise of the CCP merely altered the ideological content of this relationship rather than its underlying premise.

The establishment of the People’s Republic in 1949 generated an immense wave of enthusiasm among many Chinese-educated Singaporeans. For a community long subjected to colonial hierarchies and conscious of China’s *“century of humiliation”,* the emergence of a strong and unified China represented a restoration of dignity and civilisational confidence. Yet this same development generated anxieties among Malays, Indians and anti-communist elements within the Chinese community itself. Beijing actively sought to strengthen overseas Chinese attachment to the motherland through radio broadcasts, educational programmes, remittance networks, and appeals to ethnic solidarity. At the same time, communist movements in Southeast Asia increasingly looked to China for ideological inspiration and legitimacy. The MCP’s activities and Beijing’s persistent refusal to recognise Singapore’s separate political existence reinforced fears that local communist organisations could become instruments of external political influence. The central question for the PAP was whether Singapore’s Chinese population would ultimately understand themselves primarily as citizens of a new Singaporean nation or as members of a wider Chinese political community extending beyond Singapore’s borders.

These developments reinforced Lee’s deep suspicion regarding China’s intentions towards communist movements in Southeast Asia. In his conversation with the Chairman of the CCP, Hua Guofeng, he repeatedly sought a clear statement from Beijing that it would not support the MCP’s (Malayan Communist Party’s) claims over Singapore. Chinese leaders consistently avoided giving such an assurance. Beijing instead resorted to abstract formulations about the inevitability of communist liberation movements, the principle that foreign powers could not impose socialism on another country, and the claim that no communist country was a friend of China. Lee interpreted this ambiguity as deliberate. Beijing wished to establish normal state-to-state relations with Singapore while preserving ideological flexibility regarding communist insurgencies in the region. For Singapore, whose political leadership had spent the 1950s and 1960s battling communist infiltration, the refusal to explicitly reject the MCP’s claims reinforced fears that ethnic and ideological ties could be mobilised against Singaporean sovereignty.

His government also actively suppressed Cultural Revolution propaganda in Singapore, as it was smuggled by Chinese students, confiscating Maoist literature and prosecuting citizens who attempted to spread it. Lee feared that ideological mobilisation would reward revolutionary loyalty over competence and institutional merit. This would weaken institutions and replace administrative merit with revolutionary zeal. Looking at China after the Cultural Revolution, he concluded that the campaign had not created a new socialist society of equals as they had intended. Instead, it eroded public morality, weakened institutions, and encouraged opportunism, favouritism, and corruption.

Lee viewed this as an existential challenge for Singapore, as MCP and communist united-front groups never saw Singapore as an independent nation to work for; they wanted to capture and make it just another communist nation in the broader international revolutionary struggle and if they had succeeded, then “Singapore would have become another Cuba in Southeast Asia.” An alternative sovereign project. They operated from several layers. First, Lee explicitly states that the communists operated through “front organisations.” They would “resort to violence” and were quick to “rebuild their open-front organisations.”

Their decision to boycott the 1968 elections, inspired by Maoist revolutionary politics, became, in his account, the mistake that handed the PAP uncontested control of Parliament for decades. During the Cultural Revolution, Maoist propaganda, the circulation of the Little Red Book, and even the participation of institutions such as the Bank of China in disseminating revolutionary material demonstrated to him that Beijing remained willing to mobilise ethnic and ideological ties for political purposes. The radicalisation of China, which coincided with Lee’s own rise to political prominence, bolstered these anxieties during the Cultural Revolution. Throughout his book, he speaks of communists with a fascinating mix of fear and respect.

> ***“Knowing how skillful, resourceful, and tenacious the communists
> were in their methods of infiltration and manipulation, we were
> determined that they should not be given any chance to make a comeback
> by rebuilding their front organizations, especially in the trade
> unions.”***

This experience shapes Singapore’s nation-building project. These trade unions had to be integrated into skilling and education. Second, Lee viewed trade unions not as neutral interest groups but as possible instruments of political mobilisation. Rather than allowing autonomous unionism, he deliberately integrated unions into the state.

> ***“To maintain the symbiotic relationship between the PAP government
> and the NTUC(National Trades Union Congress), I encouraged the NTUC to
> get some MPs to work full-time with the unions, and to appoint others
> as advisers to various unions.”***

On the third front, Lee repeatedly describes politics with the communists in almost military terms:

> ***“Street fighting with them was like unarmed combat with no holds
> barred, in a contest where winner took all.”***
>
> ***“We learned not to give hostages to our adversaries or they would
> have destroyed us.”***

Fourth, Lee regarded detention powers as necessary precisely because communist activists could operate under democratic cover: ***“His detention... served to discourage other communist cadres from reactivating their cause under cover of exercising their democratic rights.”*** The phrase “under cover of exercising their democratic rights” reveals deep scepticism towards the liberal assumption that civil society organisations are inherently benign.

Throughout these chapters, the distinction between the Barisan and the communist movement is dismissed, portraying the former as merely another instrument of the latter. Several hundred suspected communists were held without charge, staying in prison or exile for years before they denounced the cause and explained themselves. The Internal Security Act, which allowed detention without trial, had such sweeping powers to neutralise various lobbies that sought to grab power—labour activists, student leaders, journalists, lawyers and church workers—over four decades of PAP rule. Some detainees were held for years without a hearing. Lee does not apologise for this, nor tries to justify it. He rewrote the Employment Act and Industrial Relations Act, restored management’s right to hire and fire, required secret ballots before strikes, created the National Wages Council, and transformed unions into partners of the state. Later, he expanded the NTUC into insurance, supermarkets, taxis, resorts, childcare and worker cooperatives, giving unions an economic stake in the system’s success. Militancy was not merely defeated; it became irrational because cooperation paid better.

He presents them as necessities in a political war rather than regrettable departures from liberal norms. Reflecting on the conflict, he asks bluntly:

> ***“Could we have defeated them if we had allowed them habeas corpus
> and abjured the powers of detention without trial? I doubt it.”***

The argument appears repeatedly throughout the chapter. The communists, he insists, were not conventional political opponents but an underground organisation operating through intimidation, infiltration and disciplined secrecy. As he puts it:

> ***“They were formidable opponents. We had to be as resolute and
> unyielding in this contest of wills.”***
>
> ***“We learned not to give hostages to our adversaries or they would
> have destroyed us.”***

Even his concluding judgement carries the language of warfare rather than politics:

> ***“Despite ruthless methods where the ends justified the means, the
> communists failed, but not before destroying many who stood up against
> them.”***

Whether one accepts this justification depends largely on one’s own political commitments. A sympathetic reading sees Lee responding to the examples of communist insurgencies across Asia and concluding that Singapore could not survive while, in his words, “communists retained their baleful influence.” A less charitable reading sees an exceptionally capable politician eliminating his most serious rivals under the language of national security. Lee himself offers little middle ground. Yet the more revealing aspect of these chapters lies elsewhere.

### **A. The Larger Picture**



LKY’s assessment of Mao Zedong and his Cultural Revolution distinguishes between Mao’s achievements in nation-building and his ideological excesses. Throughout his memoir, one notices his recurring sensitivity to questions of national dignity and historical humiliation in his discussions of China; he repeatedly demonstrates an appreciation for the psychological dimensions of international politics. Nations, like individuals, do not easily forgive humiliation. This sensitivity to historical humiliation and civilisational continuity recurs throughout his discussions of China, Japan, and Russia. This distinction between civilisation and ideology is also evident in Lee’s reaction to the collapse of the Soviet Union. In this, he considered Soviet communism economically exhausted and administratively dysfunctional; he refused to conflate communism’s failure with Russia’s decline. Watching the Soviet Union “shudder, become ungovernable, and then break up” was, for him, an extraordinary historical spectacle. Yet he immediately cautioned against dismissing the Russian people, reminding his readers of the nuclear scientists, chess grandmasters, and engineers produced under even a dysfunctional system. *“Unlike their communist system,”* he observed, *“the Russians are not a people to be consigned to the dustbin of history.”* For Lee, ideologies were transient, while nations and civilisations possessed a deeper historical continuity. Communism represented a phase in Russian history, not its defining essence.

Lee’s assessment of Mao illustrates this distinction most clearly, as his analysis of the Cultural Revolution itself rests on a distinction between Mao’s successes in nation-building and his radical ideologies. Lee appreciated Mao for reunifying China and establishing the legitimacy of the Chinese government after many years of civil war, British invasion, and humiliation. He recognised Mao’s important contribution in raising *“China from its centuries of poverty, degradation, disease, and hunger.”* Nonetheless, he drew a sharp distinction between state-building and ideological excess. Speaking about the Cultural Revolution, he drew a sharp line between state-building and ideological radicalism. In his view, the Cultural Revolution represented an attempt to reconstruct society through political fanaticism rather than institutional reform. Comparing Mao to Qin Shi Huang, Lee says:

> ***“Mao was painting on an old Chinese picture embedded in a mosaic;
> the rains would come, Mao’s paint would be washed off, and the mosaic
> would reappear. Mao had only one lifetime and did not have the time or
> power to erase over 4,000 years of Chinese history, tradition,
> culture, and literature. Even if all the books were burnt, the
> proverbs and sayings would survive in the people’s folk memory. Mao
> was doomed to fail.”***

For Lee, nations are strengthened through capable institutions and disciplined governance, not through ideological purification. Communism, as practised during the Cultural Revolution, threatened those foundations upon which Singapore’s survival depended. Lee’s opposition to communism was therefore never reducible to anti-Russian or anti-Chinese sentiment. He distinguished sharply between people and the ideological systems under which they happened to live. The failures of Soviet communism did not imply the decline of Russia any more than Maoism exhausted the civilisational possibilities of China. Political doctrines rise and collapse, but civilisations possess deeper historical energies that periodically reassert themselves. Politicians who mistake temporary ideological arrangements for permanent realities inevitably misunderstand history itself.

### **B. Lennenism**



Beneath his hostility lies an unmistakable respect for the movement he defeated. He repeatedly attributes the communists’ appeal to qualities he believed were absent from much of post-colonial Asia. According to him, Chinese students respected them because they were dedicated to their work, made sacrifices, and worked selflessly for the people. At the same time, the governments were seen as corrupt and patronising. Even in election campaigns, Lee shows that it is not necessary to spend much money to win – all you need is an efficient organisation. This respect surfaces repeatedly. The communists, he writes, were *“rational and calculating.”* Their methods were *“skilful and tough.”* They were *“formidable adversaries... serious men, committed to their cause.”*

> ***We were reminded from time to time that the communists never gave
> up. The switch to English in our schools had dried up their supply of
> Chinese-educated recruits so they tried hard to enlist the
> English-educated. Knowing how skillful, resourceful, and tenacious the
> communists were in their methods of infiltration and manipulation, we
> were determined that they should not be given any chance to make a
> comeback by rebuilding their front organizations, especially in the
> trade unions. Their ability to penetrate an organization with a cadre
> of influential activists and take control of it was fearsome.***

These are curious descriptions to apply to one’s greatest enemy, yet they reveal something fundamental about Lee’s political education. He did not seek to defeat the communists, but also to study them so they could not rise; and to ensure that happened, the PAP ultimately had to outlast every opposition movement. Modern politicians, he argues, mistake elections for the centre of politics. They judge public opinion from rallies, markets and newspaper headlines. Their strength, as he explains, rested on organisations that couch all the violence in rhetoric. All kinds of machinery are used to mobilise unions, clan associations, alum groups and neighbourhood networks; they have patiently built these institutions that tie ordinary people into a coherent political machine. *“The crucial factors are institutional and organisational networks to muster support.”*

The PAP defeated them only after modelling themselves on communist networks, working continuously between elections rather than appearing only during campaigns. Across these chapters, one begins to see that Lee’s greatest lesson from the communist movement was organisati in natureonal. Ideas alone do not build durable political movements. Institutions do. Networks do. Disciplined cadres do. The PAP’s organisational discipline was, ironically, partly inherited from the methods of the movement it destroyed. Lee had concluded from his long struggle with the communists that political durability depended on organisation. Communists had demonstrated the effectiveness of disciplined cadres and internal cohesion through continuous grassroots work. PAP therefore appropriated many communist organisational techniques while stripping them of their revolutionary and ideological content, redirecting them instead toward state-building and political stability.

Institutional control in the PAP system rested less on a formal party apparatus than on a physical network that ran from the smallest unit of the electorate up to the office of the prime minister himself. Lee built this network in stages, beginning with Citizens’ Consultative Committees and Community Centres inherited from the colonial period, when any open political affiliation could invite retribution from the communists during the Emergency, and continuing with the Residents’ Committees he introduced once the population moved into HDB high-rise blocks, each covering a precinct of six to ten blocks so that local leaders stayed in close contact with the people around them. The result was a chain running from the Residents’ Committees through the Community Centres and Citizens’ Consultative Committees and on to the Prime Minister’s Office, which Lee described as the nerve centre, so that even an opposition figure walking through a constituency on a campaign visit was moving through ground that had already been organised and tended by the PAP long before the election was called.

Control over who entered the leaderswas determined byowed talent scouting, which the formal party machinery resented. Lee describes systematically scanning the top ranks of the professions, commerce, manufacturing and trade unions in search of men and women in their thirties and forties who could be persuaded to stand as candidates. He treated the assessment of character as harder and more important than the assessment of ability, since ability could be read fairly well from academic and professional records while character could not. Watching the Apollo 13 crew remain composed during their crisis convinced him that NASA’s psychological screening had filtered out people prone to panic, and from that he decided to put prospective PAP candidates through testing by a psychologist and a psychiatrist designed to map character, intelligence, background and values before any of them were allowed near ministerial office. He later adopted a separate model developed by Shell for ranking executives based on what the company called their estimated potential, made up of analytical power, imagination, and a sense of reality, combined into an overall quality Shell termed helicopter quality, and in 1983 he replaced the inherited British public service system with this one.

Succession itself was managed rather than contested. Lee protected the financial position of the old guard through a Parliamentary Pensions Act that covered anyone who had served at least 9 years as an MP, parliamentary secretary, or minister, giving him room to advance younger candidates without leaving veterans exposed. When resistance to the pace of change came from within the old guard itself, most visibly from Toh Chin Chye, Lee dropped him from cabinet after the 1980 election specifically to prevent other veterans from rallying around him and slowing the transition, while still avoiding a public break that could be read as disloyalty. Ong Pang Boon was allowed to serve one further term before retiring in 1984, once he had accepted the direction of change. When it came time to choose his own successor, Lee did not select Goh Chok Tong personally but asked the younger ministers to decide the matter among themselves, reasoning that a successor needed the standing of his peers rather than an appointment handed down from above, a lesson he drew explicitly from Deng Xiaoping’s failures with Hu Yaobang and Zhao Ziyang and from Anthony Eden’s failure after being chosen by Churchill.

The party managed its relationship with public criticism through the courts rather than internal debate. Lee took libel action against opposition figures and journalists who accused him of corruption, including J. B. Jeyaretnam, the Far Eastern Economic Review and its editor Derek Davies, the International Herald Tribune over an article by Philip Bowring, and later Tang Liang Hong and the academic Christopher Lingle, arguing that failing to sue would itself have been read as evidence that the allegations were true. Space for structured input from outside the cabinet came instead through mechanisms built for that purpose. The constitution was amended in 1990 to create Nominated MPs who could raise indepen, and non-partisan views in Parliament withhavingding to win a seat. A Feedback ,Unit set up after the 1984 elec,tion gave MPs a channelgatherlect public opinion on policy through organised sessions. However, its purpose was to surface views for the government to weigh rather than to bind it to any particular course.

When the internal party crisis of 1957 occurred, in which pro-communist elements briefly captured the Central Executive Committee through mass mobilisation, it convinced Lee that conventional mass parties were inherently vulnerable to infiltration and factional capture. Political survival required a fundamentally different organisational model. Consequently, the PAP gradually developed one of the most sophisticated systems of internal party control in the post-colonial world.

Its most distinctive innovation was a two-tier membership structure. While ordinary members participated in party activities, only a small, carefully selected body of cadres held voting rights in leadership elections. The leadership appointed cadres after prolonged observation and vetting. This transformed the party from a mass organisation into a tightly controlled political institution, in which succession and internal decision-making remained insulated from organised factions and ideological entryism. The PAP likewise avoided organisational decentralisation. The 1961 split, when numerous local branches defected to the newly formed Barisan Sosialis, convinced the leadership that local organisations could become vehicles for ideological capture. Thereafter, branches were deliberately denied significant influence over national leadership or policymaking.

In many respects, the system bore a striking resemblance to Leninist principles of organisation. Internal discipline rested on the principle of *“public unity, private debate.”* Lee argued that public disagreements only benefited political opponents, particularly the communists during the party’s formative years. Policy disputes were therefore expected to be resolved through closed-door institutions rather than public criticism. Leninist organisational techniques were appropriated not to facilitate revolution but to prevent it. Some also completed one and a half days of psychological testing. Political exits were equally centralised. During the 1984 general election, senior leaders, including Toh Chin Chye and Ong Pang Boon, were denied renomination by the CEC and were expected to retire without contesting the succession process. To reinforce discipline with positive incentives, the PAP introduced a formal hierarchy of party honours in 1988. In this sense, the PAP’s extraordinary longevity cannot be understood solely in terms of economic performance or electoral success. It was equally the product of an organisational revolution. Lee’s greatest political lesson from the communists was that power is institutional before it is ideological. The movement he defeated became, paradoxically, one of his most important political teachers.

# **Departure of the British**



The British decision in January 1968 to withdraw military forces east of Suez shocked Singapore’s leadership. Barely three years had passed since separation from Malaysia; communal riots remained a recent memory; communist networks had not entirely disappeared; and Indonesia had only recently ended Konfrontasi—an undeclared, low-intensity Indonesia–Malaysia Confrontation war fought between 1963 and 1966 aimed to oppose the formation of the Federation of Malaysia, which he viewed as a British neocolonial project—encompassing Malaya, Singapore, Sarawak, and Sabah. Although the British announcement in January 1968 appeared sudden from Singapore’s perspective, the decision to withdraw from East of Suez was in reality the culmination of a longer process of strategic retrenchment that had been unfolding throughout the 1960s. By the 1960s, Britain’s overseas military installations in the Persian Gulf and Southeast Asia had become increasingly vulnerable. Bases originally intended as instruments of imperial projection had become exposed liabilities, requiring substantial resources to defend against rising nationalist movements, communist insurgencies and anti-colonial pressures. Rather than enhancing British strategic flexibility, these overseas commitments increasingly appeared to constrain it. British policymakers gradually concluded that the costs of maintaining order east of Suez were becoming disproportionate to the political and strategic benefits derived from imperial basing arrangements. At the same time, the United States, deeply entangled in the Vietnam War, had neither the political legitimacy nor the regional goodwill to replace Britain as Southeast Asia’s principal stabilising force.

The British military presence was a security umbrella under which Singapore’s economic principal pillars stood. Tens of thousands depended, directly or indirectly, on British military expenditure. This accounted for nearly one-fifth of Singapore’s US\$ 1.92 billion GDP. Lee described the announcement as a “heart-stopping blow.” The sudden prospect of losing both economic activity and external security simultaneously threatened the viability of the newly independent republic. The crisis reinforced one of Lee’s enduring assumptions about international politics: great powers ultimately act in their own interests rather than out of sentiment or historical obligation. Britain had founded modern Singapore and defended it for nearly a century and a half. Yet, it was now leaving because its domestic economic circumstances no longer permitted it to maintain imperial commitments, as PM Harold Wilson’s(1964-1970) Labour government was under mounting domestic pressure to redirect military expenditure towards welfare programmes, making Britain’s costly “East of Suez” commitments increasingly difficult to justify. Yet he was equally uneasy about an expanding American presence, whose intervention in Vietnam convinced him that the United States possessed “bulging muscles and a habit of flexing them. Lee’s own generation had been raised within the myth of the Empire as a natural protector of his people and the region, and a man does not unlearn that myth quickly, merely because the facts have changed. Even so, Lee preferred the British to the Americans. Singapore’s own capacity to defend itself was effectively nonexistent.

Lee never portrayed this as betrayal. Rather, it confirmed his realist understanding that “nobody owes Singapore a living.” Small states could survive only by making themselves indispensable and by developing the internal capacities necessary for self-preservation. The withdrawal therefore accelerated the transformation of economic policy into an instrument of national security. As he put it, Singapore had to make *“extraordinary efforts to become a tightly knit, rugged, and adaptable people,”* while remaining *“cheaper than our neighbours.”* Malaysians *“wanted to bypass us and render obsolete our role as the entrepot and middleman for the trade of the region. We had to be different.”* Industrialisation, foreign investment and export competitiveness ceased to be merely developmental objectives and became matters of national survival. Economically relevant in an entirely new way. As Lee later reflected, Malaysia wanted “to bypass us and render obsolete our role as the entrepôt and middleman for the trade of the region. We had to be different.” Economic openness, the attraction of multinational corporations, and the creation of a highly skilled workforce therefore emerged not from ideological commitment to free markets but also from the practical necessities of survival. Ironically, Britain’s retreat from East of Suez was itself driven by the same realist logic that Lee would later apply to Singapore: states ultimately reorder their commitments according to interests and capabilities rather than sentiment or historical obligation.

Lee feared that the separation of August 1965 might prove temporary rather than permanent. If the moderate leadership of Tunku Abdul Rahman gave way to the increasingly assertive Malay ultras within UMNO, or if sympathetic officers within the Malaysian armed forces decided to reverse the separation by force, Singapore had little capacity to resist. He had to ask for Gurkha guards to replace local police officers because neither Malay nor Chinese officers could be relied upon to remain politically neutral during communal unrest. Britain’s withdrawal in 1971 resolved the strategic dilemma that had haunted Lee since independence. For six years, Singapore had raced against time, attempting to build the institutions of sovereignty before the imperial umbrella disappeared.

After India and Egypt declined Lee’s request for military assistance, Israel quietly agreed to build Singapore’s army. Conscious that an overt Israeli presence would provoke opposition among Malay Muslims in both Singapore and Malaysia, the Israeli advisers arrived under the innocuous label of “Mexicans.” They helped design Singapore’s doctrine of universal conscription, reserve mobilisation, command structures, and the concept of a citizen army, drawing on Israel’s experience. The successful transition fundamentally altered Lee’s understanding of statehood. A nation, he concluded, could neither outsource its security nor entrust its survival to the calculations of foreign powers. Military self-reliance became a foundational principle of Singaporean statecraft. This was done with a sharp balancing act. On the one hand, they resisted pressure to distance Singapore from Israel or, later, to receive Israeli President Chaim Herzog, despite protests from Malaysia; Lee refused to allow Singapore’s national security or foreign policy to be dictated by the religious sensitivities of its neighbours. On the other hand, before this operation was started, he was aware that……

> ***Kidron demanded a quid pro quo, that Singapore recognize Israel
> officially and exchange ambassadors. He was persistent in pressing for
> this. I told Keng Swee that this was a nonstarter. We would anger the
> Malay Muslims in Singapore and Malaysia whose sympathies were with
> their Muslim brothers, the Palestinians and the Arabs. We could not
> agree to do this even if the Israelis decided to withdraw. When they
> knew that it was a nonstarter, Tel Aviv sent a message that they
> understood our position and they would be helpful, but that they hoped
> we would eventually allow them an embassy in Singapore.***

Lee consistently rejected external attempts to define Singapore’s foreign policy as being influenced by Islamic solidarity. The invitation extended to Israeli President Chaim Herzog, despite protests from Malaysia, was argued to have insulted Muslims. Lee refused to cancel it because doing so would have signalled that Singapore’s diplomacy was subject to religious pressure from a neighbouring state. For Lee, the issue was sovereignty rather than Israel itself. Singapore’s external relations would be determined by Singapore’s national interest, not by the religious preferences of neighbouring governments. Finally, Lee’s realism is most evident in defence policy.

The controversy surrounding Exercise Starlight began in April 1975 due to limited space in Singapore. Singapore Armed Forces training was valid for one year, allowing them to train infantry, artillery, armour, and commando units dispersed across Taiwan in areas used by their counterparts. Along the same lines, this demonstrates Singapore’s uncompromising commitment to strategic realism. Despite formally recognising the One China policy, Singapore continto cooperate militarilytion with Taiwan because it had no viable alternative for large-scale military training. Lee refused to frame the issue ideologically or defensively. He argued that Taiwan exercised de facto control over the territory and possessed the facilities Singapore required. Had the People’s Republic controlled Taiwan, Singapore would have approached Beijing instead. For Lee, national survival always superseded ideological consistency and diplomatic sensitivities. The continuation of military training in Taiwan, despite repeated objections from China, reflected Singapore’s determination to preserve strategic autonomy and avoid dependence on any single great power. Small states, in his view, could not afford sentimental attachments or doctrinal rigidity; they survived only through a relentless and unsentimental pursuit of their own security interests.

# **Liberalism is Ideal**



Singapore was built without equals and never considered itself equal to anyone. It is a small nation, giving you a local perspective; its total land area is roughly half that of Delhi (1,484 sq km), making it the 176th-largest country in the world. Its leaders have endured and know this well from the start; they have gutted this in their hearts. It was no wonder that when Lee visited a post-Cold War America and saw a shift in the winds toward liberal values, he couldn’t swallow it.

Singapore got everything from meritocracy and the hard work the people put in; they weren’t favoured or pitied in investment because someone had sympathy for them. It was the “Iron” of its leaders that brought it up against the world. Singapore’s manufacturing share of the GDP climbed rapidly from 14% in 1965 to 22% by 1975. The workforce evolved quickly from producing basic, labour-intensive goods to manufacturing sophisticated electronics, eventually making Singapore the world’s leading producer of hard disk drives in the 1980s. At independence in 1965, Singapore’s per capita GDP stood at roughly \$400, placing it alongside the impoverished nations of that era. By the time the book was published at the end of his tenure, this figure had exploded to over US\$23,000, giving Singapore the fourth-highest per capita real income globally. This was no small feat, and no small pity was shown in making this happen for them.

His bigger questions were about the basic assumptions of the world that liberals based their worldviews on. The United States and Western Europe emerged from the Cold War in 1994, soon after the Soviet Union’s collapse. In a confident mood, Americans sought to bring about instant democracy, confident in the efficacy of promoting human rights and democracy, having employed these principles under the Helsinki framework to pressure the Soviet Union. Democracy, in Lee’s account, is not something a society either has or lacks by choice. It requires prerequisites: economic development, literacy, a middle class large enough to have something to protect, and a civic society made up of voluntary associations, unions, and professional bodies that teach people to tolerate differing views. Freedom can only exist “***in an orderly state.”*** Where those preconditions are missing, importing institutions without any built-up stake and cohesion with their citizens in nation-building will produce instability, he pointed out. He cited the Philippines or Haiti as an example: an installed democracy collapsed within five years because it lacked a middle class and an economy to sustain it. This let him treat Western pressure for immediate democratisation less as an insult to reject than as a technical error to correct, which was a much easier position to defend in public than a flat assertion that his own society did not want what the West was offering. His aim was ***“The main objective is to have a well-ordered society so that everyone can enjoy freedom to the maximum.”***

Many in Congress believed the Soviet collapse foretold China’s own and treated it as America’s moral duty to bring about its end. Washington split into two camps on method: ***“one, favoured by President Bush, to encourage gradual change through a process of constructive engagement; the other, favoured by the U.S. Congress, to impose sanctions and apply political and economic pressure for human rights and political reform.”*** Deng refused the premise the theory rested on — that a communist system under pressure disintegrates on schedule — and built a market economy instead ***“with Chinese characteristics”*** that outlasted every annual MFN vote Congress passed against it. A parallel expectation ran alongside Congress’s, no less confident and no more accurate: Huntington had quoted in the NYT a claim that Taiwan and Singapore were the two most successful Chinese societies in 5,000 years of Chinese civilisation, and wanted East Asian “tigers” to be examples to China of free societies that had prosperous economies because of democratic political institutions. Lee dismisses the premise outright and wants a “China will chart its own way forward” and grounds the refusal in civilisation rather than strategy, in “a deep and abiding fear of luan (chaos)” that makes a country of that size test, adjust and adapt before incorporating anything foreign, never adopt wholesale. Unlike the Soviet elite, the Chinese leadership never accepted Western political and cultural norms as inherently superior or universally applicable to their civilisation, and Jiang Zemin said as much to Lee directly: “Any ‘shock treatment’ (of sudden democracy) for China, like that in the Soviet Union, was out.” Congress had bet on collapse. Huntington and the Times had bet on imitation. China gave neither wager its return.

> ***Nearly all Third World countries were former colonies that, after
> decades of colonial rule without either elections or democracy,
> adopted democratic constitutions modelled on those of their former
> rulers. But the British, French, Belgian, Portuguese, Dutch, and U.S.
> democratic institutions had taken hundreds of years to evolve.***

His larger point is that Liberal democracy depends upon more than constitutional design. It requires economic development, literacy, a strong middle class and a civic culture of values: tolerance, compromise, and institutional participation. Democratic forms of government ultimately evolve in accordance with a society’s historical traditions and political culture rather than through simple institutional transplantation. When Western nations attempted to implant democratic systems into societies lacking these cultural prerequisites, they often failed. South Korea, where a historical tradition of fighting to the bitter end led to ongoing street battles, regardless of whether a military dictator or a democratically elected president held power. Similarly, he viewed the physical brawls in Taiwan’s legislature as reflections of a fundamentally different political culture. Lee maintained that societies will inevitably evolve their own representative forms of government, tailored to their unique customs and historical realities. Liberals who, out of habit, impose abstract democratic ideals upon cultures primed for confrontation guarantee instability rather than liberty.

He stripped liberal politics of the English it was usually attached to. He was called the last Englishman in this sense as he kept running the machine after everyone who built it, including the English themselves, had stopped believing in it. The clearest statement of the collision came early, and it was made in private rather than in print. In 1978, Lee sat across from Patricia Derian, who was President Carter’s assistant secretary of state for human rights, and the conversation went nowhere for the better part of two hours because they were not actually arguing about the same thing. Derian emerged from the American civil rights movement and carried over its assumption that the individual holds rightthat s the state cannoinfringe uponch. Lee held the opposite premise, that the well-being of society takes precedence over individual rights and that a detainee in Singapore needed only to renounce violence to secure release.

> *“Singapore was a Confucianist society which placed the interests of
> the community above those of the individual. My primary responsibility
> was the well-being of the people.”*

Neither position yielded, and the ambassador who witnessed the exchange later wrote that the two never came to a meeting of the minds: *“If I followed her prescription, Singapore would come to grief.”* Convenience kept the disagreement out of public view for another decade. Singapore and the United States shared enough strategic ground during the Cold War that neither side wanted to make an issue of it, and only once that shared ground disappeared with the Soviet collapse did Lee find himself required to argue the matter openly rather than live with the friction.

Once forced to argue it, he had no ready-made alternative doctrine to rely on and had to build his own carefully, and the care itself is worth noticing because it shows how much justification the position needed before he would let it stand. His first move was to narrow the claim rather than widen it. Invited by Foreign Affairs in 1994 to weigh in on what had by then become known as the Asian values debate, he declined the term itself: ***“I avoided using the term ‘Asian values’... and instead referred to Confucian values”***, pointing out that Asia held too many different value systems—Hindu, Muslim, Buddhist, as well as Confucian — for any single label to hold. What he defended instead was something more specific: the Confucian inheritance shared by China, Korea, Japan, Vietnam, and the ethnic Chinese populations of Southeast Asia, a tradition in which the individual exists within a web of family, extended family, and community rather than as a free-standing bearer of rights. ***“There is no Asian model as such, but there are fundamental differences between East Asian Confucian and Western liberal societies.”*** It meant his argument could not be dismissed as a claim to represent a whole continent. It was a claim about one cultural lineage, made by someone who belonged to it and could speak for it without overreaching into territory he did not actually know. *“Confucian societies believe that the individual exists in the context of the family, extended family, friends, and wider society.”*

The third move was closer to home and rested on results. American liberals had told him that a free press was the precondition for clean government, since an unfettered media would expose corruption before it took root. Lee answered this with evidence rather than counter-theory. India, the Philippines, Thailand, and South Korea all had freewheeling presses, and all remained deeply corrupt. At the same time, Silvio Berlusconi in Italy owned much of his own country’s media and was investigated for corruption anyway. Singapore, without that press freedom, had been rated by Transparency International as the seventh-least-corrupt country in the world, ahead of Britain, Germany, and the United States. If the mechanism liberalism claims to require does not reliably produce the outcome it promises, the mechanism is not actually load-bearing, and a different arrangement that produces the outcome by other means needs no apology.

The welfare state gave him his sharpest material for the same kind of functional argument, and here he was willing to be blunt about what he thought liberalism actually costs a society. Describing Singapore’s preference for family responsibility and compulsory savings over state entitlement, he wrote that this stood in direct contrast to the West, where liberals actively encourage ***“people to demand their entitlements with no sense of shame***. The claim did double duty. It explained why Singapore hadestablishedt the Central Provident Fundrather thanf a pension system funded by later generations. It let him treat Britain’s postwar welfarism, sustained across party lines until Thatcher, as a cautionary tale rather than an isolated policy failure, evidence that a liberal welfare settlement erodes the very self-reliance a productive society depends on.

Even so, Lee did not let the position collapse into a blanket rejection of anything resembling universal rights. He understood that satellite television and visibility would give atrocity a new dimension, where it would be built, and that governments couldn’t expect the world not to notice it, as he predicted a slow convergence toward a shared standard of what treatment of a country’s own people was acceptable. What he refused to accept was the conflation of that standard with democracy specifically. Pressing for human rights, in his framing, was defensible and probably inevitable. Pressing for a particular electoral system on a timetable set by Washington was not the same thing, and treating the two as interchangeable was, to his mind, where the American position went wrong.

### **A. Confucianism**



The last piece of justification was biographical, which addressed a specific accusation. Critics argued that Lee’s invocation of Confucian values was a retrospective justification for Singapore’s paternalistic political order, developed to defend restrictions on liberal rights during the Asian Values debate of the early 1990s. “America should not foist its system indiscriminately on other societies where it would not work.”

Let us locate how and when such ideas emerged in Lee’s mind. The Speak Mandarin Campaign was launched in Singapore in 1979; Confucian Ethics was introduced into schools in the early 1980s, and the Institute of East Asian Philosophies was established soon thereafter. These initiatives all predated China’s emergence as an economic powerhouse, suggesting that Lee’s engagement with Confucian thought was rooted less in emulating a successful China than in his own vision of cultivating the cultural and moral foundations of Singaporean society.

The memoir itself supports this timeline as a language policy campaign. We trace the decision back to the 1978 merger of Nanyang University with the University of Singapore, after which he concluded that Chinese Singaporeans needed to speak Mandarin rather than their various dialects for the bilingual school system to work, and he launched the Speak Mandarin campaign the following year. What makes the timing significant for the larger argument is that he says the decision was driven not by language efficiency alone, but by a fear that something was being lost. As Chinese-medium schools converted to English instruction, Lee worried that the discipline, self-confidence, and moral grounding those schools had once instilled would disappear along with the language, since English-medium teachers, however competent, did not carry the same weight of inherited values and were less self-assured. Writing to Goh Keng Swee in 197,9 as hewas handedm the education ministry, Lee described the older Chinese-school teachers as instilling a sense of duty and responsibility in their students because they had greater drive, discipline, and thrust. This was 1979, a full decade before Deng’s reforms had produced anything resembling the China that later commentators assumed Lee was imitating. The concern on record is domestic and pedagogical rather than admiring of a foreign model that did not yet exist in any form worth admiring. The same continuity reappears a decade later, when China’s success was still genuinely in doubt rather than an accomplished fact. In September 1988, dining with Zhao Ziyang in Beijing, Lee was shown a television documentary arguing that China would never modernise unless it abandoned its traditional culture entirely. Lee’s response is telling, given how the book frames it. He told Zhao that China did not need to discard its basic values to industrialise, and pointed to Taiwan, South Korea, Japan, Hong Kong, and Singapore as societies that had preserved thrift, hard work, scholarship, and loyalty to family and had grown from that foundation rather than despite it. At that moment, China was dealing with a wave of panic buying, rising inflation, and a leadership that, within a year, would purge Zhao himself, hardly the picture of the economic triumph that critics later claimed Lee was retroactively attaching himself to. The argument he made to Zhao is structurally identical to the one he had already built into Singapore’s own school policy nine years earlier, suggesting a settled conviction carried into a new context rather than one assembled after the fact to fit a China that had already arrived.

# **Media Pandits**



Lee mounted one of the strongest intellectual challenges to the liberal conception of the press. Liberals who treat the media as an insurmountable watchdog, on the strength of which they build the language to criticise and infiltrate by the logic of exposing corruption and asking for accountability. Lee accepted none of these assumptions as universally applicable. He argued that such a model emerged from Western societies and could not simply be transplanted into a fragile, postcolonial, multiethnic state whose foremost task was nation-building rather than political contestation.

He was particularly critical of the *“reporting styles and political attitudes of the American media,”* which he believed were instinctively sceptical and cynical towards authority. Instead, Lee argued that the media should reinforce *“the cultural values and social attitudes being inculcated in our schools and universities”* and cultivate the discipline, skills, and attitudes necessary for national development.

Lee rejected the idea that freedom of the press automatically meant public freedom, arguing instead that *“the freedom of the press was the freedom of its owners to advance their personal and class interests.”* He accepted criticism from locally owned newspapers because they shared the consequences of Singapore’s future, but refused to extend the same legitimacy to foreign-owned publications. After revoking the *Singapore Herald’*s license in 1971, he defended the decision by asserting that *“freedom of the press... must be subordinated to the overriding needs of Singapore, and to the primacy of purpose of an elected government.”* Lee repeatedly stated that he accepted criticism from locally owned newspapers because they would “stay and suffer the consequences of their policies.” Foreign editors, whom he described as “birds of passage,” occupied no such position. They could shape public opinion, destabilise domestic politics, and depart without bearing any responsibility for the consequences. The same logic informed Singapore’s later restrictions on publications such as *The Wall Street Journal*.

Lee did not ban foreign newspapers. Instead, he reduced their circulation when they intervened in Singapore’s domestic politics and refused to publish the government’s replies. His dispute with the *Wall Street Journal* led him to conclude that the debate was often less about press freedom than about commercial interests presented as constitutional principles. For Lee, the central question was not whether the press should be free, but whether it strengthened or weakened the nation. This is an overlooked point. People usually remember the Newspaper and Printing Presses Act, but Lee devotes surprising space to describing how he debated critics rather than simply banning them. Rather than suing Bernard Levin in Britain, he invited him to a televised debate. When William Safire repeatedly attacked him, Lee agreed to a recorded interview rather than prevent publication. His concern was that Singaporeans should see their leaders as capable of answering criticism, rather than hiding from it. He explicitly writes that:

> ***“Advances in information technology, satellite broadcasting, and
> the Internet will enable Western media networks to saturate our
> domestic audience with their reports and views. Countries that try to
> block the use of IT will lose. We have to learn to manage this
> relentless flood of information so that the Singapore government’s
> point of view is not smothered by the foreign media.”***

Lee was not simply attempting to discipline the press, as most Western thinkers have thought. He was attempting to construct an alternative journalistic tradition within. We must see this in his continuation, his refusal to inherit British military doctrine uncritically, and instead his adaptation of Israeli methods to its own circumstances. Lee likewise refused to inherit the American philosophy of journalism. The American “foreign policy is often driven not by considerations of strategic national interest, but by their media.” It was a clear indication of political interference by outsiders and bad actors, so he treated the press as another institution that had to be redesigned to suit the political realities of a fragile, multicultural republic. In doing so, he challenged one of liberalism’s deepest assumptions: that the adversarial press is a universally applicable institution rather than a historically contingent product of the American political experience. In his view, the press was not an institution standing outside the nation-building project but one of its principal instruments. An institution embedded within the state-building project, whose foremost obligation was to preserve social cohesion, strengthen civic discipline, and contribute to the nation’s long-term survival. For him, the central question was never whether the press should be free, but free for what purpose.

# **The Pragmatic Solutions**



A recurring pattern throughout *From Third World to First* is that Lee Kuan Yew rarely viewed coercion as an end in itself. While decisive state action often neutralised immediate threats, it was almost always followed by institutional reforms designed to eliminate the conditions that produced those threats, rather than relying on permanent repression. This approach sought to redesign incentives, create new institutions, and gradually cultivate social norms that make stability self-sustaining. The following examples illustrate this governing philosophy across labour relations, education, race, media, and political institutions.

### **A. Flipping the Economy**



To secure the island against these converging military, racial, and ideological threats, Lee realised that political rhetoric would not suffice. The ultimate defence against communist insurgency and Malayan hostility was overwhelming economic success. Economic survival became the organising principle of Singapore’s early statecraft. Every policy, regardless of its ideological origin, was ultimately judged by whether it generated employment, investment, or technological capability. He began taking a realistic approach, assessing policies based on their potential to generate employment and growth. As one goes through the book, one sees no doubt about the realism of Singapore’s response to the crisis. This contrasted with both a conventional nationalist movement and adherence to a rigid economic doctrine. He rested on a practical orientation as the best case for a “Learner State”.

In Chapter 4, he describes his encounter with Albert Winsemius, who advises him that economics is inseparable from political stability and international perception. His recommendation to defeat the communists was development in natureal. He keenly explains to Lee that foreign investors would not commit capital to a society threatened by revolutionary politics. More revealing was his advice regarding the statue of Stamford Raffles. To many postcolonial leaders, removing colonial symbols was a necessary assertion of sovereignty. Lee rejected this impulse and decided that retaining Raffles symbolised continuity of tradition and a silent alignment with the West, signalling to Western investors that Singapore would not embark on a path of revolutionary nationalism or socialist expropriation—a break from the dominant postcolonial mood of that era.

Across Asia and Africa, newly independent states often sought legitimacy by rejecting colonial inheritances. That is to say, the nationalism they thought had to be brought to the forefront from within the native spirit and made a journey of sovereignty; Lee challenged this by forging a new model of a multiracial, multilingual nation-state, forging bonds with minorities and suppressing any divergence, while simultaneously centralising and homogenising aspects of nationality. A true sense of the Confucian model. Singapore instead selectively appropriated aspects of its colonial legacy. English remained the working language. British legal institutions were largely preserved. Administrative norms were retained and strengthened. Lee’s government viewed institutions in terms of their practical utility.

The search for economic survival also reveals the experimental character of early Singaporean development. Consequently, the government became willing to explore almost any avenue that could generate employment. Trade missions were sent to Africa. Tourism was promoted through the creation of the Merlion and the Singapore Tourist Promotion Board. Local industries, from cosmetics to mosquito coils, were encouraged. The common thread was not adherence to an economic theory but the urgent need to create jobs.

A second major theme emerges in Lee’s treatment of foreign capital. Many postcolonial governments viewed multinational corporations as instruments of neocolonial domination:

> ***The accepted wisdom of development economists at the time was that
> MNCs were exploiters of cheap land, labor, and raw materials. This
> “dependency school” of economists argued that MNCs perpetuated the
> colonial pattern of exploitation that left developing countries
> selling raw materials to and buying consumer goods from advanced
> countries. MNCs controlled technology and consumer preferences and
> formed alliances with their host governments to exploit the people and
> keep them down. Third World leaders believed this theory of
> neocolonialist exploitation, but Keng Swee and I were not impressed.
> We had a real-life problem to solve and could not afford to be
> conscribed by any theory or dogma (Pg. 58)***

Singapore adopted the opposite view. Foreign investment was seen as a source of technology, managerial expertise, market access and employment. The goal was not economic autarky but integration into global production networks. This represented a significant departure from the import-substitution strategies then fashionable throughout the developing world.

Deng Xiaoping later praised this view, making the point explicitly. After visiting Singapore in 1978, he gave a speech in October 1979 stating that he had come specifically to study how Singapore used foreign capital and how, when others invested, they also taught skills to natives. Lee adds that what Deng saw in Singapore in 1978 became a benchmark for what China itself should aim for. From this experience emerged one of the central moral principles of the Singaporean state: Lee repeatedly frames dependency as both an economic and psychological danger. Aid that encouraged passivity was viewed as harmful, even if materially beneficial in the short term. Economic policy, therefore, became linked to a broader civic ethic emphasising discipline, responsibility and adaptation. The famous statement: “The world does not owe us a living.” In this sense, Lee’s foreign policy extended the same logic internationally. Economic openness was a means of preserving political autonomy. Singapore welcomed capital, technology, and security partnerships while ensuring none became a source of strategic dependence, and that modern forces were used to propel and set the tone for how modernity in Asia would work. It could let the USA build factories, teach them things, and get their CEOs into government to lead them. But never let them (modernity and its forces—Media, narratives, International bodies) abuse them.

Just as Lee refused to allow foreign media to influence Singapore’s national psychology, he was equally uncompromising in preventing internal elements from eroding state legitimacy, and he understood that the authority to impose social discipline and pursue unpopular policies ultimately rested on its moral credibility. A government asking citizens to sacrifice, obey strict regulations and subordinate communal interests to national ones could not itself appear predatory or self-serving. This concern was deeply intertwined with Lee’s understanding of Singapore’s social realities. Unlike older nations that could draw on a shared ethnicity, language, or historical memory to hold people accountable or to use them as weapons, Singapore lacked these unifying foundations. It was a multilingual, multireligious, and multicultural society whose cohesion could never be taken for granted. The communal tensions culminating in the 1964 race riots left a lasting impression on Lee. Multiracial equality became, in his words, an article of faith. For Lee, national cohesion had to be consciously cultivated through fairness and meritocracy, while everyone had to make a shared sacrifice. Economic burdens had to be distributed equitably, opportunities had to remain open, and no community could be allowed to believe that another group had captured the state. In such a context, corruption was not merely an administrative failure; it threatened the very foundations of nationhood. Lee frequently contrasted Singapore with much of postcolonial Asia, where corruption had become normalised as an accepted feature of political life:

> *“The percentage, kickback, baksheesh, slush, or whatever the local
> euphemism is, is a way of life in Asia.”*

For him, corruption produced psychological effects. Once citizens concluded that advancement depended upon patronage rather than merit, faith in the national project would inevitably deteriorate. Meritocracy itself would become a fiction. Comparative observations of other societies also shaped his views. Lee’s assessment of British rule in Hong Kong was notably balanced. He regarded the colonial administration as generally honest and effective, except for the severe corruption that emerged in the decade preceding Governor MacLehose’s tenure. The situation became serious enough that MacLehose introduced stringent reforms, drawing heavily upon Singapore’s anti-corruption institutions and practices. At the same time, Lee did not romanticise colonial rule. He recognised that Hong Kong’s economic structure remained tilted in favour of British commercial interests, with major banks and trading houses enjoying privileged positions. Yet despite these inequities, he considered administrative integrity and bureaucratic competence indispensable foundations of political order. This comparative perspective explains why clean governance became a central pillar of the Singaporean model. The PAP leadership had accumulated immense political capital through its struggles against communal politics and communist movements. Lee understood that corruption or incompetence could rapidly dissipate this hard-earned legitimacy. As he later wrote:

> *“We were sickened by the greed, corruption and decadence of many
> Asian leaders.”*

His disillusionment extended even to revolutionary regimes that had initially promised equality and justice. He regarded the early Communist experience in China as a cautionary example of how ideological movements could degenerate into systems of patronage and opportunism, in which political advancement increasingly depended on factional loyalty rather than competence or principle.

Equally significant was Lee’s belief that institutions had to reinforce these norms. In Straddling the Middle Ground, he argues that litigation itself helped shape Singapore’s political culture. Politicians, whether in government or in opposition, were expected to substantiate allegations with evidence rather than rhetoric. Lawsuits therefore served not merely as instruments of punishment but as mechanisms for establishing political norms and altering incentives. Ultimately, Lee sought to cultivate what he called a “climate of opinion” in which corruption in public office was regarded as a direct threat to society itself. Clean governance was therefore not simply a moral preference or technocratic ideal. It was an existential necessity for a fragile city-state attempting to forge a nation from profound diversity. The legitimacy of the Singaporean state depended upon citizens believing that the system was fundamentally fair, meritocratic, and incorruptible.

### **C. Language For All**



He had much to change and much to fight for. Still, to establish Singapore as a successful nation, he needed to make it a transition state for colonial businesses while resisting the colonial agendas they brought. I can list many of them, but I must specify what works in the Indian context and what makes his ideals suitable for use in India. We must take a brief look at his language policy.

Lee’s language policy reflected the ongoing contradictions in Singaporean nation-building. Because he and his wife wereeducated in Englandd, he struggled to understand their culture and felt a sense of loss. He understood the cultural dislocation as “lost between two cultures”: English and Chinese. This differed from many postcolonial elites, who embraced English as a marker of modernity and development. Lee recognised the role of language in etching civilizational memory and social values. His decision to send his own children to Chinese-medium schools was therefore both personal, due to his respect for Chinese education for instilling discipline, filial piety and social responsibility, and a signal to Chinese-speaking parents that he wouldn’t eliminate Chinese education and that modernisation need not entail cultural abandonment. Yet Lee’s commitment to cultural preservation was not meant to cross the lines. He concluded that a multilingual society could not function without a common working language. Especially in the armed forces. English emerged not because he found any cultural value in it, but because he disliked its cultural numbing; he chose it for its political neutrality. He sets out 2 positions: one in his election of 1959, when the PAP wanted Malay as the national language due to the merger with Malaya, and English had to be the sole language of the workplace; and the other, that connecting Singapore to global markets was necessary due to its historical position as a trading centre. He introduced a 3-language policy in Chinese, Malay, and Tamil at an English school. After Singapore’s separation, many Chinese asked the government to make Chinese the official language. This was an angry response to discrimination in Malaysia.

Lee departs from both classical nationalism and multiculturalism, pursuing a model of state-directed integration in which ethnic communities retain their mother tongues while operating within a common English-speaking public sphere. This approach carried an unmistakably Confucian imprint. Lee viewed education as the cultivation of character. His admiration for the discipline and social commitment of Chinese-educated students stood in sharp contrast to his criticism of the individualism and self-centredness he observed among the English-educated students. Nevertheless, Lee was prepared to override cultural sentiment whenever it conflicted with state survival. He curbed the use of Dialect in Chinese and encouraged students to use Mandarin after the incidents surrounding the Nanyang and Singapore University joint Campus solution. He initiated the change through his speeches and stopped using Hokkien.

For Lee, language was not merely a means of communication but a medium through which one could apprehend the texture of another mind. His preference for direct conversations with leaders such as Helmut Schmidt, Valéry Giscard d’Estaing, and Jacques Chirac, all of whom spoke English, stemmed from his belief that linguistic immediacy enabled a deeper understanding of motives and intentions. This practical concern also informed his broader assessment of Europe’s future. Lee argued that until Europe settled on a common language, it could not enjoy the same economies of scale and labour mobility as the United States. The persistence of multiple linguistic spheres limited the interchangeability of engineers, managers, and technical expertise across the continent. English, by contrast, has increasingly become the language of international commerce, diplomacy, and technology. He notes with some satisfaction that even French and German executives increasingly conduct business in English and that, with the rise of the Internet, “the supremacy of the English language can be ignored only at a heavy price.” Language here appears not as an issue of cultural prestige but of competitiveness and modernisation.

English provided Singapore with a neutral linguistic platform in a multi-ethnic society while simultaneously embedding it within the emerging architecture of globalisation. In this sense, language policy became another instrument of survival, enabling Singapore to transcend both ethnic fragmentation at home and provincialism abroad. For Lee, modernity required not only industrial capital and administrative competence but also linguistic capital. Mastery of English became a means for Singapore to access the knowledge networks and economic opportunities of an increasingly interconnected world.

Hong Kong is the book’s proof that this entire apparatus was a necessity, not a preference. Lee draws the contrast with a bluntness usually reserved for adversaries rather than fellow British possessions. Hong Kongers depended on themselves and their families rather than the government, hawking, trading, and manufacturing with a drive Lee calls intense, sustained by family ties rather than by any social contract the colonial administration had bothered to offer. He credits the arrangement to the time before Milton Friedman ever turned it into an economics lecturwhen he sawing the advantages of a little or no social safety net long before it became doctrine. If exposure to annihilation were sufficient to force nationhood into being, Hong Kong should have built one. Hong Kong could run this way for structural rather than cultural reasons. It was never permitted to become a nation; China would not have allowed it, and the British never tried, so its people never organised into a collective capable of defending their own interests the way Singaporeans could. Nationhood in this account is not something a population accumulates through shared danger. It is something a state either constructs by policy, and Hong Kong sat under two powers that agreed, for their own separate reasons, to withhold it. That is the difference Lee places at the centre of the comparison, not wealth and not effort but the presence or absence of a state with a people behind it. Singapore could not choose Hong Kong’s path because a distant sovereign never guaranteed Singapore’s survival, as Hong Kong’s was, first by Britain and then by China. A state building a nation from nothing cannot leave its people to the discipline of pure scarcity, because the entire project depends on convincing a population fractured by language, ethnicity, and historical memory that this particular government deserves their political loyalty. That loyalty had a price, and the price was the welfare architecture that Lee intellectually distrusted but politically found necessary. LKY had to answer two major questions in developing Singapore. First, how could the country develop a strong economy? Once that was achieved, how could they ensure the welfare of all citizens? Or, as he put it, he wanted to leapfrog the region and then create a “First World oasis”. LKY’s strategy for the former was simple: provide goods and services “cheaper and better than anyone else, or perish.” He was proudly adamant about his country’s refusal to beg, describing on every other page how he would tell his citizens things like, “The world does not owe us a living.” Or, “If we were a soft society then we would already have perished. A soft people will vote for those who promise a soft way out, when in truth there is none. There is nothing Singapore gets for free.”

As I mentioned before, this was one of many areas where he firmly rejected conventional wisdom. In his telling, development economists and many Third World leaders of the 1960s described multinational corporations as “neocolonialist exploiters of cheap land, labour, and raw materials... but... we had a real-life problem to solve and could not afford to be conscripted by any theory or dogma.” So instead, he effectively threw open his country’s doors and said, “Exploit us!” I will explain this in the next section. To inspire confidence and demonstrate Singapore’s discipline and reliability, LKY focused on planting trees and developing parkland in the city centre and along routes to and from the airport, so people could see what it means to be a first-world country. By the 1970s, as the country’s economy stabilised, that confidence manifested in other ways, as illustrated by this interaction:

> ***When our... officer asked how much longer we had to maintain
> protective tariffs for the car assembly plant owned by a local
> company, the finance director of Mercedes-Benz said brusquely,
> “Forever,” because our workers were not as efficient as Germans. We
> did not hesitate to remove the tariffs and allow the plant to close
> down. Soon afterwards we also phased out \[other protections\].***

Viewed from a distance, the entire process follows a recognisable pattern: initial tight control, caution, and centralised planning, followed by a gradual move towards a freer economy once the system appeared stable. Concerned about governments running industries at a loss, LKY insisted that state-owned corporations either remain profitable or be shut down. As these enterprises succeeded, they were eventually privatised. Telecommunications, the port, and public utilities all began under state control before evolving into independent, profitable companies.

If he hates welfarism, then he believes in funding Prosperity. I formulate that the paradox of Singapore is that Lee spent much of his career warning against the dangers of welfarism while simultaneously constructing one of the world’s most extensive systems of state-mediated asset distribution. He sought to create dependence on ownership rather than dependence on transfers. The foundation of Singapore’s welfare system was the compulsory Central Provident Fund (CPF), which LKY viewed as a means of preserving self-reliance and preventing one generation’s welfare costs from burdening the next. As wages rose, CPF contributions steadily increased, eventually reaching a combined employer-employee contribution rate of 50% before declining. Rather than relying on large redistributive programs, Singapore built its welfare system on forced savings, allowing citizens to finance housing, healthcare, and retirement through their own accumulated assets—the same logic guided privatisations. Shares in companies such as Singapore Telecom and public transport firms were distributed to citizens, creating both a tangible stake in national prosperity and a reduction in demands for subsidies. Beyond pensions, LKY considers homeownership essential to nation-building in a society with “no deep roots in a common historical experience.” Through extensive land acquisition and the construction of HDB housing, the state transformed Singapore into a nation of homeowners, with nearly 87% of the population living in public housing at its peak. The transition was often disruptive and even absurd, with former farmers attempting to raise pigs and poultry in high-rise apartments. At the same time, many older residents never ceased resenting the loss of their traditional way of life. Yet LKY believed that ownership fundamentally altered political behaviour, noting that people who had once participated in riots later sought to protect their homes and possessions during periods of unrest.

Healthcare followed the same incentive-driven philosophy. Rejecting both the British model of universal free care and the American insurance-based system, LKY argued that both encouraged waste in different forms. Singapore instead introduced Medisave, a health savings scheme funded through CPF contributions and supplemented by targeted subsidies, catastrophic insurance, and government assistance for those without adequate means; throughout, policy remained focused on balancing welfare with personal responsibility, ensuring that essential care remained accessible without creating what LKY viewed as dependency, high costs, or unsustainable demands upon the state.

Subsidised education, subsidised healthcare, and subsidised housing all had to be introduced consciously in the early years, because the alternative was a citizenry with little reason to consider itself a nation at all. This necessarily transformed the relationship between citizen and state. Asset redistribution, subsidised housing, and state provisioning created a reciprocal social contract in which citizens increasingly viewed the state not merely as a regulator but as the guarantor of collective prosperity. Political loyalty was not expected to emerge spontaneously from shared suffering or market participation alone. It had to be cultivated through institutions that gave citizens a tangible stake in Singapore’s survival and success. A nation owes its citizens what a colony never owed its subjects. The trade-off this produced ran counter to Singapore’s own comparative advantage, creating a population that increasingly expected the government to set the finish line rather than merely provide the track, and that blamed the state.

In contrast, Hong Kongers would have blamed their own luck and tried again. Language policy and the welfare compromise were therefore two expressions of the same instrument, both representing the price of building a people where none had previously existed. Both policies sought to transform fragmented communities into stakeholders of a common political project. One is manufactured cultural cohesion; the other is material attachment. Hong Kong never had to pay that price because it was never asked to be anything more than a marketplace with excellent hawkers. Singaporeans vote for their MPs and expect rewards at the end of the race, because they have come to expect the state to continue playing an active role in safeguarding prosperity, as the state originally asked them to subordinate older communal loyalties in exchange for national belonging. The state that asked them to become a nation also promised to stand behind them once they had done so. I conclude that Singapore and Hong Kong are almost inverse experiments. The contrast between them, therefore, becomes one between two forms of social integration: one resting primarily on family and market networks, the other on state-constructed nationhood and asset ownership.

# **Swallow the Moderns**



If one may ask why Singapore was developed and not other asian tigers, one must refer to Lee’s answer to this , which he gave to the French President*: **“I thought were three primary reasons: first, stability and cohesion in society; second, a cultural drive to achieve and a thrifty, hardworking people always investing in the future, with high savings for a rainy day and for the next generation; third, a great reverence for education and knowledge.”*** Singapore became a user of modernity, and modern forces used it to propel and set the tone for why modernity in Asia would work. Underlying these episodes, one reads in the book and notices a broader insight. He has sought to position his nation as an active intermediary between East and West, a trusted node through which capital, technology and ideas could flow. Singapore would welcome Western investment, learn from Western institutions, and integrate into global networks, but would not be intellectually or strategically subordinate to them. Modernity was to be used, adapted, and domesticated rather than inherited.

Lee increasingly came to view Singapore as something larger than a successful city-state. It was to become a gateway through which modernity would enter Asia and, equally importantly, a proof to the West that Asians could master the institutions of capitalism, technology, and administration on equal terms. Singapore was intended to be a demonstration effect. Even Japan’s wartime rhetoric of a “Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere” did not persuade Lee that Asia constituted a singular civilisational bloc moving toward a common destiny. Nor did he regard Japan’s own modernisation under the Meiji Restoration as a readily transferable model for the rest of Asia. Japan appeared to him as an exceptional case: a historically continuous civilisation that had modernised without colonial subjugation or humiliation, with strong bureaucratic institutions and an extraordinary social discipline, thus possessing a cultural cohesion and institutional inheritance unavailable to most postcolonial societies. If anything, Japan showed that Asians could become modern, industrial, and technologically sophisticated. Asians, too, could become manufacturers and innovators of the highest order. Yet Japan’s success rested on historical conditions that much of post-colonial Asia lacked. Japan could inspire, but it could not simply be replicated. Singapore’s challenge was therefore not to become another Japan but to construct from colonial fragments the institutional foundations that had made Japan’s own transformation possible. Singapore and other newly independent Asian states confronted an entirely different set of problems: ethnic fragmentation, colonial legacies, and uncertain national identities. Lee’s admiration for Japan gradually evolved from what he describes as “reluctant respect and admiration” as he watched the Japanese “stoically and methodically set out to rebuild from the ashes of defeat.” He concluded that they had preserved “many key attributes that had made old Japan strong.” Singapore therefore sought something modest but perhaps more difficult: to consciously manufacture within a single generation the habits of discipline, competence, meritocracy, and administrative capacity that Japan had inherited through its own historical evolution.

This explains Lee’s view of Singapore as a gateway to modernity in Asia, a “stepping stone,” and a means of building Western leaders’ interest in the region by using him as an example. Persistent efforts to draw European attention towards East Asia. He repeatedly feared that Europe would become so preoccupied with its own internal transformations that it would neglect the Pacific. Speaking of France, he notes with some satisfaction that the old Francophone imagination had to be broken. Paris had long believed that *“it would still be French-speaking and French-inclined,”* particularly in parts of Africa and Vietnam. Only in the mid-1980s, when President François Mitterrand concluded that *“Africa* *was* *not* *as* *ready* *for* *development* *as* *Asia,”* did Lee’s efforts to redirect European interest toward East Asia begin to bear fruit. A similar pattern appears in his conversations with Helmut Kohl, who was concerned that German reunification had raised fears that Germany would have neither the resources nor the political attention to spare for Asia. Kohl reassured Lee that Germany remained *“essentially outward-looking”* and sought deeper economic and cultural engagement with East Asia. Lee, in turn, urged him not *“to leave East Asia to the Americans and the Japanese,”* thereby capturing Lee’s larger strategic vision that East Asia should not become merely an arena dominated by great powers but rather a region embedded in multiple, overlapping networks of engagement. Kohl’s proposal that Singaporean and German entrepreneurs jointly invest in China, Vietnam, and other Asian markets particularly appealed to Lee because it aligned with one of his geopolitical instincts. Singapore’s security and prosperity depended upon preventing any single power from monopolising the region. Economic interdependence was therefore a strategy by other means.

It would be incorrect to suggest that Lee’s search for developmental models was confined to the West alone. He also looked towards Japan, albeit after a prolonged effort to bring Japanese expertise into Singapore. Lee hoped that Japanese firms and technicians would help cultivate manufacturing capabilities, transfer industrial skills, and establish factories in the city-state. However, Japanese policymakers and corporations tended to evaluate developing countries primarily through the lens of their economic utility to Japan. Singapore, lacking a large market or abundant natural resources, did not initially command significant attention. Consequently, Lee had to remind Japan that Singapore possessed strategic leverage and goodwill that could serve Japanese interests. Through a combination of subtle pressure and diplomatic engagement, Singapore gradually attracted Japanese participation in its development project. It demonstrates that Singapore’s industrialisation was not simply the product of market forces but of sustained political entrepreneurship. The Sumitomo petrochemical project required repeated interventions at the highest political levels and nearly a decade of diplomatic effort before it materialised. Lee understood that capital, particularly in strategic industries, was often embedded within political considerations and could not be attracted merely through economic incentives. Lee repeatedly attempted to persuade Japanese leaders to move beyond a purely extractive relationship with Southeast Asia. To sustain its position in Southeast Asia, Japan would have to demonstrate a broader commitment to the region’s development by assisting industrialisation and transferring productive capacity. Economic relationships that appeared extractive would inevitably generate political resentment. Industrial partnership, by contrast, would create legitimacy and long-term strategic goodwill. What particularly impressed Lee was Japan’s temporal orientation.

Japanese policymakers were not attempting to recreate an idyllic pre-modern Japan of samurai and sailing ships. They understood that national survival depended upon continuous adaptation and the acquisition of new competencies. Having completed the phase of technological catch-up, they were already thinking about the next frontier: knowledge industries, energy conservation and technological innovation. Japan’s developmental state was therefore characterised by a disciplined futurism, a capacity to orient institutions towards emerging historical conditions rather than inherited nostalgias. We see a **Civilisational synthesis** in LKY, where Singapore actively sought to incorporate Japanese and other national industrial competencies into its own developmental model, treating foreign civilisations as repositories of techniques that could be selectively appropriated and domesticated. This meant he would continuously supply the party’s cadre with the nation’s top talent, no matter how much it affected the old guard. He was a swallower of Modernity and its forces.

# **Vietnam**



This infamous war, which the media reports as a war lost by Americans and a war of wrong, has a strong effect on Asian people. Lee did not agree with everything Americans did; he knew full well they were problematic and unprincipled. Americans were rough-handled because they were “without history”; it was not just him but a general observation of thinkers about the USA. Henry Kissinger: ***“America frequently approached foreign societies as problems of institutional engineering rather than historical organisms shaped by unique traditions and accumulated experiences.”*** Protected by geography and animated by universalist ideals, the United States frequently assumed that institutions and values that emerged from its own historical experience were universally applicable. As we saw earlier, democracy was such a universally applied principle for them. This generated a tendency towards heavy-handed interventionism in countries such as the US, as its organisations had an evangelical path to follow, so any form of nationalist movement within nations, and the historical social orders they constituted, was misunderstood as resistance. Only to be treated as a deviation from a presumed universal path of development. Despite all of its faults, LKY needed the US and its support to buy time:

> ***After the collapse of the Soviet Union, Americans have become as
> dogmatic and evangelical as the communists were. They want to promote
> democracy and human rights everywhere, except where it would hurt
> themselves as in the oil-rich Arabian peninsula. Even so, the United
> States is still the most benign of all the great powers, certainly
> less heavyhanded than any emerging great power. Hence, despite
> differences and frictions, all noncommunist countries in East Asia
> prefer America to be the dominant power in the region’s balance of
> power.***

Lee states that America *“expended blood and treasure in Vietnam from 1965 to 1975 and checked the spread of communism.”* America absorbed the military cost of stopping communism in Indochina. Southeast Asia did not have to fight that war alone, nor did it have to lose it. The correlation runs through economics next. Lee writes that American companies first came to the region *“to set up repair facilities to support the U.S. forces in Vietnam.”* These companies did not leave once repair work slowed. They built factories unrelated to the war itself and exported the output back to America. Lee calls this the trigger for the *“industrialisation of Southeast Asia, including Singapore.”*

So the threat and the correlation work in two connected steps. First, the Vietnam War bought time by holding communism away from Singapore’s border. Second, the same war laid the industrial base that let Singapore’s planners act with confidence during that borrowed time. The threat did not just win public consent for control. It paid for the machinery that made the control worth having.

# **What is Singapore for Lee**



To characterise Singapore as an American-forked experiment or a successful instance of Westernisation in the East wouldn’t be enough to understand the nature of the Singaporean project, which has more layered currents. LKY did not seek to imitate any single fixed model, but rather to selectively borrow from different civilisations, appropriating institutional strengths while rejecting their accompanying weaknesses. Singapore emerged as a consciously engineered synthesis: Confucian in its conception of social order, British in administration and statecraft, American in economic understanding, and Japanese in labour ethics and developmental discipline.

At its social foundation lay a distinctly Confucian worldview. Lee consistently rejected the liberal assumption that society is merely an aggregate of autonomous individuals possessing inviolable rights. Instead, he viewed society as an organic moral community held together by hierarchy, duty, family responsibility, and collective obligations. The individual existed within webs of social relationships and obligations rather than as an isolated bearer of rights. This communitarian orientation informed Singapore’s paternalistic governance, its emphasis on social discipline, its restrictions on excessive individualism, and its insistence that order and stability must precede political freedoms. Incidents such as the Michael Fay controversy demonstrated Singapore’s refusal to conform to American liberal prescriptions despite close strategic relations with the United States.

Yet Singapore’s economic philosophy was profoundly influenced by the American experience. Lee deeply admired the dynamism of American capitalism, particularly its openness to innovation, entrepreneurship, global talent, and relentless pursuit of productivity and competitiveness. He recognised that the post-war industrialisation of East Asia itself had been made possible by the American security order and by the opening of American markets to Asian exports. American multinational corporations played a central role in Singapore’s industrial take-off, bringing capital, managerial techniques, and integration into global production networks. Lee accepted the logic of global capitalism, free trade, financial openness, and meritocratic competition, but he consciously rejected the social and cultural consequences of American individualism. In essence, Singapore sought American economic vitality without becoming socially American.

Equally significant was the Japanese influence, which perhaps shaped Singapore more deeply than is generally acknowledged. Lee was profoundly impressed by Japanese discipline, collective responsibility, productivity consciousness, and managerial practices. He contrasted Japanese executives who rose from the factory floor and shared workers’ lives with British managerial elites who remained socially distant from production. Japanese notions of continuous improvement, worker-manager cooperation, national mobilisation, energy conservation, and long-term strategic planning became central inspirations for Singapore’s own developmental institutions. The National Productivity Movement, corporatist labour arrangements, and the broader ethos of disciplined national effort owed much to Japanese examples. Lee admired Japan not as a civilisation to be copied wholesale, but as proof that an Asian society could modernise while preserving social cohesion and cultural confidence. At the same time, Singapore remained deeply British in its administrative inheritance. The rule of law, incorruptible bureaucracy, fiscal conservatism, parliamentary procedure, and elite civil service all emerged directly from British colonial institutions. Yet Singapore arguably preserved these virtues more faithfully than Britain itself. As post-war Britain increasingly abandoned Victorian ideals of discipline, merit, and elite stewardship, Singapore retained and perfected them. Lee believed deeply in the moral obligations of governing elites, rational administration, long-term planning, and the idea that competent institutions could transform society. His political temperament resembled less that of a post-colonial nationalist than that of a nineteenth-century imperial administrator committed to progress, order, and civilisational uplift.

Three first-wave Tiger economies treat their situation as a shared category: states that had to industrialise on borrowed time because each faced a live threat to its continued existence as a separate political entity: Taiwan from the mainland, Korea from the North, Singapore from a region that had just expelled it.

In this sense, Lee Kuan Yew may be regarded as one of the last Victorian politicians. He possessed a distinctly Victorian faith in reason, discipline, progress, public morality, and the duty of elites to guide society. Singapore itself became a curious historical anomaly: perhaps the last genuinely Victorian polity, not in Europe but in Asia. It preserved British administrative virtues after Britain had entered post-imperial decline, fused them with Confucian notions of moral order, infused them with Japanese developmental discipline, and embedded them within an American-led global capitalist system. Singapore thus demonstrated an alternative path to modernity. It challenged the assumption that development necessarily required wholesale Westernisation or liberalisation. Instead, it suggested that modernity could emerge through the selective appropriation and synthesis of multiple civilisational traditions into a coherent and functional national project. Far from being a Western replica, Singapore became one of the most successful examples of post-colonial civilisational bricolage, a state forged from several inheritances yet reducible to none of them.

# **Diversity of Conclusions**

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0R0-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc373b073-4af7-43ef-944c-f619658abb87_1024x1024.png)





***^(Singapore was born from the necessity of making difference inhabit the same space. Lee Kuan Yew’s statecraft was the art of turning a crowd of competing histories into a people with a common horizon.)***

We observe that students of public policy and international relations often tend to reduce their understanding of statecraft to the administration of an island city-state. Diversity is not inherently destabilising. It becomes destabilising when citizens lack a shared civic identity and trust in state institutions. In such conditions, citizens understand themselves primarily through communal affiliations and group identities, negotiating with the state and transforming political life into a competition among groups rather than participation in a common national project. The success of diverse societies depends less on the degree of diversity itself than on the state’s ability to cultivate trust, enforce impartial institutions, and construct a shared civic identity. Where these conditions are absent, diversity ceases to be merely descriptive. It becomes politically salient, therefore encouraging communities to relate to the state as competing corporate entities rather than as equal citizens within a common nation. What most are unable to see is that such an intellectually complacent assertion overlooks the fundamentals of governance itself. Any nation then requires homogenization, not of races or cultures, but of shared space—a common place where the shared space of convergence doesn’t feel like a distant possibility. That’s why the formation of a nation requires convergence; ultimately, it’s a condition in which different social universes become mutually legible, and a deliberate curation of a unified national identity is frequent, with “Size of diversity” invoked as an inherent barrier to emulating this model. Diversity only becomes an insurmountable friction when the civic bond degrades into a purely transactional exchange of rights and the apparatus of the state is reduced to a vehicle for collective mistrust. Suppose the only thing connecting a diverse group of citizens is a transactional legal contract mediated by a government they actively distrust; any difference—whether ethnic, cultural, or ideological— will inevitably widen into an unbridgeable chasm. One can see this model in societies where Feudal groups have a space to act as more than citizens and to assert new contracts with the state.

Whether confronting Malay communalism at home, Islamic solidarities abroad, or Western universalist assumptions regarding China, Lee consistently approached politics through the lens of collective identities and the constraints they imposed upon abstract liberal principles. Individuals might formally possess equal rights, but in practice they continued to act based on inherited loyalties to ethnicity, religion, and civilisation. The task of statesmanship, therefore, was not to abolish these identities but to recognise their enduring power and carefully manage their political consequences.

Lee Kuan Yew was sworn into office on 5 June 1959. He sought a single book that could teach him how to build a nation. He looked for a book that addressed the task of constructing a state whose people spoke multiple languages, traced their ancestry to different homelands, and practised different religions. Frankly, quite the opposite of any Western idea, Singapore stood as a constitutional and political experiment for which history had left few precedents. That absence followed logically from where Western statecraft had developed. Much of its canon evolved within societies that, whatever their internal differences, rested on a broad civilizational continuity: a shared Judeo-Christian heritage, combined with common legal traditions and a dominant linguistic or cultural core running beneath the surface. Classical political thought concerned itself with sovereignty, liberty and the distribution of power within a political community already imagined into being. It rarely asked the prior question: how a nation itself gets forged from populations sharing no single cultural or linguistic identity. While Malays were contesting the history they shared for an Islamic future as superiors and by Chinese who were marching as communists, dreaming of a distinct land—China. The American Constitution, however, marks a departure from this by devising institutional mechanisms — federalism, checks and balances, and the separation of powers — to reconcile competing regional and political interests. Even this unique experiment rests on the idea of a singular political nation accommodating diversity through individual citizenship rather than through any shared national identity built across genuinely distinct civilizational communities. Lee inherited Singapore as a state without a nation. His challenge was to create a national identity among his people. His task therefore extended beyond constitutional design or administrative efficiency to creating a political community where none had previously existed. Governance literature could teach a man how to administer an already-formed state. It had nothing to say about manufacturing cohesion among people separated by language, ethnicity, religion and historical memory.

When Deng arrived in Singapore, Lee Kuan Yew, also a Hakka Chinese, seized the opportunity to deliver a message. If China wanted support from Southeast Asian nations on issues like containing Vietnam’s incursion into Cambodia, then the pattern of China using multiple methods to undermine Southeast Asian governments and pressuring overseas Chinese in non-linear warfare inside ASEAN countries should stop. Lee added that continued use of these tactics not only hardened ASEAN attitudes toward China but also made it increasingly difficult for Chinese minorities in these countries to coexist. Deng listened to these arguments, and within two years, as new Chinese political warfare tactics were being developed, Deng applied these tactics in the ASEAN region.

Lee’s foresight in shaping Chinese behaviour without breaking ties underscored his unique brand of principled pragmatism. He showed that even a small state, if well led, could influence great powers and protect regional stability. Unfortunately, no such conversations took place on the American side, and Chinese non-linear warfare against the West increased steadily during the 1980s. Through the Ye-Xi clique, a network of princelings who fused Party power, military intelligence, and organised crime, Beijing exploited U.S. openness to erode trust in democratic institutions, siphon technology, and entrench influence operations. Where Lee compelled Deng to restrain destabilising behaviour, Washington lacked a statesman who could force a similar recalibration, leaving the U.S. exposed to decades of asymmetric political warfare that continues today.
