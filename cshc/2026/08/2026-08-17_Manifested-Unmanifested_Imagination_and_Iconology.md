# **Introduction**



Following the southwesterly monsoon winds, ships sailing all the way from the Mediterranean Sea and the Persian Gulf awaited the sighting of an elephant carved in rock on an offshore island. The elephant monolith on a rocky cliff signalled the nearing of the much-fabled land, called by many names. Its people called it Bharatvarsha, those from Persia called it Hind, and the visitors from the Hellenistic sphere called it India. Later in the 16th and 17th centuries, the Portuguese voyagers called the island Elephanta. The ancient Indian inscriptions referred to it as Puri, and locals have continued to call it Gharapuri. Behind the elephant sculpture was a centre of flourishing civilisation, with water tanks, colossal sculptures and a longer memory of travellers, religion, patronage, and piety.

Off the Konkan coast, where now stands the metropolis of Mumbai, the island’s hard rocks were chiselled to create space for the Lord of Caves or ‘Guheshvara’. In the early sixth century, master craftsmen, under Kalachuri patronage, began working on a series of structures. The Great Cave at Elephanta, with its monumental sculptures and colossal reliefs, was completed between 530-550 CE and remains one of the earliest and most vibrant artistic expressions of Indian philosophy and the dharmic system. The cave at Elephanta was not an isolated phenomenon but part of a larger socio-cultural network of caves, traceable earliest to the Barabar caves in the north, Bhaja and Karla in the Deccan, and the Sittanavasal caves in the southern part of the country. Scholars have generally held that Elephanta’s immediate predecessors were the caves at Jogeshwari/Yogeshwari located on the mainland coast of Konkan. The cultural milieu of the Elephanta caves spread far and wide in the Deccan, across the caves of Ellora, the temple of Kailashnatha, and the caves at Badami.

With eight high-relief panels, an open-pillared linga shrine, and the colossal multi-faced representation of Shiva at the centre, the complex has attracted artists, photographers, painters, and historians. Apart from its artistic programme, Elephanta has stood out for the philosophical system it embodies. The cave pillars seem to bear the weight of the mountain, allowing devotees to enter and experience Shiva’s transcendental consciousness. The diversity of iconographic representation at Elephanta did not neatly align with any established system of iconology as known from the texts of ancient India. These caves are, however, attributed to Pasupata Shaivism, which considered Shiva as the supreme being and everyone else as an aspect of him. Pasupata Shaivism was founded by a man named Lakulisha, born in Lata desha (modern-day southern Gujarat), who is considered to be the 28th and final incarnation of Shiva. The sect of Lakulisha was influential in the early centuries of the first millennium. Temples to Lakulisha and royal donations to the sect are found across the country, from the Jageshwara Temple Complex of Uttarakhand to the Mukteshwara Temple of Odisha. At Ellora, the Rameshwara cave and Dhumar Lena were also constructed by *pasupatacharyas* and have been crucial to the comparative analysis and artistic reconstruction of the much-damaged reliefs at Elephanta.

The treatment of space at Elephanta conveyed not just material or religious reality but also conceived it as a way to realise Shiva as transcendental consciousness. Stella Kramrisch, an Austrian artist and scholar, saw a graded presence of Shiva at Elephanta. Kramrisch has argued that while Shiva manifests in the eight panels, he remains the unmanifested consciousness in the form of linga. Thus, he is simultaneously present but absent, expressed but unexpressed, and manifested but unmanifested in the cave of Elephanta.

On the art and style of Elephanta, Stella Kramrisch has argued that it is the climax of Indian art, where figures seem to emerge out of the ground. Commenting on the carving style at Elephanta, Parul Pandya Dhar argues that because the rock is not flat, the minor sculptures are carved in depth. It creates a grotesque effect. The central sculpture gives the impression that it is being meditated upon and is not static. The movement and chaos around the central figure further enhances the stillness of the central image. The cave as a whole conveys a sense of manifestation that begins at the centre and then returns.

Kramrisch has argued that the cave at Elephanta follows the *vyakt-avyakt* system of thought, in which Shiva manifests while remaining unmanifested. At Elephanta, Shiva is present at three levels- in the linga form he is the unmanifested whole (avyakt), in the ashtamurti or eight relief panels he is the manifested being (vyakt); and in the colossal Sadashiva monolith he is the transcendental consciousness which is unmanifested in the manifestation (vyakt-avyakt). In the following essay we shall look at the artistic programme at Elephanta in greater detail and understand it as part of the philosophical and literary milieu of its time.

# **Lingamurti: The Unmanifested** 



The complex of Elephanta is double-focused. The first is the colossal Sadashiva, and the second is the mulavigraha, or the linga form. The linga is housed in an open four-pillared hall known as a Sarvatobhadra in Sanskrit texts on vastu. The linga’s energy pervades all directions. The shrine entrance is flanked by two *dwarapalas*, one on each side, equal in height from the base to the ceiling, and they guard the sanctuary. The linga holds the seed which has the power to procreate.

# **Sadashiva: The** ***Vyakt-Avyakt***  ****



In Pasupata Shaiva theology as propounded by Lakulisha, Shiva is the supreme consciousness who manifests himself, but his totality remains unmanifested. In his five-faced representation, known as Sadashiva, each face represents an aspect of the totality. These four faces are also associated with mantras from the Taittiriya Aranyak and represent one of the five *panchabhootas*. The four visible faces are Sadyojata, Aghora, Tatpurusha, and Vamadeva, with the fifth face, Ishana, considered present atop the four heads, facing the sky. Among the *panchabhootas,* or the five elements, Sadyojata is associated with earth, Vamadeva with water, Tatpurusha with air, Aghora with fire and Ishana with ether. The colossal central panel at Elephanta is dedicated to Sadashiva, whose three faces are visible to us: the central face of Tatpurusha; on the right is Aghora; and on the left is Vamadeva. The fourth face merges with darkness in the chamber, and the fifth face of Ishana, representing Shiva’s transcendental nature, can’t be seen by human eyes and is therefore not carved.

The central face of Tatpurusha stands for the fullness of absolute knowledge - peace. The rasa of tranquillity pervades the composition. With a smooth forehead and heavy closed eyelids which touch the weighty lips, it creates a sacred composure. The central face is crowned by a symphony of jewellery rising in cascades of filigree ornaments around the radiating hairlocks, making the *jatamukuta* a symbol of creative energies. The face has elongated earlobes with heavy jewellery intact. Tatpurusha’s left hand holds the *beejapuruka,* a symbol of male fertility and the seed as a source of life and creative energy. The right hand is broken.

From the tripartite crown on the right juts out the fierce Aghora representing Bhairava. His face seems to be charged with energy, and the irascible gaze is fixed upon a serpent that rears out of Bhairava’s hand. His moustache has been twisted by rage, and the sunken-eyed skull stares into nothingness. His crown bears wriggling serpent locks with tender leaves, a raised hood, and a staring skull. On the left emerges, in all grace, Vamadeva, who represents the divine feminine or Uma. Holding a lotus flower in hand, the goddess seems to be absorbed in herself as her gaze is inward-looking. When juxtaposed with Bhairava, Uma’s visage conveys the mystery of the absolute’s coexistence. Vamadeva’s hair is studded with jewels and crowned by an ardhachandra from which beads hang down.

As per the Taittiriya Aranyaka and the Vishnudharmottara Purana, the five faces of Sadashiva are also associated with five of the panchbrahamana mantras. The mantras are as follows:

1.   **Sadyojāta:** सद्योजातं प्रपद्यामि सद्योजाताय वै नमो नमः। भवे भवे नाति भवे
    भवस्व मां भवोद्भवाय नमः॥

> *“ I take refuge in the First Born; verily, I bow to the First Born.
> Do not consign me to birth after birth; Guide me beyond birth. I bow
> to the Causer of birth.”*

2.   **Vāmadeva:** वामदेवायनमो ज्येष्ठाय नमः श्रेष्ठाय नमो रुद्राय नमः कालाय नमः।
    कलविकरणाय नमो बलम्, बलाय नमो बलम्, बलविकरणाय नमो बलम्, बलप्रमथनाय नमः।
    सर्वभूतदमनाय नमो मनोन्मनाय नमः॥

> “*I bow to the Noble One, the Eldest; to the Best, to Rudra and to
> Time, I bow to the Incomprehensible, to Strength, to the Causer of
> various forces, and to the Extender of Strength. I bow to the Subduer
> of all beings, and to the One who kindles the Light.”*

3.   **Aghora:** अघोरेभ्योऽथ घोरेभ्यो अघोरघोरेतरेभ्यः। सर्वतः शर्वः सर्वेभ्यो नमस्ते
    रुद्र रूपेभ्यः॥

> *“ I bow to those not terrible and those who are terrible, and to
> those who are both terrible and not terrible. Everywhere and always,
> Śarva, I bow to all Thy Rudra forms.*”

4.   **Tatpuruṣa:** तत्पुरुषाय विद्महे महादेवाय धीमहि। तन्नो रुद्रः प्रचोदयात्॥

>  *“ May we know that Supreme Person and meditate on that Great God;
> may Rudra impel us!*”

5.   **Īśāna:** ईशान सर्वविद्यानामीश्वरः सर्वभूतानां ब्रह्मादिपति ब्रह्मणोऽधिपतिर्।
    ब्रह्मा शिवो मे अस्तु स एव सदाशिव ओम्॥

> *“Ruler of all knowledge, Master of all beings, Commander of all study
> and devotion, That God Auspicious to me, Be He just so, the
> Ever-Auspicious Om.”*

The sadashiva chapel occupies the central chapel on the southern wall. It is flanked by dwarapalas who stand relaxed, and their facial expressions resemble those of Mahadeva, making the point that only Shiva is the essence pervading all. Adjoining the central chapel, the side panels depict Shiva Ardhanarishwara and Siva-Gangadharamurti, further highlighting the vyakt-avyakt nature of the cave shrine.

However, the sculptural and iconological programme at Elephanta doesn’t follow a strict textual adherence. While texts suggest placing the central Sadashiva image in the western direction, the cave at Elephanta places it on the southern wall, making Tatpurusha face east rather than north. This deviation from the *sastric* norm was not accidental but constrained by the island’s geology. In a way, the natural, or *prakritti,* element dictated how the form of supreme consciousness would be visualised/realised. This follows the view of Ananda K. Coomaraswamy, who, in his analysis of the Nataraja form of Shiva, has argued that the cosmic dancer is limited and held within the circumference of flames, which represent the element of *Parvati,* who herself is a manifestation of *prakritti.*

This observation also aligns with K.C. Bhattacharya’s classification of four stages of consciousness: empirical, objective, subjective (or spiritual), and transcendental thought. Bhattacharya has argued that the highest form of consciousness is transcendence, which cannot be expressed in any word. When expressed, such consciousness loses its essence and slips down. By not carving the ethereal element of Ishana, the sculptor reiterates the same. By following the rock’s geology in carving the cave and placing the panels, it becomes even more evident that the transcendental can’t be expressed in any language, be it words, bodily gestures, or stone. The *avyakt* aspect can only be realised from within.

From the vyakt-avyakt colossal Sadashiva, we now move on to analyse panels which depict Shiva in various forms, the vyakt or the manifestations.

# **Ashtamurti: The Manifestations** 



The cave at Elephanta is approached from the east. The path of *pradikshna,* or ritual circumambulation, would have been anti-clockwise, as per the Pasupata sect, which sought to emphasise receding oneself from worldly affairs.

***Ravana-anugrahamurti***: The first of the eight panels one encounters on his right is of a many-armed man trying to uplift a mountain upon which sits a divine couple. It is the scene of the beneficence of Ravana. The mountain is Kailasa, the divine abode of Mahadeva, in whose lap sits Uma, the consort. Below the mountain, Ravana tries to uplift it, referring to the Puranic episode in which Ravana, overwhelmed by the prowess of his shoulder and bhakti for Mahadeva, seeks to uproot Kailasa and place it in Lanka, his country. To humble Ravana and make him realise the limitation of his strength, Shiva presses his toe upon the Kailasa under whose weight Ravana gets trapped. Humbled and trapped under the mountain, Ravana sings a praise for Shiva’s supremacy, after which point the lord appears, rescues him and blesses him.

Charles Dillard Collins, in his iconological reading of the Elephanta cave and arrangement of panels, has argued that the placement of *Ravana-anugrahamurti* announced the sectarian superiority of the Pasupatas. However, in my understanding, this episode and its descriptions suggest that the devotee should leave his ego, whatever it may be, outside when he enters the complex. Ravana, who owned a Lanka of Gold, whose strength was unmatched and who was indeed the greatest devotee of Shiva, could not overcome his ego; eventually Shiva had to humble him.

Moving ahead in the pradakshina, the devotee would have encountered Shiva in two contrasting forms. The Lord of Yogis, who has renounced his senses from the world, and Shiva as Lord of Dancers, who expresses his divine or whole being in the form of a cosmic dance. A verandah was added in front of the Yogeshwara and Nrittamurti to provide space for people to pray.

***Shiva Yogeshwara:*** Situated on the side of the Northern entrance, on a lotus emerging from the ground, Shiva is seated in the yogic pose of padmasana, unmoved by the happenings around him but being aware of them. A pair of nagas hold the lotus stalk. He wears a highly ornamental jatamukuta with hair falling upon his shoulders. Around him, a number of celestial beings and gods such as Brahma and Vishnu can be identified. His eyes are closed, with a facial expression resembling that of Tatpurusha of colossal Sadashiva, conveying all-pervading peace and tranquillity. Like other panels, this one is also damaged, but the mood has remained intact, exuding the same rasa as it would have millennia ago. Some scholars have argued that this panel represents Shiva-Yogeshwara and Lakulisha at the same time. Since Lakulisha is considered the 28th avatara of Shiva, his representation as a yogi is possible, but he is generally shown holding a mace or club, which is missing here.

Opposite yogic Shiva is Natesa, whose cosmic dance precedes creation and follows the annihilation of the material world.

***Nrittamurti:*** Indian traditions have long given immense importance to dance and music. One of the world’s oldest treatises on music has been the Samaveda. Indian sage Bharatamuni saw the world as animated by an undercurrent of a singular cosmic rhythm which pervades all living beings. He understood that by aligning oneself with the cosmic music and following the dance, one can achieve liberation. Thus, he composed the Natyashastra, a 2nd-century BCE Sanskrit treatise on dance, drama, and music. Bharatamuni saw in Mahadeva the essence of all the arts and envisioned him as a cosmic dancer, depicted in a relief at Elephanta as the Nrittamurti. This imagery later evolved into Nataraja iconography in the plains of Kaveri under the patronage of Chola kings and has survived to this day as Shiva’s cosmic representation.

In the Kurma Purana, the lord of yogis danced with the entire cosmos as the scene, alongside Vishnu, and became one with him. Shiva’s Ananda Tandava, or the dance of bliss in the hall of consciousness, is Shiva’s dance within the heart of man. Nataraja’s dance shows fivefold activity and expresses divine totality. It conveys the fivefold action of creation, maintenance, dissolution, veiling-unveiling, and liberation. At the end of time, Siva will dance again. With the stamping of his foot, mountains shall be tossed. His matted hairs will scatter the stars around. This will be the dance of Shiva as kala-mahakaala, the destroyer, the destroyer of destruction.

In the relief at Elephanta, Shiva is shown in the dwibhanga stance, with his now-damaged legs in a chaturtandava pose, creating a rhombus-like shape with one heel lifted. The facial expression here resembles the composure and quiddity of Tatapurusha’s face from the colossal Sadashiva. The figure has eight arms, of which most are broken. In his upper left hand, he seems to hold a cloth, which is generally read as representing the act of removing the illusion of maya. In his upper right hand, he holds an axe with a naga around it. His lower right hand is stretched across the body, which would have been in the gaja-hasta mudra. According to Natyashastra, such a posture resembles Lalita-tandava. The grandness of Shiva is conveyed through his scale, in which his jatamukuta touches the top of the grotto.

Parvati, the cause of the cosmic dance and representation of the divine feminine, stands on the left. The grotto around the central imagery is populated by gods and divine figures such as Indra on his elephant, Vishnu on his Garuda, Brahma seated on a lotus and Ganesha. Behind Brahma, a sage couple can also be spotted. On the ground, an emaciated figure can also be noticed, which most probably represents Bhringi. Around the jatamukuta are the celestial beings who generally populate the entire upper register, but given the grandness of the cosmic dancer, they have also been moved aside. In the right corner, a male figure seems seated, possibly Sage Tandu, who learnt the cosmic dance and carried the knowledge for the liberation of society.

Moving ahead towards the western portico, from Nrittamurti, which permeates a sense of awe, one encounters Shiva as a ferocious being slaying the demon Andhaka. This ferocious being of his is coupled with Shiva as a tender, young bridegroom accepting the hand of Parvati in marriage. The visual scheme and emotive appeal of both these panels are so starkly different that one immediately feels transported to two different mental states. This contrast resonates with the faces of Aghora and Vamadeva placed opposite to each other in the central monolith of Sadashiva.

***Andhakasuravadha:*** The chapel, much destroyed in its present form, gives us a sense of how the *krodha* or *ugra* *bhava* of Shiva is manifested. Eight-armed Shiva bears a cruel expression on his face. His jatamukuta is adorned with skulls, a cobra, and an ardhachandra, further adding to his belligerent gaze. In two of his intact hands, in one he holds a sword and a bowl; in the other, to slay and collect the blood of the demon. In his upper two hands, he holds the skin of an elephant, Nila, who was Andhaka’s friend and would have come for battle before him. The topmost register of the chapel is populated with celestial figures witnessing the act of slaying demons.

***Kalyansundaramurti:*** The union of Mahadeva and Uma, the daughter of mountains, is depicted in the scene of Kalyanasundaramurti. Shiva occupies a central position with a jatamukuta and a halo to emphasise his divine status. Next to him stands Uma, her head slightly tilted towards Shiva, who holds her hand (now broken) in the moment of panigrahan sanskara. Behind Uma stands Himavan, the king of the Himalayas and her father, who facilitates the exchange of hands. In the right corner, emerging from the wall, is Chandra, the moon god, carrying a pot of water for the panigrahana sanskara. In the lower register, sits Brahma as the officiating priest. The celestial figures of gandharvas, vidyadharas, and apsaras in the upper register celebrate, in an eclectic mood, the coming together of the divine consorts.

In the pradakshina ahead, the devotee comes towards the triptych on the southern wall which has the colossal Sadashiva in the centre flanked by dwarapalas and adjoined by two more relief panels, Siva-Ganghadharmurti and Ardhanarishwara. Charles Dillard Collins, in his analysis of space at Elephanta, argues that cells have been attached on both sides of the central panel. These cells would have provided space for the ascetics to sit.

***Siva-Gangadhara***: How did the ancient Indian visualise the myth of Ganga’s descent? How would a river and the force she brings along from heaven have been thought of? The chapel to the left of the colossal Sadashiva hints at that. The chapel’s central figure is a four-armed Shiva in abhanga, tilted to the left. From his jatamukata, he stretches out a hair-lock with his hand, meant to capture the flow of the river, which descends from heaven with force and, if left unchecked, can cause havoc upon the earth. Above Shiva’s crowned head is Ganga, a triple-busted female figurine whose clothes flutter due to the force with which she descends. On the right of Shiva is King Bhagiratha, whose penance made the descent of Ganga possible.

The chapel is made more emotive and dramatic by the presence of Parvati, who bends on the opposite side of Shiva, creating a visual gap and an allegorical reference to her jealousy towards another woman who will rest on Shiva’s head. In the gap between Shiva and Parvati, the sculptor placed a gana figure who looks at the scene of descent with astonishment. Kneeling Bhagiratha and the astonished Gana capture how different people would have witnessed and felt about this supernatural phenomenon. In the topmost register are celestial figures such as Vidyadharas and apsaras, elated and overjoyed to witness the cosmic act.

***Ardhanarishwara:*** Carved with grace, elegance and fine balance, the Ardhanarishwara panel represents the non-dualism of consciousness and the material world. Standing in tribhanga, the male aspect of Mahadeva leans back on Nandi, his bull, to balance the exaggerated amplitude of Parvati’s left hip and breast. According to Stella Kramrisch, the god and animal create one whole, with Nandi’s figure supplementing Shiva’s shape. In this visual metaphor, Nandi belongs more to the male aspect, conveying Shiva’s being. The goddess’s hand holds a mirror, a symbol and restatement of Shiva’s being. Centuries later, writing in the valley of Kashmir, Abhinavagupta’s hymns praised Shiva as a mirror of undifferentiated consciousness.

The recess of the chapel is populated by several celestial figures with their focus fixed on Ardhanarishwara with Nandi. The frenetic mood of the vidyadharas, gandharvas and apsaras in the topmost register of the recess is juxtaposed with the tranquillity and composure of the central figurine. Around the cosmic form of Ardhanarishwara are the depictions of Kartikeya, Vishnu aloft his garuda, Brahma seated on a lotus and Indra on his elephant. The goddess’s female attendants, Jaya and Vijaya, are also present in the panel.

# **A Note on the Iconology of Elephanta Caves:** 



When compared with the later temples at Khajuraho and Thanjavur, which follow a coherent iconological scheme, the iconography of Elephanta doesn’t strictly follow any textual source. Prof. Parul Pandya Dhar remarks that the period under consideration was marked by vibrant sculptural imaginations and a coherent iconological programme developed late, from the 10th century onwards or so. However, scholars like S. Kramrisch and C. D. Collins have attempted to understand the meaning and underlying logic behind the placement of these icons.

Flanking the northern entrance are the depictions of Shiva in two of his most iconic forms, the cosmic dancer and the meditator. Charles Dillard Collins comments that the planners of Elephanta placed Yogeshwara, the immovable aspect of the god, complementing Nrittamurti, the god’s eternal movement. By doing so, art paired the state of self-trance with entrancement.

Regarding the Andhakasuravadha episode, Collins followed the description as found in the Vamana Purana. He narrates that when Andhaka troubled the gods, they prayed to Shiva, who first meditated and then performed a cosmic dance to battle the demon Andhaka. He slayed Andhaka, removed his ignorance, and turned him into a gana. In the Andhakasurvadha episode, Shiva performed an ominous dance holding the skin of the elephant Nila in his hands. As Collins notes, the dance of creation is followed by that of destruction, resembling the cosmic cycle of creation and destruction of the world, in which Shiva, the infinite consciousness, remains above and unmoved as a Yogi.

Kramrisch, in her eloquence, described the biaxial plan of the cave holding three levels of meaning in relation to how Shiva has been imagined. On the first level she notes:

“The highest transcendental level is realised in the garbhagrha, where Siva is invisibly present in the linga. The simple stereometry of the linga denotes Siva’s unmanifest presence. The austere square of the garbhagriha enshrines it. Nothing but the linga is in this innermost sanctuary…The innermost sanctuary of the great cave temple of Elephanta encloses this linga in its simple, abstract shape, a symbol of the unmanifest transcendental Siva.”

Regarding the Astamurtis, Kramrisch notes that Shiva manifests in eight contexts, each evoking a timeless aspect of his being. He is the yogin, the bridegroom, the dancer, the god whose half is woman, the one who holds Ganga in his hairlocks, the one who slays demons, and the one who resides on Kailasha. He is each one and all the others.

On the colossal Sadashiva, which forms the central aspect of the cave, Kramrisch described the central face of Tatapurusha as forming the monolith, arranged around the shape of a linga. She notes, “Seen in profile, the central head, with its high coiffure (jatamukuta), has the shape of a linga. Even in a front view, the face and the bejewelled crown are linga-shaped. The central head is set at a right angle to the profile heads. Together, their curved contour suggests the domelike termination of a linga.”

Stella Kramrisch has also drawn our attention to the difficulty in visualising Sadashiva and corroborating the five faces with the respective mantras. She notes that the mantras mentioned above follow a vertical order. From the earth element of Sadyojata to Ishana, the ether element, the mantras follow a cosmogonic order that traverses the cosmos along the vertical and forms the “body” of Siva, the totality of Siva manifesting as cosmos. When spoken in the medium of stone, these mantras lost their cosmogonic vertical sequence as they became represented as faces in sculpture. Horizontal alignment placed them in the four directions in conformity with cosmic order; the fifth (invisible) head retained its hierarchical position above them.

Shiva’s presence in the great cave is not only graded but also contradictory. Stella Kramrisch has argued that the polarities of fire and water, sun and moon, male and female coexist within Shiva. Should one cancel out the other, at that very moment creation itself would cease to exist and the world would be lost. Shiva’s orbit is marked by cooperating opposites such as snakes and jewels. What is death or poison to the ignorant is bliss or release of meaning for the knowing ones. He includes the ultimate pair: creation and the uncreate. He is the consciousness. The Great Yogi carries within himself the consciousness of the Uncreate, the Absolute. In meditation, he withdraws within himself; he returns to the uncreate. Opening his “sun and moon” eyes, he sees himself in everything that exists. He is Astamurti, Existence itself. Siva Astamurti is the Existence: Shiva Yogeshwara, the consciousness of Existence and of the Universe. Siva in dance renders himself. His dance complements his tapas. Both are forms of creative fire, the one turned inward in trance, the other turned outward in ecstasy.

Kramrisch has further elaborated that Sadashiva permeates the created cosmos, is one with it, and transcends it. The aniconic linga in the garbhagriha symbolises the immanence of transcendental Shiva. The gigantic relief expands the linga shape to become an image of Shiva’s presence in man and cosmos, situated on the “third level of meaning” implied in the total conception of the cave temple. Thus Kramrisch concludes that

“Shiva is triply present in his great cave: imperceptible and transcendental within the linga; in his quintessential being, emanating from the linga in the colossal sculpture; and manifest in the eight sculptural configurations carved in the grottos.”

# **The Cultural Milieu of Elephanta**



A monument or structure, even on an island off the coast, is not an isolated entity but part of the larger cultural milieu of its time. We have already considered above how Elephanta was part of the subcontinental rock-cut caves network and how its sectarian affiliation had a presence across the Indian subcontinent. To understand how Shiva was imagined and understood in the time period of Elephanta, we shall consider the epics and puranas.

From the time of the Vedas to that of the Puranas, an unbroken oral and written tradition about Shiva’s themes continued. In the Satapatha Brahmana of the Yajur Veda, he is invoked as Rudra, being copper-red, with a white hue throat and white n, andeck w a thousandand eyes. He holds a bow and quiver of arrows, mounted on a bull, wearing the auspicious sacred thread, and covering himself in the hide of a tiger or lion.

Within him exists the duality of being benign and malignant at the same time, foreshadowing the later concept of Sadasiva, in which the faces of Aghora and Vamadeva coexist. In the Vedic corpus, he is also the lord of creatures, pasu-pati, a concept that later evolves into the main principle of the Lakulisa-Pasupata sect as the liberator of enchained souls. Rudra is lord and leader of thieves, robbers, pilferers, pickpockets, plunderers, and cheats, which, in addition to showing the dark side of Rudra-Siva, gives a precedent for sanctioning the outlandish behaviour prescribed later for adherents of the Lakulisa-Pasupatas.

In the third chapter of the Svetasvatara Upanishad of the Krishna-Yajurveda, the supreme being, or Brahman, is one with Rudra-Siva, who creates all beings and merges them at the end of time. He has an eye, a face, an arm, and a foot on every side; he created the golden germ (Hiranyagarbha); he dwells in the mountains and holds an arrow; he is lord of bipeds and quadrupeds. The Upanishada thus says -

*“He is the first-born of the universe, the eternal witness. Realizing Rudra as the Self, the yogi attains unity with the supreme Brahman.*

*The universe is pervaded by Rudra, the Supreme. Knowing Him as the Self, the wise overcome ignorance and attain liberation, free from duality.”*

Moving beyond the Vedic corpus, we encounter epic and Puranic literature, which offer much more vivid portrayals of the narrative. Among the two epics, the Mahabharata discusses the Fall of Ganga and, to a lesser extent, Shiva as half-female and Shiva with Uma upon the mountain. Ramayana, in its books one and seven, both later additions, elucidates the episode of Ravana shaking the mountain Kailasha and little on the marriage of Shiva and Parvati. Among the Puranas, the Vamana and Linga Puranas have helped in reading the narrative at Elephanta. However, both were completed long before the excavation of Elephanta. The Linga Purana of ca. 600-1000 contains substantial material relating to the Pasupata ritual and sect. It helps to clarify the panels of Siva as Lakulisha, the dance of Siva, the marriage of Siva, and Siva as half-female and, to a lesser extent, the Andhaka story, the fall of Ganga, and the Mahadeva bust.

While Stella Kramrisch called Elephanta the climax of ancient Indian art, we do not have extant textual materials around the episodes depicted there. This suggests a peculiar possibility: that *sthapatis* planned and carved the sculptures at Elephanta before any detailed textual source was in circulation. Thus, Elephanta drew upon the long tradition of oral narratives and from one more unorthodox source- the works of Mahakavi Kalidasa.

In the period under consideration, the patrons, planners and the common population were surely aware and critical appreciators of Kalidasa’s work. For Collins, Kalidasa’s literature embodies hallmarks of the classical Gupta period such as a revival of the past, restrained innovation, religious tolerance, and beauty reflecting harmony through ritual. It is presumed that the patrons of such a stupendous project as Elephanta would have been sophisticated in their knowledge of classical Sanskrit drama and poetry as well as religious sources and that, perhaps in this case anyway, the two coalesced to result in a remarkable moment of equilibrium in the arts.

The tolerance characteristic of religious sects under the Gupta rulers was also a trait of Kalidasa, who invoked Vishnu and Brahma as well as Siva in his works. According to D.H.H. Ingalls, for Kalidasa Siva was the manifestation which represented the “reconciliation of good and evil, of beauty and ugliness, of life and death, that made the anticipation of retirement unnecessary, for the vision solved all problems and could transmute a man’s suffering into joy.” Thus, a bearing of Kalidasa’s literary oeuvre on the harmony of opposites at Elephanta can be argued for.

# **Siva, Shraavan and the Civilisation at Elephanta** 



The monsoon held a unique place within India’s cultural and philosophical milieu. The monsoon winds that brought traders also marked the beginning of Chaturmaas. Ascetics, monks and saints sought refuge in caves on hillsides known as viharas. These viharas were not isolated retreats but often spaces filled with debates, devotion, and visits by the royal and laity seeking direction and divine benediction. However, in the rugged terrain of the Deccan and its hard rocks, collecting freshwater would have been an important task. At the Bhaja caves, one comes across a water tank which collected fresh rainwater and helped the community sustain itself during the monsoon.

This year, before the onset of the monsoon, Elephanta also revealed deeper and hitherto unknown aspects of its layered past. The complex at Elephanta, located on an island, was not an offshoot but part of the larger Indian civilisation. Recent discoveries by the Archaeological Survey of India and Deccan College, Pune, have unearthed a massive [stepped reservoir](https://www.thehindu.com/society/history-and-culture/steps-into-past-1500-year-old-reservoir-unearthed-on-elephanta-island/article70839592.ece) on the island. This discovery of a water tank opens a momentous chapter in writing the history of Elephanta and ancient India. The tank is a massive T-shaped stepped water reservoir. This 1,500-year-old structure measures approximately 14.7 by 6.7 to 10.8 metres and was built from stone blocks brought over from the mainland. Excavators uncovered twenty perfectly aligned steps. The fact that the stone used to build it was not indigenous to the island points to complex planning and logistical manoeuvring by its ancient builders. Experts opine that the water tank was built to collect and store fresh rainwater, which would otherwise have slipped into the sea.

This stepped reservoir can be considered the earliest in the network of stepped wells which abound from the 7th century onwards in western India. Excavations have also revealed a dyeing vat for textiles, large storage pots, thousands of sherds of Mediterranean amphorae and West Asian torpedo jars which conclusively suggest that Elephanta was not just a sacred cosmopolis but an industrial and maritime trade centre. These findings place Elephanta within the ancient Indian Ocean world, with links extending to Rome and Mesopotamia. Since geological studies suggest that the modern Mumbai coastline must have been even further out in the ocean than it is today, Elephanta would have served as an entrepot in the western Indian Ocean. The island is thus revealing itself and its deeper layers of the past, making the present an epiphany of the *vyakt-avyakt*. A lot remains to be known about Elephanta.

With the scenery covered in dark green and streams gushing out of the hills, the arrival of monsoon in India has been celebrated as the coming of life, especially in regions like the Deccan, which lacked perennial rivers. When men, women and children, overjoyed with the rains and bountiful crops, danced and sang, how could their god have stayed away from the ecstasy?

In the Skanda Purana, with the beginning of the monsoon, gods assembled on Mount Mandara to witness Siva dance for the pleasure of the goddess. Musical instruments arrived in thousands. Bhutas and ganas assembled around the yogi. Siva appeared as Mahanata, the cosmic dancer. Five-headed, ten-armed, ash-smeared, emerald-crested, wearing garlands and armlets, Siva’s body expanded through the three worlds. Mountains resounded with the beat of Siva’s feet. Parvati, appeased by Siva, becomes one with the dancer. They continue to illuminate the cosmos, adorned with a garland of one half skulls and the other half flowers.
