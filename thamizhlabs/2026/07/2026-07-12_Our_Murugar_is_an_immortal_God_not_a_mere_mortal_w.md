Dhanush recently announced his next collaboration with Dravidawood director Vetrimaaran, with a teaser titled “Thamizh Murugan” that has set social media on fire. The project draws its story from a book of the same name by the writer Arivumathi, whose stated aim was to recover the “original” Sangam-era or prehistoric Murugan. The teaser shows a chiseled, muscular Murugar seated on an elephant, wielding a spear, with no religious symbols in sight. Let’s dismantle the book’s arguments one by one, and then take a look at why they’re doing this.

#### Distortion \#1: Murugar was a Tamil warrior king, not a God



Arivumathi’s book (pamphlet would be more fitting) starts by saying **Murugan is a Thamizh Kadavul who alone is an exception** to the rule of Gods having religious identity. He claims Murugar has only ethnic identity. He calls Murugar as Dravidian dheivam, giving a brand new definition to the word dheivam as “warrior”.

Claiming Murugar as irreligious Dravidian warrior king, not God

**If hero-cult deification were the norm**, Tamil Nadu should be littered with temples to celebrated warrior-kings like Karikalan, Rajaraja Chola, or Senguttuvan. It is not. Inscriptions show these kings building temples to gods, not becoming the objects of temple worship themselves. The Chola and Pandya kings who Arivumathi’s model predicts should have become deities remain, quite comfortably, historical kings. **Again, why is Murugan alone an exemption, then?**

If Murugan had genuinely begun as an extraordinary historical king whose memory later hardened into divinity, we’d expect some trace of that transition: an intermediate layer where he shows up merely as a celebrated ruler before slowly picking up divine status. There is no such layer. The earliest literary references we have already treat Murugan as a full-fledged divine being: tied to a specific landscape (the Kurinji hills), honoured through both Vedic and tribal ritual, carrying his own iconography (peacock mount, rooster flag), and wielding supernatural attributes (six faces, a divine spear). He arrives in the record already a god. There’s no warrior-king phase to dig up.

Arivumathi leans on Purananooru elsewhere to make his case, so let’s quote his own source against his argument. In song 56, poet Nakkeerar compares a Pandya king to the “Big 4” deities:

> 1.  blue-throated, matted-haired Shiva;
>
> 2.  plough-wielding, fair-skinned Balarama;
>
> 3.  dark-hued, Garuda-mounted Vishnu; and
>
> 4.  bright-looking, peacock-mounted Muruga.

By Arivumathi’s own logic, are we now to believe Shiva, Thirumaal, and Balarama were also historical Tamil kings quietly promoted to divinity? Purananooru doesn’t call Muruga a warrior here. It calls him a God, in the same breath as three deities nobody disputes.

#### Distortion \#2: Murugar was the Lord of Neithal (coast) more than Kurinji (hill)



Having built his warrior-king narrative, Arivumathi now needs to dress it up so it doesn’t look like absolute balderdash (if it wasn’t already). Who was this warrior born to? Kotravai, goddess of the Paalai (desert) region. Fine. Which region did he rule? Enter the submerged, mythical Kumari Kandam continent. Once this absurdity door is opened, Arivumathi lets his imagination run: a warrior king defending his clan against a rival clan led by Sooran, deploying both elephants on land and ships at sea.

The main problem now? Tolkappiyam, the oldest Tamil text available. It calls Muruga (Seyon) as the Lord of Kurinji (hills). Why would a hill-tribe deity need a navy? A coastal tribe would need one; a hill tribe wouldn't. Second problem: Sangam poetry already assigns the coastal landscape (Neithal) to a different deity entirely: Varunan. Arivumathi's creative solution is to simply make Murugan the Lord of Neithal too, which leaves us with five Sangam landscapes and somehow only three Lords between them, once you throw “Sanskritic” Indra too out for good measure. You’ve already entered Dravidian LaLa land, so please forget logic when traveling through this magical territory. Five Sangam Landmasses with 3 lords, ogay?

He cites *Silapadhikaram* 23.188 to claim the submerged Kumari Kandam legend. Read the actual verse, though, and the scene is different. Kannagi, searching for her husband, walks fourteen days along the banks of the Vaigai until she reaches the sacred hill of Neduvel, a temple to Murugan. The verse describes a Murugan who once tore into the sea to destroy the Asuras (the word Ilango uses is *avunar*).

Poet Kaduvan Ilaveyinaar tells the same story in the Sangam anthology *Paripaadal*, describing Murugan’s spear defeating the very same “magical Avunars.” This is the Surapadman legend (the Asura who fled to the sea and took the form of a tree) and its iconography lines up exactly with the Skanda Purana’s account of the same battle. Arivumathi blatantly distorts this as a Neithal tribe king fighting using a navy. How sacrilegious!

#### Distortion \#3: Murugar ruled the submerged continent of Kumari Kandam aka Lemuria



Arivumathi quotes Silapadhigaaram (11.19–20) yet again to claim that Murugar ruled the Kumari Kandam, as described by Ilango. This is where Arivumathi’s cunning comes across. The quoted verse was actually said by an elderly Brahmin named Maangaattu Maraiyon (Marai is the pure Tamil word for Vedas), who introduces himself to Kovalan by praising his Pandya king. This was the Brahmin who shared the route to Madurai to Kovalan and Kannagi.  
The irony of quoting a Brahmin’s word to make your Dstock deception argument ;)

> The Brahmin praises the Pandya king thus: "Hail the Chandravanshi
> (திங்கள் செல்வன் திருக்குலம்) king, ruler of the south, who added to it the
> Northern Ganga and Himalayas, and who, to show his prowess to other
> kings, threw his spear which had the power to consume the river
> Pahruli and Kumari region and adjoining hills. Long live the king who
> wears the garland of the rain-controlling Indra."

This is obviously a poetic praise of a king Ugra Pandyan (not Murugar), whose legend of stemming the tide with his spear is mentioned in Thiruvilayadal Puranam too. If Arivumathi distorts this as King Murugar who ruled the submerged Kumari Kandam, does he also accept Murugar as a Chandravanshi Pandya king who wears the garland of the rain cloud controlling Indra, from the same verse? Cherrypicked distortions won’t cut it.

#### Distortion \#4: Murugar was the only son of Kotravai



In Dravidian LaLa land, only Murugan is worshipped, his elder brother Ganesha is a Marathi God, Shiva is a North Indian God, so they are all cancelled. So Arivumathi makes the claim that this human warlord Muruga was the ONLY son of Kotravai, by quoting Thirumurugaatrupadai 258, written by Nakeerar (also a Brahmin). But here is the distortion - Nakkeerar only states Muruga as son of Kotravai, this “ONLY” qualifier was added by Arivumathi. He also conveniently avoids discussing paternity of his Dravidian Muruga, as it opens inconvenient doors to North Indian Gods like Shiva.

The same Thirumurugaatrupadai, by the same poet, goes on to describe Murugan with six faces (verse 95): one of which delights specifically in receiving Vedic yagna offerings chanted by Brahmins. If we're meant to believe this is a reliable historical record of a prehistoric Dravidian king, someone owes us an explanation for how that Lemurian king was born with six faces, and why a purely secular warrior would have a face devoted to enjoying fire-ritual chants, an “Aryan” import. Arivumathi has nothing to say about this part of the poem, because it does not fit his agenda.

It gets worse for Arivumathi. The same Nakkeerar records a festival procession at Aavinankudi (today’s Palani), describing it as a holy place with temples for all four great gods Vishnu, Shiva, Balarama, and Murugan. He records a festival procession where Lord Vishnu comes first behind his Garuda banner, then three-eyed Shiva with his Nandi flag, then Indra, celebrated for a hundred Ashwamedha sacrifices, then Murugan, with four-faced Brahma presiding over the whole procession. That is not the record of a secular hero-cult being slowly deified. That’s a full-blown, multi-deity temple festival, with Murugan sitting comfortably among gods nobody else disputes.

It gets even worse for him. Silapadhigaaram’s Vettuva vari verses describe Kotravai unambiguously as the consort of Shiva, slayer of Mahishashura. Do read for yourself. Ofcourse, our Murugar is the son of Parvati, who blessed our God of war with the divine spear that slay demons like Sooran.

#### Distortion \#5: Tamils opposed grain offerings in worship



One of the sharper misreadings involves Purananooru 335, a poem about *nadukal* worship: the Tamil custom of erecting and honouring memorial stones for fallen warriors. Arivumathi casts the poet here as a proto-rationalist rebel, pushing back against an “Aryan” ritual of offering grain (*nel*) at these stones.

Read the poem, and it says the exact opposite. The poet’s actual point is that there is no better way to honour fallen warriors than to place grain offerings on their hero-stones. It’s an endorsement, not a protest. Grain offerings run right through Sangam poetry and Silapadhikaram itself: Kannagi’s own wedding, conducted by a Vedic Brahmin under the Rohini nakshatra, includes the *paaligai mulai kudam niraththal* rite, where pots are filled with nine grains, wheat among them. Arivumathi’s answer, when confronted with this, is to call it a “later distortion.” No manuscript basis, no textual argument. Just a label, applied whenever the primary source refuses to cooperate with his agenda.

#### Distortion \#6: Aloe vera, coconuts, and other tsunami survival kits



Then comes the part of the book that stops pretending to be historiography and starts reading like speculative fiction. Tamils keep aloe vera by the doorstep, Arivumathi says, as tsunami first aid. Coconuts sit outside homes not to ward off the evil eye, but to provide milk after a disaster wipes out nursing mothers. Kavadi-bearing memorializes ancient migrants hauling their belongings out of a drowning Kumari Kandam, as if it were an act of mourning a sad memory. How sacriliegeous!

#### Why They’re Doing This



None of this is happening in a vacuum. Reading gods as deified mortals is an old interpretive habit going back at least to Euhemerus, who argued the Greek Olympians began as flesh-and-blood kings. They eventually used this technique to de-sacralize pagan gods and convert them to the cult of their ONE TRUE GOD.

You can see the same playbook here:

> First they will call a God Muruga as a Tamil ancestor who's worshipped
> by an ethnicity, but also respected by other religionists as a
> Muppaattan.   
>   
> Then they will reduce the Muppaatan to a secular philosopher whose way
> of life should be followed by all, like Valluvar or Vallalaar.  
>   
> Then they will strip him of all religious symbols and add him to their
> pantheon of saints. They will write books and Wikipedia articles
> saying St Murugan met St Thomas in the hill and St Murugar generously
> offered the St Thomas Mount to him.  
>   
> Then they will call Thai Poosam a secular Tamil festival, even
> lighting lamps in their places of worship in honor of St Muruga,
> offering Beep as padayal to him saying it is ok in their indigenous
> tradition and ask you to respect diversity.

This is how they dilute and steal and denigrate other gods, because their loyalty is to their ONE true God only.

#### CONCLUSION



Don't let your love for Tamil or Murugar blind you from seeing this disrespectful sacrilege for what it is. ஆசாமி ஆசாமி தான், சாமி சாமி தான்.  
  
Thamizh Kadavul Murugar is dear to us and his worship may have begun at the ancient town of Tiruchendur/Thiruparankundram, but he is a pan-Indian God of war worshipped by all language speakers - Kannada, Malayalam, Telugu, Hindi, English and even Russian.   
Our Murugar needs absolutely no "ancestry" certificate from those who do not worship him or from atheist comrades. Its not a good idea to provoke the God of war's anger.
