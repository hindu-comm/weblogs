The gap is not glorious

I received the Call for Abstracts below from Scott Stroud (University of Texas at Austin). He added, “The call for abstracts is deliberately wide-ranging, since there are so many useful angles to consider here. Approaches from a range of different scholarly fields are also welcome.”

It sounds like a fascinating project, and I encourage our blog’s readers to consider submitting abstracts!

> **Call for Abstracts**

> ***Pragmatism and India: Histories and Futures***

> Edited by Scott R. Stroud & Danish Hamid

> Pragmatism is an American tradition of philosophy that has made
> wide-ranging impacts into American politics, educational practices,
> and intellectual debates. It includes a diverse array of thinkers as
> John Dewey, William James, Charles S. Peirce, Jane Addams, Richard
> Rorty, Cornel West, W.E.B. DuBois, and more. It has also traveled to
> various parts of the world, growing and evolving and featuring in
> debates in international arenas. For instance, John Dewey’s students
> from China such as Hu Shih spawned many political reforms in China in
> the early 1900s, and set the context for Mao’s own philosophy of
> revolution. William James had followers and interlocutors who
> developed his vision of pragmatism in new ways in Italy and Britain.
>
> Recent attention is turning to the applicability of pragmatism to
> India. What stories wait to be told if we investigate the historical
> connections of pragmatism to India, or consider its usefulness and
> limitations in engaging contemporary problems in Indian democracy?
>
> This edited book project seeks planned chapters that explore a range
> of pragmatism’s past and future connections to India. There are a
> range of approaches that articles can take. A few useful paths to
> consider are as follows:
>
> 1.  *Historical articles*that expand what we know about a figure in
>     India and their connection to the pragmatist tradition, or that
>     examine a pragmatist thinker’s engagement with Indian figures and
>     traditions, such as Ambedkar and Buddhism. Such studies might
>     focus on Dewey’s knowledge or writings on various Indian
>     traditions, or on Indian figures’ reception of Dewey (such as
>     Nehru, Gandhi, or Ambedkar), or on William James’ knowledge of
>     Indian philosophy, or focus on how a thinker like Phule or
>     Ambedkar represent a novel addition to the pragmatist tradition.
>
> &nbsp;
>
> 2.  *Comparative*or*constructive**articles*that emphasize a novel
>     and creative engagement between a specific form of pragmatist
>     thought and Indian thinkers and traditions, such as Buddhism or
>     Ambedkar. These studies might usefully examine specific topics or
>     themes across traditions, such as the role of experience in
>     education in Dewey and early Buddhism, or education and democracy
>     from Ambedkar’s pragmatism, or explore relationships, resonances
>     or dissonances between the discourse on race and caste which
>     engages pragmatist thinkers such as Jane Addams, Alain Locke, or
>     later (neo-pragmatist) figures like Richard Rorty or Cornel West,
>     whose work on race and democracy might resonate with caste and the
>     social thought of a Phule, Gandhi or Ambedkar.
>
> &nbsp;
>
> 3.  *Contemporary applications*of pragmatism to problems or topics in
>     India. For instance, what is the usefulness of Dewey’s thought for
>     India’s future? Might Peirce’s semiotics address a problem with
>     language in Nyaya thought? Studies in this category might use
>     Dewey’s notion of education to evaluate contemporary approaches to
>     education in India, for instance, or enunciate problems in Indian
>     society with a pragmatist account of democracy as a way of life.
>
> &nbsp;
>
> 4.  *Pragmatism beyond the Americas.*scholarship that explores themes
>     in philosophical traditions and thinkers which can be usefully
>     interpreted as characteristically pragmatist in nature. Rather
>     than tracing direct historical contact or influence, these studies
>     would ask which Indian philosophical traditions or figures
>     independently exhibit pragmatist commitments, such as an emphasis
>     on experience over abstract system, the practical consequences of
>     belief, fallibilism, or the inseparability of thought from action
>     and social life. These may be prior to or apart from any encounter
>     with American pragmatism. A representative theme might be the
>     Buddha’s pragmatic orientation toward suffering and the famous
>     refusal of metaphysical speculation, Nagarjuna’s treatment of
>     concepts as tools rather than final truths, the Carvaka school’s
>     empiricism, debates on the relationship between meaning and
>     action, or later figures such as Phule, Ambedkar, Vivekananda or
>     Iqbal considered as independent architects of a pragmatist
>     sensibility rooted in Indian social and religious life.
>
> We are interested in a wide range of studies that imaginatively
> explore the potentials of looking at Indian problems, traditions, and
> authors in connection with the varied and diverse pragmatist
> tradition. Scholars can take a range of approaches stemming from a
> variety of disciplines: philosophy, history, rhetorical studies, and
> more.
>
> Interested authors should submit**500-word
> abstract**and**title**for their proposed chapter, along with
> a**short author bio**, on or before**January 1, 2027**to the
> editors of this book project:
>
> Scott R. Stroud, University of Texas at

>
