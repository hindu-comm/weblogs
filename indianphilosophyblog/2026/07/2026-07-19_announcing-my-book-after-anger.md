[![](https://loveofallwisdom.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/book-cover-2.jpg)](https://loveofallwisdom.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/book-cover-2.jpg)

Readers, I’m delighted to announce that my first book is officially scheduled for publication! It’ll come out in November but you can [preorder it now](https://www.shambhala.com/after-anger.html), and I would really like to encourage you to do so if you’re interested: preorder numbers encourage bookstores to order more copies, which will help the book do well. I am particularly delighted that the book is coming out with Shambhala Publications – the esteemed Buddhist publisher of Ken Wilber, whose work has inspired [a ton of this blog’s reflection](https://loveofallwisdom.com/blog/tag/ken-wilber/), and Pema Chödrön, whom I’ve increasingly found a [powerful source of wisdom](https://loveofallwisdom.com/blog/2024/11/improving-on-the-buddha/).

The book will be called *After Anger: What Buddhism Can Teach Us About Our Culture of Rage*, and it philosophically defends Buddhist ideals of mindful serenity – but you don’t have to be a Buddhist to believe it. The book starts out by jumping into contemporary debates on race and gender – where [Myisha Cherry’s *The Case for Rage*](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-case-for-rage-9780197557341) and [Amia Srinivasan’s “The aptness of anger”](https://doi-org.ezproxy.neu.edu/10.1111/jopp.12130), among others, have argued that anger is a helpful or appropriate response to racial and gender injustice. Drawing on contemporary psychology and on Stoic philosophers like [Seneca](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/) as well as Buddhists like Śāntideva, I show that anger’s benefits are much smaller, and its harms much greater, than these defenders claim. I make a few qualifications to the point – like [transition-anger](https://loveofallwisdom.com/blog/2019/07/nussbaums-revised-view-of-anger/) – but against our contemporary culture that proclaims “If you’re not outraged you’re not paying attention”, the book shows that *in general* anger is a bad thing.

The most important qualification is that [*repressing* anger can be worse than expressing it](https://loveofallwisdom.com/blog/2009/08/repressing-and-reducing-anger/): if you just suppress the outward expression of anger but keep the anger inside, that can lead you to passive aggression or other forms of harm that are even more serious. So, the book argues, we need to reduce anger at its roots – in the feeling of mental suffering that comes when we don’t get what we want. But of course that’s easier said than done: how can we *actually* reduce that suffering?

On that question, too, our contemporary culture of rage gets it wrong. Here too, too often we put the blame on our external social or political circumstances – on insults or material deprivation. But the book shows why that blame doesn’t work. Compare the lives of Kurt Cobain and Anthony Bourdain, on one hand, with the life of [Thich Quang Duc](https://loveofallwisdom.com/blog/2019/07/the-importance-of-being-thich-quang-duc/) on the other. All three ended their lives deliberately – but where Cobain and Bourdain had every material advantage one could imagine yet still found their lives not worth living, Quang Duc remained calm and serene while literally on fire. (*Serenely On Fire* was my original proposed title for the book, and it’s more descriptive of the book’s overall content, but I trust the publisher’s judgement that the anger-based title is going to get more people noticing the book.) The examples show that our mental suffering comes not from bad external circumstances, but from the *interaction* of those circumstances with our own states of craving and resistance –states tragically strong in the minds of Cobain and Bourdain, but which Quang Duc had tamed with a lifetime of training.

So, I argue, we need to tame our craving and resistance as well, with the virtue of [mindfulness](https://loveofallwisdom.com/blog/2019/09/on-mindfulness/) essential to that taming. We may not want to go to Quang Duc’s extreme (or even to the level of a more normal monk), but we do need to tame those troublesome mental tendencies even in a worldly life. We do still need enough resistance to [change the things we can](https://loveofallwisdom.com/blog/2021/06/the-courage-to-change-the-things-we-can/) – but no more. For this reason I discuss the [Serenity Prayer](https://loveofallwisdom.com/blog/2019/10/the-wisdom-of-serenity/) in close connection with a [similar verse of Śāntideva’s](https://loveofallwisdom.com/blog/2021/06/frustration-where-mind-meets-world/) – noting that it’s important to know the difference between what we can change and what we can’t, but even in the former case it’s important to reduce our mental suffering.

The book is aimed at a popular audience (and multiple editors have helped me stick to that), so you should find it at least as accessible as my work on this blog, probably more so. It is *also* written as a serious scholarly argument, with copious endnotes so you can check that I’m not making things up – but you can skip them if that’s not your thing.

Longtime IPB readers with strong memories may recall that I had already said I was working on a book [six years ago](https://indianphilosophyblog.org/2020/08/16/a-book-on-how-virtue-helps-us-flourish/). It is the same book *project* that I said I was working on then – this project will have been more than eight years start to finish – but the book has taken a very different turn over the six years since I wrote that. My older idea was to defend eudaimonism – the idea that virtue helps us flourish as human beings. As is the case with so many early ideas, that one turned out to be too big for one book. I narrowed in on the *specific* virtue that Śāntideva calls *kṣānti* – which is most commonly rendered “patient endurance” in English, but which I translate “serenity” to highlight the connection to the Serenity Prayer.
