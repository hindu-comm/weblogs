The roaring neighs of Turkish stallions pierced through the plains of northern India, raising dust and instilling alarm and fear in the minds and hearts of a beleaguered populace. This was the Indian subcontinent in the thirteenth century CE. The throne of Ḍhillikā (Delhi) had already succumbed to invasions from Ghor, as had the opulent Gahadvalas of central India with their capital at Varanasi. Loyalists of the Turkish Ghurids were installed to administer the subjugated territory, laying the foundations of the 'Delhi Sultanate' – an ambitious imperial formation that actively sought to extend its frontiers and consolidate its political authority. It was not the first time that the subcontinent had witnessed a political invasion or foreign rule. In fact, it had previously fended off intrusions from Śakas, Hūṇas and various other formidable 'foreign' powers who had made their entrance into the subcontinent from the same north-western corridors, but had gradually assimilated into its civilisational matrix.

What was relatively unfamiliar, however, was the sharper religious dimension that sometimes accompanied the assertion of the new political order, particularly where local religious practices came into conflict with the convictions of ruling elites. The new rulers were fervent to overwrite the faith of this bewilderingly diverse race of image-worshippers whose practices they deeply abhorred. One dynasty succeeded the previous one. The Khiljis succeeded the Mamluks, and Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq founded the Tughlaq dynasty by replacing the Khiljis. Political and religious tensions, however, continued unabated.

Ghiyasuddin’s eldest son, Prince Jauna Khan (who succeeded to the throne as Muhammad bin Tughlaq) led violent expansionist campaigns in the peripheral regions. Out of rampant terror and fear of loss of religion, Harisingh Dev, a *Kṣatriya* chieftain of Ayodhya,[1](#footnote-1) retreated into the forest and went incognito. The vulnerable city gradually became a military station of the Delhi Sultanate. Over the course of decades, some thousands of the city’s residents were coerced into converting to Islam, including Harisingh’s nephew Gaj Singh and his family. For decades, these converted masses, led by Gaj Singh, went from place to place, visiting the *pandits* of yore and requesting for the means of their purification and a way to *return* to the Hindu fold,[2](#footnote-2) but to no avail. The scholars offered no solace, remarking that once someone had fallen from *dharma*, they were permanently polluted and could not be absolved. Not finding any hope or solution to their plight, they grew despondent.

Meanwhile, in a quiescent cavern on the Panchganga Ghat in Kashi, *the city that illuminates all,*[3](#footnote-3) resided a radiant *yogi*, the resounding sound of whose conch tore through the ears of the tyrants, and whose form exuded transcendental brilliance. His glimpse bestowed immense comfort upon the suffering populace. This was none other than Swami Rāmānanda, the progenitor of the stream of *Rāma-Bhakti* in medieval north India. When the suffering Gaj Singh heard of the glories of this saint who had even admitted a Muslim, Kabīra, into the *Vaiṣṇava* fold, he gathered hope. He arrived in Kashi, and humbly pleaded their case. **"Fear not, O son"**, said the effulgent saint, **"the fall from** ***dharma*** **under duress or situations of emergency does not lead to permanent degradation.[4](#footnote-4) Thirty days from now, I shall come to Ayodhya and redeem you all. Observe a fast, and wait for my arrival.”** As if resuscitated, the *Kṣatriya* bowed in reverence and returned to Ayodhya.

#### **Rise Of The ‘Second Sun’**



A Kanyakubja Brahmin couple resided in the holy city of *tīrtha-rāja* (‘king’ of all holy places) Prayag, where the Ganga and Yamuna make a confluence with the invisible Saraswati, giving the place the name ‘*Triveṇī*’. Austere and pious, Punyasadan Vajapeyi and his wife Sushila[5](#footnote-5) were devout in their observance of dharma and worshipped Veṇī Mādhava[6](#footnote-6) with great devotion, and carried in the abyss of their heart the wish of having an offspring as luminous and auspicious as Rāma. One day, they supplicated at the feet of Veṇī Mādhava. As a signal of divine succour, the *dakṣiṇāvartī* (clockwise) conch of Veṇī Mādhava fell into Sushila’s outstretched veil, assuring that their prayer was heard and a divine being would soon be born.

The seventh of the waxing phase of the lunar month of Māgha in Vikrama Saṁvata 1356 (1299-1300 CE)[7](#footnote-7) arrived. Coincidentally, it was also *Makar* *Sankranti*, the auspicious day of the solar calendar marked by the sun’s ascent into Capricorn. Sixty six thousand holy *tīrthas* were covertly present in the city.[8](#footnote-8) In the biting cold, Prayag was beaming with huge crowds of devotees flocking to the confluence of the holy waters, while ascetics made their residence in humble hamlets in the floodplain to perform *kalpa-vāsa* (month-long residence in Prayag during the winter month of Māgha while observing austere vows). Vedic scholars began reciting auspicious incantations, and an ethereal energy filled the ambience of Punyasadan Vajapeyi’s home. Under such extremely auspicious astronomical arrangements, the propagator of *Rāma-Bhakti* and an embodiment of Rāma himself[9](#footnote-9) was born from Sushila’s pious womb.

***āvirbhūto mahāyogī dvitīya iva bhāskaraḥ । rāmānanda itikhyāto lokoddharaṇa kāraṇaḥ ॥***

**“The great** ***yogi*** **by the name Rāmānanda appeared. As if a second sun, he was the cause of upliftment of the world.”[10](#footnote-10)**

After the *janma-saucha* (period of ritual pollution following birth) was over, the *Nāma-karaṇa* sacrament was performed, and the brilliant child was named ‘Rāmadatta’.[11](#footnote-11) Some months later, in an auspicious *hour*, *Anna-prāśana* ceremony was held and the child was offered a number of preparations, of which he only accepted *kheer* (rice pudding) made with the milk from superior cows. For his whole life, he is said to have lived only on *kheer*.[12](#footnote-12)

#### **The Divine Conch Reverberates!**



One morning, as his father sat in meditation before the home altar, the child Rāmadatta sneaked in, picked up the divine conch and blew it resoundingly, such that everybody who heard entered a state of momentary trance. Upon regaining external consciousness, the parents rushed to find the young boy with the conch in his hand. At the same time, a divine speech reassured them and instructed them to gladly give the conch to the boy. Thenceforth, the luminous Rāmadatta would blow the conch every day at dawn, noon and dusk, bringing transcendental joy to all pious beings.[13](#footnote-13)

#### **The Journey To Kashi**



At the age of five, Vājapeyi Ji began imparting Vālmki Rāmāyaṇa and the Gitā to Rāmadatta, which the erudite boy memorised and recited with great precision. After some time had passed, the *Upanayana saṁskāra* was performed. The *brahmachari* Rāmadatta was given the sacred thread and initiated into the *Gāyatrī mantra*, and was customarily instructed to "go to Kashi for the study of scriptures." Rāmadatta rose from his seat and began proceeding towards Kashi. As is the custom even today, the relatives pleaded with the *brahmachari* to convince him to turn back. However the truthful Rāmadatta was undeterred, and the parents ultimately relented. Rāmadatta was brought to Kashi, where he began his Vedic learning at *Śrī Maṭha,*[14](#footnote-14) the seminary of Swami Rāghavānanda,[15](#footnote-15) an illustrious *Vaiṣṇava* renunciate, on the Panchganga Ghat in Kashi.

Panchganga derived its name from the presence of ‘five holy waters’. A *purāṇic* shrine, the temple of Bindu Mādhava ruled its skyline; it was among the five key *tīrthas* in Varanasi’s pilgrim geography.[16](#footnote-16) The French merchant Jean-Baptiste Tavernier, who toured ‘Benares’ in 1665[17](#footnote-17), described it as the "most imposing structure along the Banaras waterfront." In its vicinity, he spoke of the presence of a Vedic seminary with ‘a temple of Rāma’,[18](#footnote-18) which very possibly stood in the place of the then extinct Śrī Maṭha, lost during the destruction of Kashi’s riverscape by Sikandar Lodi in late Fifteenth century CE.[19](#footnote-19) A re-inception of the Śrī Maṭha was completed in the twentieth century around a modest but intact marble shrine of Swami Rāmānandācārya’s sandals (*pādukā*).

#### **From Rāmadatta To Rāmānanda**



After successfully completing his education in the Vedic scriptures, the *samāvartana saṁskāra* (graduation ceremony) was done. While the parents expected the *brahmachari* to prepare for married life ahead, as do all others, Rāmadatta’s heart brimmed with renunciation, devotion and intense empathy for the society. The tyranny of the times and the suffering of his people invoked the purpose of his birth. He sat humbly at Swami Rāghavānanda’s feet, and requested to become a renunciate. The all-knowing spiritual master accepted, and with the consent of the venerable mother-father, initiated Rāmadatta into the *Śrī Vaiṣṇava* tradition with the *pañca-saṁskāras* (five sacraments) – which included branding the shoulders with heated metal seals (*tapta-mudrā-s*) of Bhagavān’s divine weapons (*āyudha,* mainly *dhanur-bāṇa*, i.e. bow-arrow and *śaṅkha-cakra* i.e. conch-discus),[20](#footnote-20) applying the vertical Tilak mark (*Ūrdhva-puṇḍra*) on the forehead and eleven other spots on the body, awarding of a Vaiṣṇava name, imparting of the six-syllable *Rāma* *Tāraka* *Mantra*, and tying Tulasi beads around the neck.[21](#footnote-21) With great elation, Swami Rāghavānanda then initiated his extraordinary disciple into the renunciate[22](#footnote-22) order, and named him ‘Rāmānanda’. He also blessed him with divine *yogic* abilities.

#### **A Fellowship Beyond Birth And Caste**



With the ascent of the radiant Rāmānanda as its Ācārya, Śrī Maṭha manifested itself as the ‘*grand Gomukha*’[23](#footnote-23) from where the *Rāma-Bhakti-gaṅgā* (Ganga of devotion to Rāma) began to flow and inundate the northern lands. Soon, word spread about the austerities, yogic potencies and devotional discourses of Swami Rāmānanda. Deserving seekers began to come and take *dīkṣā* from him.

Besides Anantānanda and many other Brahmin disciples like Sukhānanda, Surasurā, Narahari, Bhāvānanda, Yogānanda and Gālavānanda, Swami Rāmānanda also graced Pīpā who was the Rajput king of Gagron, Dhannā who was a Jat agriculturist, Sena who was a barber of Bāndhavagaḍha as well as Kabīra (Kabīra) who was raised in the family of Muslim weavers[24](#footnote-24) and Raidāsa (Ravīdāsa) who was born in the family of tanner-cobblers, both of them being his two most well-known disciples. Quite revolutionarily, he initiated women as disciples, three of whom are known through the *Bhaktamāla* – Padmāvati as well as wives of Pīpā and Surasurā. Not only did Swami Rāmānanda award *Vaiṣṇava dīkṣā,* he even initiated many of them into the renunciate order (*Bairāgīs*) regardless of their birth, considering their renunciation and devotion as the foremost qualifications.

#### **The Glory Of The Rāma-Tāraka Mantra**



The *Ācārya* revealed the meaning of the name ‘Rāma’, **"The Supreme Absolute Truth Who is infinitely blissful, and in Whom wise** ***yogis*** **take delight and merge their minds, He is called** ***Rāma.”***[25](#footnote-25) He further elaborated on the glories and the spiritual succession of the *Rāma mantra* that had been received by him from his preceptor Swami Rāghavānanda, and had now been passed on to his disciples –

**"The six-syllable** ***Rāma Mantra*** **is** ***Mantrarāja*****, the king of all spiritual mantras. It is also called** ***Tāraka*****, since it delivers its** ***jāpaka*** **(chanter) from the ocean of material existence. The Supreme Being Śrī Rāma revealed this** ***mantra*** **to Brahmā.**[26](#footnote-26) **Lord Śiva also meditated upon it for ages and propitiated his dearest Lord Śrī Rāma, who then granted a boon that anyone who receives the** ***Tāraka Mantra*** **by Lord Śiva on their deathbed in Kashi shall be liberated without doubt. O dear disciples, a devotee who meditates on the** ***Rāma Tāraka Mantra*** **becomes** ***paṅkti-pāvana*****, that is, he purifies the rows in which he sits and eats.**[27](#footnote-27)

Hearing the discourses on the divinity of Rāma’s name, form and attributes, the disciples were ecstatic. In a state of devotional trance, Sena composed and sang an offering to his beloveds, Guru and Govinda –

**“*****Having prepared it with incense, lamps and ghee, I go to offer this āratï to You, O Husband of Lakṣmī.***

***Hail to You, O Lord Hari, Hail to You! Ever Hail to You, O Rāja Rāma, Ruler of All!***

***Sublime is the lamp, and taintless is the wick. You verily are immaculate and pure, O Lord of Kamalā!***

***Rāmānanda knows devotion to Rāma. He describes it as complete and supremely blissful.***

***He of wondrous form, the master of the senses, who delivers across the terrifying world-ocean, says Saina, remember Him, the embodiment of supreme joy!'”*** [28](#footnote-28)

#### **The Promise Fulfilled**



Gaj Singh, the nephew of Ayodhya’s exiled chieftain, and the numerous forced converts of Ayodhya awaited the arrival of the divine saint who had promised to come to their rescue. On the said day, the miraculous Swami Rāmānanda, flanked by Anantānanda and many other prominent disciples, arrived in Ayodhya. After taking *darśana* at the birthplace of Rāma, he arrived at the banks of the Saryu, where along with the king and all his kinsmen, many hundreds of forcibly converted Hindus lined up in the waters of the holy river Saryu. At that time, the Swami blew his miraculous conch, and led a loud chant of ‘*Śrī Rāma-nāma’* and behold! The marks of conversion[29](#footnote-29) diminished from their bodies. Then, anointing their foreheads with the illustrious *Vaiṣṇava tilak*a and placing Tulasi beads around their necks, the benevolent *Ācārya* imparted the all-purifying and all-liberating supreme *Rāma Mantra* to all the initiates, marking their return to dharma. Thus, the inhabitants of Ayodhya who had become “*mlecchas”* under force and had been deserted by a rigid orthodoxy, were not only embraced by Swami Rāmānanda but also bravely restored into the mainstream.[30](#footnote-30) With *Tulasi* beads around their necks, their tongues uttering the name of Rāma, and the vertical *Tilaka* markings on the forehead, even the polluted ones became purified as Vaiṣṇavas under the influence of Rāmānanda.[31](#footnote-31)

#### **Pīpā, Gagron And The Call To Renunciation**



At the earnest request of his illustrious disciple Pīpā, Swami Rāmānanda graced his province of Gāṅgarauna (Gagron) in Rajasthan, residing there for some time with his disciples. Pīpā was overwhelmed at the sight of his guru. In divine ecstasy, he exclaimed –

***pīpā ke piṁjare basyo, rāmānanda ko rūpa।***

***sabe andherā miṭa gayā, dekhyā ratana anūpa॥***

**“Within the cage of Pīpā’s heart resides the form of Swami Rāmānanda. All dark ignorance has evaporated, for I have beheld the unequalled jewel (of Rāma)”.**[32](#footnote-32)

Pīpā expressed his desire for renunciation to his spiritual master, and was initiated into the *Bairāgī* order. When Swami Rāmānanda prepared to leave for Kashi, Pīpā’s youngest queen, Sita Sahchari Ji, who was equally detached from mundane leisurely life, was also allowed to join the retinue.

#### **A Sacred Geography Of The Digvijaya**



To spread his social programme of universal welfare through devotion and self-surrender *(prapatti)* to Rāma, Śrī Rāmānandācārya resolved to tour the country with his entourage of disciples.[33](#footnote-33) Leaving from Kashi, they arrived at the mouth of the Ganga on the shores of the ‘Eastern Sea’. After taking a bath in the Gangasagar *tīrtha*[34](#footnote-34) on the Sagara island where the hermitage of Sage Kapil had once stood before it submerged in the sea, Swami Rāmānanda revived the holy *tīrtha* by consecrating an idol of sage Kapil, along with images of Goddess Ganga and King Sagara.

Abu, Rajasthan), Sursurānanda inquired about various vital aspects of *Vaiṣṇava dharma,* such as the ideal *mantra*, the prescribed methods of meditation, **the **symptoms of a Vaiṣṇava, their activities, their places of residence. The luminous *Ācārya,* seated on the *Vyāsa-āsana*, replied in detail to each of these spiritual inquiries. This conversation came to be canonised in the form of ‘*Śrī-Vaiṣṇava-Matābja-Bhāskara*’.[36](#footnote-36) Born out of his answers to these spiritual inquiries by Sursurānanda, it encapsulated his magnanimous vision that would lead to ‘the success and spread of the sect across the length and breadth of the subcontinent’.[37](#footnote-37) Finally, travelling through Chitrakoot, Haridwar, Vrindavan, Prayag, Mithila and Ayodhya, he returned with his retinue to Kashi.

#### **Who May Take Refuge?**



Not only did the compassionate Swami Rāmānanda defend *dharma* against totalitarian tyranny, but he also attempted to emancipate a suffering Hindu society that had become infested with sectarian infighting and internal alienation. He declared emphatically in the *Śrī-Vaiṣṇava-Matābja-Bhāskara*:

***sarve prapatteḥ adhikāriṇaḥ sadā śaktā aśaktā padayor jagatprabhoḥ।***

***apekṣyate tatra kulaṁ balaṁ ca no na cāpi kālo na ca śuddhatāpi vai॥***

**“All are eligible at all times, for taking refuge at the lotus-feet of the Lord of all the worlds. Neither their birth, nor their strength, disability, time or purity is to be considered.”**[38](#footnote-38)

The door to Rāma’s sanctuary was thrown open to every human. There was no gatekeeping to devotion.

***jāti pāti pūche nahi koī, hari ko bhaje so hari kā hoī.***

**“Let no man’s caste or creed be asked; whoever adores Hari is His own.”**

The great *Ācārya* did not stop here. In a clearly revolutionary declaration for the existing social order, Swami Rāmānanda advised that Vaiṣṇavas born in lower *varṇas* should be served with reverence.[39](#footnote-39) By establishing that Rāma was pleased by someone who serves his devotees, who are all venerable irrespective of their birth or their social standing, he bolstered the worldview wherein devotion, not birth, reigned supreme.

#### **Bhakti Over Birth**



By incorporating people from virtually all social locations in the generous distribution of the *Rāma Mantra*, Swami Rāmānanda kindled a characteristic transformation in northern India, heralding the arrival of a prolonged current of *Bhakti*. Besides, the hagiographies are also replete with narratives of his display of mystic powers to subdue hostile adversaries viz. *Tāntrikas*, *Kaulas*, *Aghorīs* etc as well as his dialogues with stalwarts from various schools of philosophy, from *Vedantīs to Yogīs* and *Jainas*. According to Pitambar Datt Barthwal, many vernacular compositions are attributed to him, one of them being a mystic incantation by the name *Ādhyātma Rāmarakṣā*. Besides, Swami Rāmānanda also composed the popular *Āratī* of Hanuman, **‘*****Aratī kīje Hanumān lalā kī, duṣṭa-dalana Raghunātha kalā kī*****’**.[40](#footnote-40)

After gracing the world in his earthly form for a long lifetime, Swami Rāmānanda **passed the final instructions to his disciples, and departed to Sāketa (the heavenly abode of Lord Ram). As is the case for his birth, the date and year of his disappearance are also highly contested. *Agastya Saṁhitā* and *Prasaṅga Pārijāta* give 1411 CE and circa 1458 CE respectively as the year of his disappearance.[41](#footnote-41)

#### **The Rāmānandī Hagiographical Tradition**



In northern India, hagiographical texts predominate in medieval literature pertaining to religious sects and saints, the basic structure of which was laid out by the *Bhaktamāla* (fl. 1580-1624 CE) and its commentaries.[42](#footnote-42) A highly celebrated text containing anthologies and life sketches of devotees of all four ‘*yugas*’ and across sects, it was composed by Nābhādāsa, who was fourth in the disciplic lineage from Swami Rāmānanda, from the distinguished Rāmānandi seat of Galta near Jaipur. Soon after, the *Bhakta-Paracaī* texts were composed by Anantadāsa (fl. 1588), who was also a Rāmānandi and a disciple of Nābhādāsa’s godbrother Vinoda Dāsa. [43](#footnote-43) According to Lorenzen, the ‘explosion’ of hagiographies during this period was owing to the increasing reliance by devotional communities centred on their founders as sources of divine authority.[44](#footnote-44)

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4do1!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F93e55ab3-c6cd-424d-b03d-1aa08272757a_1280x833.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4do1!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F93e55ab3-c6cd-424d-b03d-1aa08272757a_1280x833.jpeg)





Nābhā Dāsa (left), the author of the *Bhaktamāla*, in the presence of his guru, Swami Agra-devācārya.

Rather than mistaking hagiographies for ‘mere panegyric accounts’ glorifying the *‘saints’* of their respective *guruparamparās,*[45](#footnote-45) it is important to understand their role as exemplars, as mirrors to larger historical context shaping the lives of their subjects, and as ‘multiple lenses through which a particular historical time and larger attitudes of the period towards the past and present of that period could be seen’.[46](#footnote-46) Moreover, the contradictions between memory and history, in which memory stands for orality and subjectivity and history for literacy and objectivity, so often highlighted in memory studies in the case of hagiographies, is not always tenable.[47](#footnote-47)

#### **Prasaṅga Pārijāta And The Recovery Of Traditional Memory**



In the 1930s, the librarian Balak Ramji Vinayaka came across an ancient manuscript of a text named *‘Prasaṅga Pārijāta*’ in the stacks of Sarnath Library, written in the extinct literary language of Paishachi. The author ‘Chetana Das’ introduced himself as a disciple of Swami Rāmānanda, and stated the completion of the text to Samvat 1517 (circa 1461 CE). Composed in 108 *aṣṭapadīs* (ritual stanzas of eight couplets) each with a *phala-śruti* (merit), the text came with a warning that pre-empted its publication for a long time –

**“…that the story be reduced to writing in extraordinary rhymes and language which none may follow unless explained and which may be preserved by the enlightened beings, for there are some portions of it which should not be publicised and be kept confidential unless the events foretold in them happen … therefore, this text is composed in the divine Prakrit in Paishachi language by adding symbolic words … Whosoever reveals these details before time shall go mad. But one who recites them after they’re manifest, will obtain tattva-jñāna and the want of four puruṣārthas will be fulfilled.”**[48](#footnote-48)

The text was eventually brought to light in a literary conference in Lucknow, not without troubles, and later published for the first time from Ayodhya in 1951. Although replete with supernatural elements, and despite a bleak sense of chronology, *Prasaṅga Pārijāta* seems to have inspired many twentieth-century works of Hindi literature, viz. the poetic hagiography ‘*Śrī-Rāmānandāyana*’ by Swami Srī Jayaramadeva (1940 CE), later reworked in prose ‘*Śrīmad-Rāmānandācārya-Carita*’ and the novels ‘*Śaṅkhanāda*’ and ‘*Kāśī Mārtaṇḍa*’ (both in Hindi, with later English translations), thus contributing to collective narrative memory of Swami Rāmānanda amongst the Rāmānandīs.

In Sanskrit literature, the first contemporary hagiography appears to have been *Śrīmad Rāmānanda Digvijayaḥ* (lit. ‘T*he Triumphal Tour of Śrīmad Rāmānanda*’, first published in 1928) by Bhagavad-dasa Brahmachari alias Swami Bhagwadacharya, followed by *Śrī Ācārya Vijaya* of Gosvami Harikrishna Shastri, a scholar of Vallabhacharya’s Puṣṭimārga. ‘*Śrī Rāmānandācārya Caritam’* (2022) by Swami Rambhadracharya remains the most recent Sanskrit work on Swami Rāmānanda.[49](#footnote-49)

#### **The ‘Historical’ Rāmānanda**



The paucity of veritable records, the conflicting accounts of hagiographical narratives, the explicit ambiguity on Swami Rāmānanda’s periodisation as well as the absence of “a hard biography” has invited the speculation marks from Western scholars such as Richard Burghart and William Pinch on his historicity, suggesting him to have been a “conjectured” by the Rāmānandis for legitimising claim to an independent identity. This runs utterly disregardful, if not oblivious, of the fact that not only does he feature in the compositions of his disciples of the ‘*Sant Mat*’, namely Sena, Pīpā and Kabīra,[50](#footnote-50) but is remembered in the hagiographic accounts of the *Kab*ī*ra-panth* and the *Ravidāsa Rāmāyaṇa* of the Ravidāsīs.[51](#footnote-51)

A composition of Swami Rāmānanda is also included in the *Adi Granth Sahib*,[52](#footnote-52) the Sikh holy scripture beside many compositions of Kabīra, Ravīdāsa, Saina (Sena) and Dhanna, where they are referred to as ‘*Bhagats*’. The Sikh hagiographies of Bhai Gurdas also recollect the anecdotes of the aforementioned devotee disciples, with particular description of Kabīra’s initiation from Rāmānanda.[53](#footnote-53)

The earliest known hagiography, the *Bhaktamāla*, has also recounted Swami Rāmānanda by listing his distinguished spiritual progeny, through whom he erected a spiritual ‘bridge’ to deliver innumerable ‘refugee’ souls (*prapanna-s*) –

**“Anantānanda, Kabīr, Sukhā, Sursurā, Padmāvatī, Narahari, Pīpā, Bhāvānanda, Raidāsa, Dhannā, Sena, Sursurā’s wife;**

**and many more illustrious disciples and grand-disciples; (they were) the support for the welfare of worldly beings and the champions of the ten-fold** ***Bhakti*****.**

**Residing in his corporeal form for a prolonged time, he carried the sheltered souls across the world-ocean. Just like** ***the Lord of the Raghu clan*** **(Rāma), Śrī Rāmānanda created a second bridge for crossing the material world.”**[54](#footnote-54)

#### **‘Living’ In Memory And Geography**



While traversing parts of Rajasthan, including Jaipur and Sikar, in the course of my fieldwork for this project, as well as in my previous peregrinations to Vrindavan, Ayodhya, Chitrakoot, Kashi, and Prayaga, especially during the *Mahākumbha* of 2025, I could somewhat fathom and admire the vast presence of the Rāmānandi ascetics and mendicants throughout the length and breadth of the northern plains, and much more pronouncedly in the holy cities. In the eighteenth century, the Rāmānandis were at the forefront of the militarisation of Vaiṣṇava ascetics into regiments, in the wake of various forms of threats to Vaiṣṇava religious places and communities.[55](#footnote-55) The new complex of Shri Ram Janmabhumī Temple renders homage to the great saint by naming one of its entrances ‘Jagadguru Rāmānandācārya Dwāra’. It bears witness to the significant role played by Rāmānandi ascetics[56](#footnote-56) in the history of the Rāma Janmabhumī.

The order of ascetics founded by Swami Rāmānanda came to be known as *Bairāgī*, a denomination which later came to include ascetics of four major Vai*ṣṇ*ava sects. The *Tazkirat-ul-Fuqra*,[57](#footnote-57) an Urdu text that gives accounts of the various denominations of ‘*faqirs*’ (mendicants and mystics, both Muslim and Hindu) describes —

**“The** ***Bairāgīs*** **are those who have abandoned worldly attachments and desires (*****bairāg*** **meaning detachment or renunciation). They wear simple garments, often smeared with ash, or wear white or ochre-coloured clothes. They keep their minds absorbed in the devotion of the Divine (frequently Viṣṇu or Rāma) and wander from place to place visiting holy pilgrimage sites (*****tīrath*****). They maintain strict dietary rules, abstain from meat and alcohol, and subsist on simple vegetarian food or alms obtained without coercion.”**[58](#footnote-58)

Gorgani further describes in his ‘account of Rāmānanda, his sect, and followers’[59](#footnote-59) – "They apply a yellow *tilaka* on their foreheads, wear a necklace of *tulsi* *mālā*, and chant the name of Rāma. Their core belief is that God is One and Rāmacandra is his incarnation (avatāra)."

A more well-known Persian text of 17th century CE, the *Dabistan-i-Mazahib* or ‘School of Religions’, describes the four sects of the *Bairāgīs*, and mentions that “Whoever among the Hindus, Muselmans, or others, wishes, is received into their religion; none are rejected, but, on the contrary, all are invited.”[60](#footnote-60)

#### **A Living Legacy Of Unbounded Love**



Reflecting on how Swami Rāmānanda is remembered in the collective memory, both sectarian followers and the polemics in academia would agree to perceive him as the pioneer of a broad, inclusive devotional stream that swayed and advantaged individuals and communities across castes, tongues, genders and geographies. Swami Rāmānanda brought about the actualisation of the principle of spiritual egalitarianism through unalloyed devotion (*Bhakti*) and complete self-surrender to God (*Prapatti*), which was also enshrined by Rāmānuja in his illustrious Sanskrit commentaries. The philosophical ‘synthesis’ of Swami Rāmānanda lies in making the Supreme Being, *Para-brahman* Śrī Rāma (in both His personal manifestation as well as His omnipresent being) a ‘*domestic asset*’ – available and accessible to all individuals — not merely in principle, but also in lived experience and everyday performance.

The vision didn’t remain confined to principles enshrined in philosophical texts, but indeed percolated in practice through numerous lineages of preceptors and saints that sprang from his followers. Of Swami Rāmānanda’s twelve prominent disciples, Raidāsa (Ravīdās) was revered as *guru* by not only the unprivileged castes, but also by disciples like Mīrā Bāī and queen Jhālī Bāī, both belonging to royalty in erstwhile Rajputana. While Kabīra was accepted as *guru* by Raja Vir Singh Baghel[61](#footnote-61) of Kashi, Sena (Saina) was accepted as their guru by the royal family of Bandhavgarh principality in Madhya Pradesh.

**“Rāmānanda stood at the dawning of a new age of religiousness all over North India, an age in which full participation in a life of devotion was not the prerogative of the elite few, but the claim and faith of all – the illiterates, the downtrodden and the outcastes.”**[62](#footnote-62) *In his being converge three luminous characteristics – the distribution of pure devotion, the defence of dharma*, and the emancipation of those on the periphery.

PE Burchett observes with regards to the *Bairāgīs*, **“While contributing to the expansion of a broad** ***Bhakti*** **public and sensibility; they took part in a vibrant transreligious culture of song and performance; they participated in a shared grammar of asceticism and pragmatic power (embodied in charismatic saints); and they engaged in new aesthetic trends and the expansion of vernacular Hindavi beyond the oral and popular realms into the written and literary-courtly spheres.”**[63](#footnote-63)

In this way, the legacy of Swami Rāmānanda and the Rāmānandi *Bairāgīs* is manifold. It flourishes not only in the pilgrim places and sacred nodes of the subcontinent like Ayodhya, Mathura, Prayaga or Puri. Rather, it has permeated the very veins of rural north, central and western India. Whether it be their direct physical presence, or intangible role in the lila traditions inspired by the magnum opus *Ramcharitmanas,* or in memory, like Tulsidas’s *Hanuman Chalisa* and Kabīra’s couplets. Having diffused into all four corners of the subcontinent, the Rāmānandi sect is a vibrant, indelible element of Bharat's socio-religious traditional milieu. ***The second sun shines still, drawing one and all into the boundless light of Bhakti.***

Jain recollects the series of iconoclastic invasions that Varanasi witnessed, beginning with the first invasion in 1033 by the troops of Ghaznavi’s son, followed by attacks during Alauddin Khilji’s reign (early 14th century), whereupon the city endured wave after wave of attacks. Numerous sites held holy were destroyed and mosques erected in their place. When Sikandar Lodi ordered the demolition of all temples at Varanasi in 1494 CE, the sacred complex of Panchganga Ghat was razed to rubble. No temples were built for at least eighty years; people consoled themselves by paying homage to temple ruins (Ibid., 91-92).

The last and most devastating destruction occurred at the orders of Aurangzeb. A grandiose mosque was insolently erected on the foundations of Bindu Madhava temple, as also over the temple of Vishwanath, to make the sites ‘permanently closed to Hindus’ and to give to them ‘continuous humiliation’ (Ibid. 103-104). The city of the Puranic *mahatmyas* had been completely obliterated (Eck, Diana L. *India A Sacred Geography*, 84).

Around the late sixteenth century, the site was grandly reconsecrated by Man Singh of Amber, only to be demolished less than a hundred years later on the orders of Aurangzeb in 1669. It is very likely that Tavernier’s descriptions of temple and Vedic school, both said by him to be built by Mirza Raja Jai Singh, were of the precincts of the erstwhile Śrī Maṭha (Divay Gupta 2024, 1-18) of the Rāmānandīs who considerably influenced the religious engagements of the Amber royalty \[from Prithviraj’s time onwards\] on account of Galta becoming the seat of the Kacchavahas’ preceptors (PE Burchett 2019, 108). While the rest of the structures were destroyed along with Bindu Madhava, less than two years after Jai Singh’s demise, some structures survived possibly on grounds of Amber’s protection or patronisation.
