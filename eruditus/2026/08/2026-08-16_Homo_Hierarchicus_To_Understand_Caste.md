In this write-up, we will try to understand **hierarchy and caste from a historical lens**. Attached below in the form of a screenshot from X are the questions that have been asked of us. They’re genuine sentiments that most modern and educated Hindus feel. There is an underlying embarassment and shame whenever one broaches the question of caste or hierarchy with this cohort. These are reasonable demands, and let us try to provide reasonable answers for them.

Over the past few centuries, all reformist movements in the subcontinent have made a gross mistake in interpreting our past: **they have taken prescriptive texts written in a certain historical context in the pre-modern age at face value.** As a result, they have created a scare-crow of how our past looked like, and used it to frighten anyone who has asked the forbidden second question: *why do we judge the past based on the moralities of the present?*

**Let us first understand why we think what we think.** The modern idea of what is right and what is wrong, what is justice and what is injustice, come from two things:

1.  The values of liberty, equality and fraternity that were products of
    the Enlightenment and The French Revolution, universalized and
    spread after the second World War in the package of Western, Liberal
    Democracy (WEIRD) by the unipolar and dominant Anglo-American world
    order. [1](#footnote-1)

2.  Mass abundance, prosperity and high disposable incomes of the
    post-Industrial Revolution Western World. The automation of most
    manual labor in the developed world, and mass literacy creating a
    new civic culture which looked down upon hierarchy due to the
    greatest rate of social mobility in millenia.

You are ***able*** to worry about inequality, lack of social mobility and birth-based injustices because you live in a world of abundance where you do not worry about where your next meal will come from. You open an instant food-delivery app and order a dosa or biryani while scrolling X posts about environmentalism, unfairness, and inequality. Do you understand the luxury you enjoy? Can you ***possibly*** think of the world in the same way as a Bronze Age agriculturalist in 1500 BCE Punjab who lives in a tribal society?

You cannot. **This is an important and honest admission to make**. You cannot conceive of the world, of your relation to reality, the village, society, community, hierarchy and survival in the same way as your ancestors 3500 years ago. Can you possibly think of *jyotiṣa* in the same way as a Vedic-era man from 950 BCE Kurukṣetra? When he looked up in the sky, he saw *this (c.f. pic below)*. Every hour of the day, every month.

Ever wondered why the Sarasvatī and Dṛṣadvatī were thought to literally be celestial rivers descending from the sky on the sacred ground of the Land of The Kurus? See this, this is what they saw, every single day when they looked up. What do *you* see when you look up? What do you hear when you close your eyes on a road at 6 PM? Can your noise and light polluted eyes and ears possibly have the same relation to nature that someone in the Bronze Age did back then? *Be Honest.*

Before I explain the historical context of hierarchy and caste, I want you, the reader, to stop making judgements on the past based on the world you live in today. Can you do that for me, for 15 minutes?

Civilization was built on extracting surplus value from agriculture, and using that food, that *agrarian surplus,* for occupational specialization. The primary worry of most small-scale tribal societies was day-to-day survival. Hierarchy was not only seen as completely natural, but also essential for the act of converting order into chaos, and controlling the entropy of the universe, to some extent. Most occupations that could move beyond agriculture or the labour related to it and gain control of land and labour, generally rose up in the ladder in any tribal society. People passed down fiercely contested fiefdoms and occupational specializations to their own kin: **if you are a warrior and control a domain because of your skill with the horse and sword, why would you transfer that advantage to people who are not your kin?**

This is a world defined by scarcity, and it is a world defined by tribal relations and limited social mobility. There are no degree-granting universities in the Bronze or Iron Age for you to graduate from or LinkedIn portals to apply to for a job. You do what your father did, and he does what his father did. Social mobility in the pre-modern is limited not by some evil moral choice of keeping the have-nots down, but because most people simply do not trust the weak state and world they live in to take care of them, if they lose their only advantage in life. Risk-taking, by definition is extremley low (though it brings great rewards).

You yourself see many hierarchies as perfectly natural, do you complain about having a manager, director, professor, teacher or parent? Perhaps you are a rebel, and you do. **What truly bothers you is the fact you associate the Hindu Caste System and all pre-modern hierarchy with immutability, permanence and unfairness attached at birth.**

The problem is, you blame these things on prescriptive idealistic texts like the Mānava Dharmaśāstra, and give no charity to the socioeconomic structure of society in the Bronze Age or the world those people lived in or the historical context of the texts. In reality, there was always a fair degree of mobility within all pre-modern hierarchies as well as a fair-bit of rigidity. We’ve understood that hierarchies are less mobile, natural in the pre-modern world and exist due to the relation of land, labour and capital that is different from ours. This is part of the socioeconomic context.

**Let us see the historical context of the oldest origins of distinction.**

The Hindu Caste System was born as a tribal hierarchical system of the Indo-Aryans. They were a tribal, semi-nomadic and pastoralist people living in a country occupied by other tribal people (who often did not follow their rites, worship their Gods or speak their language). They had good relations with some tribes, and bad relations with others. The fourth varṇa, that of the *Shūdra,* was their way of assimilating rival para-Indus tribes who did not worship their Gods or follow their rites. The entire system of the three, and then four *varṇas,* provided a framework to expand their population, spread their religion and culture, and assimilate newer tribes into their worldview. The word is not of Sanskritic origin, and it is the name of a ***tribe*** that existed somewhere in the North-West of the subcontinent.

The Rigveda contains names of Harappan (*likely*) chieftains designated as *Dāsa-s* that are completely non-Sanskritic in origin. However, these *Dāsa* chiefs are rich and wealthy landowning patrons who worship the Vedic dieties and patronize Vedic poets.

`श॒तं दा॒से ब॑ल्बू॒थे विप्र॒स्तरु॑क्ष॒ आ द॑दे । ते ते॑ वायवि॒मे जना॒ मद॒न्तीन्द्र॑गोपा॒ मद॑न्ति दे॒वगो॑पाः ॥`

*A hundred did I, the vipra, receive from the Dāsa Balbūtha, from Tarukṣa. These are your people, O Vāyu, who rejoice with Indra as their protector, who rejoice with the gods as their protectors.*

What kind of a Sanskrit name is *Balbūtha Tarukṣa*? You don’t even need to be a linguist to see it is not. These are non-Arya people, much like the *Shudras* of old, who were assimilated into the order of the Arya. When the oldest legal texts of the Arya are talking about the fourth *varṇa,* they are talking of it in the *inherited* context of a rival non-Arya tribe that frequently conflicts with the Arya. The fact that these people are a **tribe** is pretty clear when you look at the Atharvaveda mentioning them **along with groups who are clearly tribes.** [2](#footnote-2)

The Veda, its language, the rites and religious sacrifices were limited to the first three varṇa-s because those are the varṇa-s that literally made up the Proto-Sanskrit and Vedic dieties worshipping Arya people. **Why would a pre-modern tribe ever include a rival tribe that does not speak its language, does not worship its Gods or believe in rites as part of their in-group?** This does not happen even today in Amazonian tribes, some of which practice ritual cannibalism against the others, forget Bronze Age tribes. [3](#footnote-3)

The modern word Shudra, and the later varṇa, however, is not a tribe: it is a label. Just as the word Slave does not mean of Slavic origin today, the word Shudra becomes a label for anybody the expanding Arya people refuse to assimilate or do not get along well with. Now we get to the heart of something important: **a legal text or Vedic era manual written in a historical and socioeconomic context of tribal warfare is stripped of its entire context, and suddenly used as a universal text that is describing a historical reality of senseless evil.**

If the historical Shudra were a far North-Western non-Sanskrit speaking tribe, how on earth does a modern farmer from Assam or Telangana (who might feel like he belongs to the label Shudra) have anything in common to do with those people? And how on earth does that modern farmer and his “Shudra” caste fit into the historical context of the texts the earliest ritual and legal manuals were written in? **He does not.**

Not only were hierarchies normal in that world, so was tribal violence and tribal warfare. There was nothing inhumane about two rival tribes fighting each other and degrading each other in their texts or writings.

**You blame the Vedic people for this because their side of the story is the only one that survived, due to their complete dominance in spreading their religion. The other side of the story simply died out. This is why you have no oral texts, no written inscriptions or religious works from the Bronze Age non-Sanskrit speaking, non-Vedic worshipping tribes. They simply died out, or assimilated into, and became Arya.** ***Do you think Bronze/Iron Age Tribes that fought the Vedic people would have spoken nicely of them in their own stories?***

There is a lot more context I can go into, but this is an article, not a book. I might make more posts on this if the demand exists. I wanted to use one example to give you a peek into how scripture is actually to be interpreted and seen when you view it from the lens it was written in, and don’t just cast immediate judgements.

I now come to the second part of his question. The idea that certain ideas are fixed to be divine in our religion. This assumes two things:

1.  No traditional scholar or school in Hinduism ever interpreted
    scripture non-literally (i.e: we are *forced* to accept the entire
    received tradition as *unfallible*, *ahistorical* and unchangeable).

2.  There are no contrary opinions with regards to Varṇa in the received
    tradition.

This is again, untrue. Hinduism, like most polytheistic religions, is extremley ecletic and open to diversity in a lot of peripheral viewpoints, which is what makes the multiplicity of regional *acāra-s* and their limited but real validity for all legalist authors. What is also true is that no primary and organic religion that has survived for four-thousand plus years has done so without constantly evolving in a lot of things. Secondary religions, like Islam or Christianity, are frozen in time and frozen with one corpus of texts, this is what defines them. Scriptural literalism is the bedrock of their faith ***because their faith rests on historical facts; ours rests on the codification of eternal truths of our species into a formal system of philosophy and ritual.***

If Christ literally did not rise from the dead, or did not view himself as God, as most Biblical Scholarship views *today*, then all of Christianity is *meaningless*, and it falls from its foundations. Whether the core of the Rāmāyaṇa happened in 1500 BCE, or 800 BCE, or whether a Smṛti from 300 CE is divine revelation or not, does ***not*** serve as the litmus test of our religion. The *Vaiśya* *Varṇa* evolved from refering to agriculturalist producers (as it was in the RV, AV) into urban landowning merchants (as it became in the *Jaṭaka* texts). This does ***not*** falsify or prove anything about our religion.

The meat of the cow was eaten in the age of the *Gṛhya Sūtras*, but it has been prohibited for us for thousands of years now. This does not mean we will pick verses from those time and justify this habit today, because, truth be told, in a religion so vast and so old, you can justify ***anything*** from scripture. Historically, how evolutions and new ideas about right and wrong were codifed into our legal texts was based on collective wisdom of the smartest people in our religio-political sphere, combined with state power enforcing or shaping those ideas. The concept of *Kalivarjya*, or activities forbidden in the age of Kali, is not a concept that exists in the *Apastamba Sutra* that is dated to many centuries before the Common Era, but it is mentioned in every early-modern *Smṛti* since it serves as a powerful tool by the *Siṣṭa-Jana* of the time to rationalize their encounters with modernity.

In fact, an entire Smṛti, the *Parāśara*, comes up that declares itself as the legal text of the Kali Age (*kalau pārāśarāḥ smṛtāḥ*). Another *Smṛti* from Sindh, the *Devala*, declares the exact process for ritual purification and re-conversion of Hindus who were made Muslims and forced to eat beef, or Hindu women who were forcibly converted to Islam and made to have children with the Arabs in Sindh. Are these by any means situations that someone in the Vedic Age would have to deal with or discuss? If the legal code and its interpretation was frozen the way most Islamic jurisprudence is, could we have dealt with any of this? What about the ban on sea-travel, which started two thousand years ago in the *Baudhayana* but was cleverly done away with by great scholars like *Mahāmahopādhyāya Tāranātha Tarkavācaspati* during the 19^(th) century? Can we in any shape or form, pretend to harbour a serious religious tradition if we believe in every legal injunction from every *Smṛti* without paying heed to the world around us?

The problem you face today is a tradition that has no funding, no state patronage (lost in the 11^(th) century) and no mass recognition beyond lip-service is incapable of coherently providing modern defenses or interpretations that keep the best elements of our past while advancing forward. Take the question of caste.

In the pre-modern world, a stable hierarchical system that provided kinship, clan, business and marriage served as a building block of missing state capacity in a rural world that was centered around survival. *Jāti* networks helped enforce legal contracts in a world where the state’s legal apparatus was often missing. Different Jāti-s claimed to represent and belong to different *Varṇa*-s of the old three/four-fold classification.

In the past few centuries, have we ever tried to decide what the traditional approach to this should be in the modern world? Should we advocate for the survival of Jāti networks but the dissolution of *Varṇa*-based claims? There is, after all, the well-known idea in scripture that all the Varṇa-s were one in the Golden Age, and all of them will dissolve into one by the end of the age of Kali. (*c.f. Bhṛgu-Bharadwaja dialogue in the Shanti-Parva, na viśeṣo 'sti varṇānām*). Should the state take this approach? Should we do the opposite? When the Japanese were attempting to modernize, they would always rationalize present day measures (i.e: the abolition of certain caste distinctions) as pre-existing traditional solutions that always existed in the old Japanese state. If the Samurai caste was to be abolished, it was because in the old Japanese religion/state, before the Ashikaga Shogunate, it had never existed. It was a temporary measure that served its purpose and hence dissolving it was a return to tradition as well.

In India, the state and the people have never seriously engaged with any of these questions. Rather, our modernization has been a bonafide adoption of English Common Law and Western ideas wholescale into our society, with no hoots given to tradition. This creates a mess where every individual is left to decide his own opinion on these issues. Rather than updating our legal jurisprudence in line with our own inherited tradition, we have thrown the baby out with the bathwater.

So you get a society which not only does not want to modernize, it blames the past for the problems it suffers today, it harbors a deep distrust of pre-modern hierarchy and caste, while at the same time institutionalizing it in the political architecture of the state. The elements of our society that claim to adhere to the old traditional religion, do so with deep suspicion of any modernization, and view all of it as an affront to an eternal, unchanging and fixed tradition.

The complete lack of leadership by the Indian state post-independence for half a century created a boiling pot of distrust and suspicion among all communities, the brunt of which was inevitably borne by a religion that was a lot smarter than its adherents today. Hinduism suffers from a caricature of something it never was. A religion and civilization that, more than two thousand years ago, spread from Kabul to Sumatra, is today seen as an insular and restrictionist tradition due to the failures of those who claim to adhere to it today.
