*Book To Review: To Stand With The Nations Of The World: Japan’s Meiji Restoration in World History, Mark Ravina*

This article at *The High Table* (this is, indeed, the new *āṅgla-nāma* for our *goṣṭhī*) will serve as a succint review of Ravina’s contribution to understanding the Meiji Restoration and the people that drove Japan to radical change. In addition to giving you a quick guide on the book, and whether you should read it or not, I also hope to shed light on my understanding of Meiji-era Japan and where it is located in 18^(th)-19^(th) century Asia.

Japan, the *only* Asian nation to *stand* toe-to-toe with the European world at the peak of its power, has held a special place in the hearts and minds of most of the colonized world for many centuries, but especially for the Indian Subcontinent and Eastern Asia, given our close cultural, religious and political ties. [1](#footnote-1) We study Japan so we can make sense of our counties, and some of the reasons they failed to go on the same path: the *why*. It is the same for me, so the Meiji Restoration was not a new topic, but not one I had re-visited in a long time. After reading this book a few weeks ago, I spent some time thinking about the issue of modernization structurally and I think I finally understood the *why*, which I will link back to the contents of the book.

Let us begin, *alam vistareṇa.*

The title of the book, *bankoku ni heiritsu suru*, itself tells us a lot about how the Meiji reformers saw themselves, standing separately but equally with the tallest nations of the world. This theme of maintaining *separation* (of religion, culture, identity, founding mythology) but having *equality* (of industry, army, technology, strength) is something that echoes throughout the book, with Ravina usually listing out the Japanese words and phrases related to cultural pride and modernity that gained public parlance in the Meiji-era. The phrases or words themselves are not important to memorize, and you will likely even forget most of them, but an impression of a *people hyper-aware of and concerned with both the preservation of their unique national identity, and the transformation of their material reality* will be imprinted in your brain.

Ravina divides the book into six main chapters, with two additional chapters that serve as the introduction and conclusion. The first two main chapters focus on giving the reader a context of the origins of the Tokugawa Shogunate, and the political-cultural sphere that the Japanese people had inhabited for the better part of a millenia in the 19^(th) century. The introduction sets up the central theme that Ravina finds in Japanese history: *Japan is a nation that has, for more than 1500 years, constantly inhabited a politico-cultural realm dominated by a greater power, and it has constantly borrowed, innovated and adapted political, cultural, religious and technological ideas from them, changing them to fit Japanese realities; hence, the Meiji Restoration was not a one-off and unique event, but the conclusion of a long process that the Japanese people had always been used to.*

He puts to pen two concepts that we subconsciously understand, and any reader of history will recognize: ***cosmopolitan chauvinism*** and ***radical nostalgia***. This is the idea that radical changes to the decaying present of a country are justified as a return to the glorious past, and any borrowing of ideas from other cultural spheres are actually universal values not unique to the culture that created them, and thus, their universality actually rendering them useful to the culture that is doing the borrowing.

This is a pattern we are familiar with: whether it was the *Arya* *Samājī* reformers who justified the abolition of older *Paurāṇika* ideas as a return to the glorious *Vaidika* tradition, the *Puritans* in New England who justified abolishing Christmas on the charges of Pagan idolatry, or the *Salafis* who cast aside old Sunni tradition, on the grounds that they were bringing back *even older* Sunni tradition from the first three generations of Muslims (*the Salaf*). I think Ravina does not do enough justice in highlighting how radical nostalgia was never unique to Japan, and a lay reader might end up viewing it as a Japan-specific innovation.

The uniqueness of the Meiji reformers lay not in their ability to cast the present and future in the vocabulary of the past, or the fact that they mimic the courtly rituals and political symbolisms of the Tang Dynasty, ***but that they generally steer clear of wrong policy choices, and do the right policy decisions, consistently, for the better part of half a century*****.** They speed-run the entire process of modernization, and during the process, they rarely make radically wrong decisions, and the ones they do, they self-correct on.

The average Meiji reformer reminded me much of Sardar Vallabhai Patel, consistently more sensible than not-sensible, and somehow driven by a north star of national integration and prosperity maximalization. ***The difference is that while we had one, or two Patels, the Japanese state was filled with thousands of them, from the top to the bottom*****.** As a result, the not-so-sensible people in the Meiji state generally ended up on the losing side of the equation, either through war, or through elite replacement. A noticeable reversal in the way most third-world countries operate, *where the somewhat-sensible elites are a needle in the haystack.*

Why was this the case?

The first and second chapter of Ravina’s book offers the clues that one has to read through. The Meiji Restoration was not a completely unexpected reform movement and novel worldview that sprung out of nowhere, out of some static and feudal past, but it was the final conclusion of a much older project of reformation and power consolidation that had begun in the Tokugawa Shogunate itself. For my Indian readers, understand it this way: The Tokugawa Shogunate can be seen as a ***hypothetical Indian dynasty that had managed to consolidate complete power, break sub-national feudal domains and local dynasties, conquer most of the country and be at relative stability for centuries, then begin reforming on its own during the last stages of its rule, upon first contact with the Europeans***.

The Tokugawas were the Marathas if they had been in power for two centuries across India, and had ample time to consolidate everything or the Mughals if they had immediately started learning Copernicus and Newton upon contact with the English. So the *Meiji reformers begin with a state that is already national, consolidated, under a single imperial identity and has been absorbing European ideas for a while. Their job is to take it to the finish line, then beyond, and they do that very well.*

“The Tokugawa shogunate engaged in three periods of intense reform: the Kyōho reforms (1716–45), the Kansei reforms (1787–93), and the Tenpō reforms (1841–43). The boldest and most successful was the first, led by the eighth shogun Tokugawa Yoshimune. ”

The Kyōho reforms are largely economic and bureaucratic, and Ravina covers them in a few pages in the first two chapters. A more interesting insight is into some of the ideologues behind the reforms, men like Ogyu Sorai and Nishikawa Yoren. These state-connected philosophers openly advocate for embracing European knowledge via the Dutch, who used to trade in Japan then. [2](#footnote-2) This Dutch studies movement, *rangaku,* runs parallel to a nativist movement, *kokugaku*, that wants to purge not only Western but also Chinese influence from Japan, seeking to reconstruct Japans old pantheon of Gods free from the shadow of the Han, and purge the language of Chinese vocabulary.

The middle-ground is found in the *Mitogaku* school, which originates from the Mito domain (c.f. domain is a very large *zamindari*, sometimes like a princely state) and fuses the best of both ideas, coining the infamous phrase *Sonno Joi: Revere The Emperor, Expel The Barbarians.* The author points to the irony of how the Mito domain, where the school is from, is ruled by a cadet branch of the Tokugawa House, and how the ideological fuel for Imperial Restoration and reform that brings down the Tokugawas, was provided by the Tokugawas. This is a trend we see again and again throughout this period: *Samurai bring down the Samurai order*. Mito scholars like *Aizawa Sensei*, whom we have posted about **before**, writing in the 19th century, argued about how Christianity served as a way of mobilizing and consolidating political power for Europeans, and how Japan likewise needed to unify and integrate around the idea of the Imperial Throne.

“But he (Aizawa) astutely understood the need for a new Japanese ideology, something that could unite the Japanese people. Aizawa’s thinking thus encompassed a powerful tension: he was repulsed by Christianity but enthralled by its power. His vision of Japanese religion was thus both retaliation and imitation. “We must transform \[the Western barbarians\] by appropriating the very methods that they now seek to use to transform us.” The only way to resist the West was to emulate the West, using religion for mass mobilization.”

By the end of the second chapter, it becomes clear the seeds of what would become the Meiji were more than in place, they were sprouting plants, and the political structure was still a lot more united than anything we had in India. Any post-Mughal Indian power had to first focus on the project of political power consolidation by conquering different recalcitrant dynasties in a much larger geosphere, before they could even focus on reform. This project was cut-short by the British Raj, but in Japan this project had been completed centuries before. ***It only needed a regime change.***

The next four chapters discuss in detail the period from the *1850s-1880s* and the book roughly cuts off there. So for someone looking for a complete political and socioeconomic history of Japan well into the 20th century, the conclusions of the Meiji period and the beginnings of the new Japanese State, this is not the right book. This is a small and tasty morsel that makes you extremley curious about the period, and encourages you to feast upon whatever else you find.

Ravina discusses that before Perry even came to Japan, the cost of military modernization had become an important topic in Japanese politics, since most of the different domains did not want to pay out of their pocket to build warships and the tax base of the Shogun was limited. Upon his arrival, the desire to modernize simply gains more steam, it does not just spring up from nowhere. His arrival brings neither astonishment nor fear, but evokes even greater curiosity.

“But in Japan, many were more curious than astonished by Perry’s machines. Having examined translations of gunnery manuals, for example, Japanese military experts were eager to see American cannon firsthand and they exhausted Perry’s staff with detailed and exacting questions. Perry’s two steamships made a striking impression as they billowed smoke and sailed into the wind. But local military experts had been studying steam engines for years, so the technology was well known, if only in the abstract. Similarly, ordinary samurai were more curious than terrified by the foreigners and their weapons.”

The period between 1854-1868, the *Bakumatsu* or last days of the Shogunate, sees the Shogunate lose political authority quite rapidly, at the cost of a rising Imperial loyalist faction and greater Daimyo authority. ***Tokugawa Yoshinobu***, the last Shogun, was a very competent leader who had begun a massive modernization program and regained authority for the Shogunate with the Imperial court, and by 1867, the Shogunate was arguably more ‘modern’ than an Imperial-restoration coalition made up of the ***Satsuma-Chosu*** domains that was opposing it.

“As a result of these changes, by late 1867, the shogunate was more “modern” than the imperial alliance. The shogunate had, in embryonic form, anticipated many early Meiji reforms: a European-style cabinet system with nationwide financial and economic authority, economic development informed by the advice of Western technical specialists, the abolition of hereditary status distinctions and obligations, and national conscription for a new, modern army and navy.”

The Tokugawa Shogunate was not finally toppled because it was a static and closed system that was only jolted to action by the iron battleships of Commodre Perry, but because the militarized domains of Southwestern Japan, led by the newly industrializing ***Satsuma*** domain, wanted to carry out a coup and believed they could do the mission of modernization better, and faster. In fact, the key demand of Imperial restoration, the desire to bring power back to the Emperor, had been granted by Yoshinobu before the coup even began, much to the fury of the ‘Imperial’ coalition, whose demands were now more or less exposed as the naked power grab that they were.

When war began, it was not a coalition of modernists fighting a coalition of feudalists, *it was two modernizing parties from different dynastic domains fighting for control of the nation*. As Ravina points out later, the new Meiji State was simply the old Tokugawa State with a new ruling regime since a large chunk of the old elite had been incorporated into the new state. The pace of reforms would stay slow, and gradual over a decade, till they finally got radical enough to dismantle the old Samurai order completely, which would trigger, much like the United States, the ***Japanese Civil War of 1877***, led ironically, by the Satsuma domain.

You would think what triggered a civil war is the Meiji State issuing a proclamation to end the Daimyo order and abolish the feudal domains, but on the contrary, Ravina says the old order goes away without a fight when the proclamation to end the domains is given in 1871, because the liege-lords had themselves been co-opted into the new Meiji regime, given generous pensions, privileges and senior gubernatorial positions ruling their own domains but as servants of the state, not hereditary feudalist lords. ***This is only partially correct.***

While the Samurai elites had been co-opted in most of the country, many *common Samurai increasingly found themselves developing resentment at both the nature of reforms, as well as the direction of Meiji foreign policy*. The nascent Meiji ruling elite had split itself into two: a delegation that toured the Western world for two years, known as the Iwakura Mission, and a caretaker government in Tokyo that stayed behind to govern the country. Both factions were comprised of the Samurai aristocracy that had helped topple the Tokugawa Shogunate, but both took increasingly different approaches to the future of the Japanese polity and its foreign policy.

The Iwakura Mission was star-struck by the nature of development and industrialization that it witnessed in Europe and America, and wasted no time in learning the historical and political context behind these changes. They absorbed the Napoleonic Wars, the Rise of Prussia, the American Civil War and the Glorious Revolution of England. They quickly became converts to the Prussian state-building model and became suspicious of the excesses of the French Revolution, becoming convinced that a pre-modern and agrarian country could not be industrialised with mass democracy and a cacophony of bad advice from all corners.

“The members of the Iwakura Mission, by contrast, were committed to the steady and deliberate consolidation of state power. It would take generations, not months or years, for Japan to rival Western economic power and, in the interim, the Japanese people needed guidance more than liberation. How could Japanese commoners lead the state when they lacked an appreciation of the Industrial Revolution? The transformation of Japan into a great power would require not liberation but state leadership, especially in the economy. The Japanese people could enjoy civil rights but only under the aegis of a powerful, developmental state”

The caretaker government back home had become swooning defenders of the principles of the French Revolution, and saw the creation of a populist Republican democracy with individual rights and liberty as essential to the process of modernization. Ravina takes care to point out that historically, the caretaker government were seen as conservative reactionaries opposed to industrialization, but in contemporary writings they were using the all-too-familiar language of the rights of man. The detail that matters here is that they had fused Republican ideals with Samurai populism. Rapid social reforms by the caretaker government were justified as the democratization of the Samurai spirit to all of Japan. Rather than discarding everything about the old ruling caste of the country as antiquated and backwards, the government would extend the best of their values to the entire nation: stubborn Samurai honor, aggressive war diplomacy and mass conscription that will make the common peasant pay the price of building modern Japan in blood.

“While actively dismantling the hereditary privileges of the samurai estate, the caretaker government sought to create a Japanese nation that embodied the best of samurai tradition.”

Of course, it is a different matter that some Japanese peasants literally thought the last part was about a blood tax that would be sucked out of them, so they started panicking. Dissenters within this ruling bloc argued that Japan was an extremely backwards nation that was unprepared for such hasty and unthoughtful policy. It did not have a comprehensive tax structure, any modern civil or criminal law code and it had no governing structure of who could do what, which agency was responsible for what, if it even existed. It could not be made into France or Germany with two years of primary schooling and brash rhetoric about conquering Korea on the slighest of perceived insults.

“But the Japanese people were simply not that sophisticated. Japanese merchants haggled over petty profits instead of engaging in world trade. Japanese craftsmen were ignorant of modern machinery, and Japanese farmers relied on the local gentry for their knowledge of agriculture. The samurai were supposedly the “parents of the people,” but they were, in fact, ignorant of both warfare and public administration. It would take years to raise the Japanese people to Western standards. In the interim, the rashness of the caretaker government’s reforms was toxic, like a too-potent medicine that kills rather than cures the patient. The government needed to move cautiously and to emphasise state power over popular support”

The Iwakura Mission would come back horrified to find that the state was catering to populist sentiment of disgruntled Samurai and riling up common peasants to go invade Korea and Taiwan. Rather than taking a measured approach to building state power and carrying out state-led industrialization, they were teaching Thomas Paine’s The Rights of Man to illiterate Samurais in Satsuma and recklessly printing money to finance their public spending without first reforming and streamlining their domestic revenue collection.

Almost all Samurai had been given generous stipends and grants after 1871, but many squandered that money rather than investing it or starting a business with it, since they saw knowledge of business as an insult, and took pride in being financially illiterate, to the point where doing business like a Samurai (*shizoku no shōhō*) became a phrase of mockery. To people like this, you can see why the idea of invading Taiwan to defend Imperial honor or extending Samurai honor culture to all of Japan sounded quite appealing; it provided one avenue to avenge their lost pride and status by an instrument they were all too familiar with.

“For many ex-samurai, the imagined glory of conquering Korea and Taiwan was a substitute for their lost privilege and prestige. That emphasis on samurai honor fused surprisingly well with demands for popular governance. Insisting that there was a popular mandate for war, activists recast samurai belligerence as a democratic value.”

The caretaker government losing power to the returning Iwakura Mission that cracked down on all of these reckless antics would not be seen kindly by this dissatisfied faction, sowing the seeds of rebellion.

Shortly after the Iwakura Mission takes back power with the support of the Imperial Court, the now terminated members of the caretaker government form a political oppositional bloc that doubles down on the need for democratic values, free speech and a free press. Democracy is essential to get your views across when one is not in power, and it is essential to appeal to the demos to hold on to power. *What if appealing to the demos is not the right thing to do at the time? This is not something to worry about for now.*

Mimicry of the West was mostly seen as high status in Meiji political discourse across both sides of the aisle by now, so the opposition to the Okubo-led government would style themselves as American revolutionaries opposing no taxation without representation, while the Okubo [3](#footnote-3) government would see itself as prudent and careful Prussians cutting down the false siren of *Liberté*, *égalité*, *fraternité.*

“The losers of the 1873 Political Crisis, now out of power, insisted that the state heed popular grievances. In January 1874, key members of the now-defunct caretaker government published a petition denouncing the Ōkubo administration as autocratic (dokuyūshi) and declaring the people’s right to participate in government affairs. They invoked both Western principles, such as “no taxation without representation,” and older Confucian-style critiques of the government for its failure to acknowledge respectful dissent (genro yōhei).”

The similarities to the United States would not just be limited to boisterous rhetoric by one side, with even the Okubo-administration taking conscious policy decisions to mimic *Americana*, nudged by their American advisors on the issue, they would go on to create their own ideal of Manifest Destiny with the ‘settler-colonization’ of Hokkaido. The fact they have a civil war on the issue of the nature of reforms in Japan shortly after the American Civil War is just cherry on the cake. *To me, it really did seem like Japan speed ran 100 years of American history in 10 years*. Perhaps this is a comment on how quickly transformative change spreads, but how slowly it originates, or perhaps it is just my sentimental reading of the whole thing.

“These (American) advisors explicitly cited the settlement of the American West as a model for Hokkaido. Japan, they declared, needed land grant colleges, like those created by the Morrill Act of 1862. It needed to encourage the migration of farmers by copying the Homestead Act of 1862. It needed cattle ranches, dairy farms, and apple orchards, all ideally with American-style organization and architecture. “The inaugural issue of the official publication, the Hokkaido Development Journal (Hokkaidō kaitaku zasshi), compared Japanese settlers in Hokkaido to the Mayflower Pilgrims, pioneers in a new land”

Another thing that I realized, which I had not thought about before, was that Japan was behaving and acting like any other ambitious European (or, American) country that was behind England in the 1870s. Japan was not starting its reforms after the start of the post WWII socioeconomic liberal order, but almost a hundred years before it. Japan’s Civil War was nearly contemporaneous to America’s Civil War. Japan became a world power standing shoulder-to-shoulder with the United States, it was not thrown into a duo-polar hegemony as a post-Colonial nation whose confused elites were educated with the progressive wisdom of the 1920s. *It could afford to ignore bad advice, because to a good extent, there was a lot less of it.* Most ‘enlightened’ countries then agreed on the primacy of nationalism, on the value of industrialization, and the need for a national identity.

They differed on how to go about getting it, but by the 1960s, there wasn’t even a consensus on these things. In the 1960s, there were changed expectations on how a nation should act and behave; it was not normal to fight a bloody civil war to crush oppositional dissent or casually speak of denying franchise to illiterate peasants or burn endless coal to build factories. The world had changed. There were international agencies to regulate everything now. There were carbon credits. There was universal adult franchise. There was *world peace*. *Japan, unlike India, did not come into this changed world as a still-born; Japan is what **led** to it.*

The inevitable Civil War of 1877 that we were building upto so far was a coalition of a wide-range of actors who all shared some grievances with the Okubo government, and the only thing that united them was, ironically, their dislike of the government. The powerful Satsuma domain that led the Meiji restoration leading the rebels might come of as surprising till you realize that it had the highest Samurai population in all the domains. Nearly 1/3^(rd) of the domain was Samurai, and they extended all the way into the deep rural areas of Satsuma. That was a lot of disgruntled people who felt betrayed by the very state they helped create. The people who fought to bring on the restoration would now be its first real casualties of war.

Satsuma was entirely governed and controlled by rebellious Samurai led by the great Saigo Takamori, one of the ‘Three Great Nobles’ who brought about the Meiji Restoration. A high population of Samurai combined with regional military training academies (*Sagakko*) and a disgruntled but respected grand old man led to fertile grounds for rebellion, and Tokyo was rightfully concerned about it, frequently granting them special exemptions to national laws and ignoring loose implementations of land tax reform.

This was, however, not just a rebellion of disgruntled Samurais clinging on to ancient privileges, but it had popular support from ‘*Samurai populists*’ who saw themselves as enlightened and educated Samurais opposing the tyranny of an autocratic government that was defiling Japanese honor by either not creating a popular national assembly, or not having a more militaristic approach to international diplomacy. This was linked to the broader political climate of the time, with the Okubo government cracking down heavily on dissenting newspaper articles in a country that was increasingly reading more and more.

Just like at the start of the Meiji Restoration, even now, both parties in the Civil War wanted a strong and powerful Japan that honoured the Imperial Throne, and both parties did not want to abandon Japanese tradition. They different on how to get there, what pace of reforms to have and *which* Western idea or nation to emulate. Sure, there were radical Samurais that wanted to fight with steel swords and consult oracles before every battle, but even the most reactionary and the most well-respected of their leaders, Saigo Takamori, was committed to the above-mentioned principles.

“But Saigō’s vision of Japan went well beyond a defense of tradition. His approach to the Shigakkō, for example, suggests that he was hoping to transform the samurai estate into a self-reliant yeomanry. At the school directly patronized by Saigō, the Yoshino Kaikonsha, students read Chinese classical texts and practiced military drills, but they also raised their own food crops, such as millet and Japanese sweet potatoes. While many of the Shigakkō were fiercely xenophobic, Saigō recruited foreign language teachers and invited a British physician to Kagoshima, the Satsuma castle town, to teach medicine. Scattered in Saigō’s aphorisms and poems was a vision of the samurai as frugal, courageous, self-reliant young patriots who could thrive even when stripped of their hereditary income and authority. That vision was capacious enough to enthrall both advocates of constitutional government and defenders of the samurai tradition.”

There was no doubt that Japan could not progress ahead on all this without finally having a long-due reckoning on what was going on, and for that reason, it seems as if the Civil War was essential in their roadmap of nation-building, for it removed, for once and for all, the possibility of bringing about radical change in the country through violence and bloodshed.

“In that sense, the War of the Southwest was a necessary final step in the consolidation of the Meiji state. The Tokio Times accordingly compared the victory of the Meiji government over Satsuma to the victory of the Union over the Confederacy: “Widespread throughout the empire it is accepted and appreciated, as never before, that this is one country;—not a bundle of semi-sovereign and jealous powers, but a nation. . . . That this, an inevitable crisis, here as in America, has been fairly met and satisfactorily adjusted is matter for congratulation.”

The Satsuma Rebellion ended on September 24, 1877 when Saigo and his lieutenants died in a final suicide charge. Much like the American Civil War, these were not two alien powers from distinct cultures and nations fighting a war of conquest, they were brethren who had fought together to bring about this new order but were not spilling each other’s blood to decide who would shape it. This woe is perhaps best captured in Yamagata Aritomo’s final letter to Saigo asking him to surrender, not included in this book, but worth quoting here.

As the leader of young vigorous men of the nation and resisting a great and famous army through many fierce battles, your fame is already well enough known to the world. But now, most of your brave generals are dead or injured, and your military power is daily growing weaker. It is already clear that your Satsuma army is unable to keep its spirit. I don’t know what further things you can hope to achieve, except a hopeless defensive battle. If you, Saigo, rather than lengthening the rest of your days a little, prefer to cause hundreds and thousands of dead and wounded from both armies, there is nothing that I, Aritomo, can say to you. I beg you to judge for yourself as you hold matters in the palm of your hand, and that you will save both armies from death and injury. Ah, you are a man famous and known in the world. About our national constitution, naturally it is not only Aritomo who knows what you really think. But the matter has been settled over many years of public debate. I, Aritomo, earnestly beg you as your old friend. Writing this, my tears fall like rain, and I cannot express myself at all. Please sympathise with the sincerity of my pain.

Okubo would have his own John Wilkes Booth and was assassinated just around six months later, on May 17, 1878, by six former Samurai who claimed to be upholding the true principles of the Imperial Edict of 1875 against the tyranny of the autocratic government.

*History doesn't repeat itself, but it often rhymes.*

The book ends about here, with the three great men (Kido, Okubo, Saigo) who brought about the Imperial Restoration all dead, and with Ravina taking a few more pages of the last chapter to hastily explain the political changes and power grabs after Okubo’s death. This is perhaps not the best treatment of the period between 1878-1890, since it seems that Ravina is trying to just end the book here, but it does give us one clear idea. The Meiji oligarchy would finally agree to a National Assembly and Constitution, but one that it would write. It would take on the pretenses and ritual necessities of being a sensible constitutional monarchy, but still hold on to the reins of power as the paternalistic guarantor of prosperity for the Japanese people. Much like Bismarck, it would have a rubber stamp assembly, and it would ‘listen’ to popular discourse in newspapers and by dissenting political parties, but still do what it felt was best. Just like the United States or early Britain, it would have the right to vote, but only for 1% of the population.

Above all, the state would be committed to the idea of gradualism.

“The 1881 Political Crisis, however, marked a decisive narrowing of political choices. Instead of rapid and radical change, the Meiji state was now decisively committed to gradualism. Japan would challenge the unequal treaties and build its own colonial empire, but only after decades of economic development and domestic reform. The people would have a voice in their government, but that, too, would be part of a gradual state-directed process. The government would fulfill its 1881 promise of a constitution, but that document would be drafted in secret by Itō Hirobumi and presented to the Japanese people in 1889 as a gift from the emperor. While cognizant of popular discontent, the state officially treated popular constitutional drafts as irrelevant and illegitimate. Once promulgated, the 1889 constitution remained, unaltered and unamended, as Japan’s supreme national law until 1947. ”

What emerges from the crucible of these twenty years of turmoil is a settled national consensus on the approach to governance and development. For better or worse, this consensus would dominate Japan till the Second World War. Ravina’s book is a hard recommend for a short understanding of this period of turmoil. It is the backdrop to the big picture that Japan will produce shortly after, and it is also the backdrop to understand our own countries, and the causes of their failures in not building the same consensus.

Japan had the fertile ingredients of reform: it was a largely ethnically and linguistically homogeneous country ruled by one Imperial House for almost 2000 years. It was not yet a nation, but it was ripe to be one. It had a long tradition of absorbing knowledge from China, from India, from the Dutch and the Portuguese, before first encounter with Perry’s Americans. It was in the right place, at the right time, it just caught the last ticket to the ship of rapacious industrial growth at all costs before it sailed, and it caught the last ticket to the ship of limited popular franchise in a largely illiterate and agrarian world. It had enough sailors from the elite section of its ruling caste that could steer the ship, people who were willing to abandon their own privileges for the greater good of the nation, and it had enough grit to put down those same people when they threatened the Restoration.

These were ships we failed to board as a nation, these were winds and tidings we lacked and capable sailors we did not have. So here we are.
