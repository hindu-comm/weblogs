devAmR^ita-varShiNI – देवामृत वर्षिणी

## Notes on the Vaiṣṇava Contexts of Kṛtyā, Pratyaṅgirā, and the VaikhānasaDhātrī

[![](https://goghritam.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/gemini_generated_image_bsnldibsnldibsnl.png?w=572)](https://goghritam.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/gemini_generated_image_bsnldibsnldibsnl.png)

Following the āchārya’s note on the [kṛtyā Pratyaṅgirā,](https://manasataramgini.wordpress.com/2005/02/20/pratyangira-and-kritya-prayoga/) this brief note is meant to capture a couple of contexts for the deity known as Pratyaṅgirā/Kṛtyā across the Vaikhānasa and Pāñcarātra saṃhitās as well as assorted Vaiṣṇava ritual material.

The Pāñcarātrika *Parama Saṃhitā* explicitly recommends that Durgā and Kṛtyā are to be invoked for *śatru-nāśana*, noting that both devatās arise from (or are stationed within) the gadā Kaumodakī of Viṣṇu. Apart from this unambiguous mention, the pairing of Kṛtyā and Durgā is particularly noteworthy, as it forms a consistent motif across several texts and is even corroborated iconographically in certain temple complexes where these devatās appear as a pair.

In another Pāñcarātra saṃhitā detailing a *māraṇa-prayoga* utilizing Vaiṣṇava mantras, it is prescribed that after fourteen days of continuous homa, a Kṛtyā arises from the homa-kuṇḍa around midnight. The description of this Kṛtyā closely mirrors that of a fierce devī bearing a triśūla, kapāla-mālā, and kuṇḍalas, her tongue rendered bright red with blood. This rite is followed by the japa of the Nārasiṃha and Aparājitā mantras on the twenty-second day, accompanied by a lakṣa-japa of the aṣṭākṣarī.

This formulation/practice of the kR^itya generating rite, being followed up by the nR^isiMha and aparAjitA (the pA~ncharAtrika synonym for durgA – the vaiShNavI aparAjitA mantra) appears to be a precursor to the classical *bhūta-nigraha* rites centered on Narasiṃha flanked by Nārasiṃhī and Viṣṇu-Durgā. As in the *Parama Saṃhitā*, here too Nārasiṃhī and Viṣṇu-Durgā occur as an indissoluble pair, where Nārasiṃhī is positioned to the right of Nṛsiṃha and the eighteen-armed Viṣṇu-Durgā to his left. Notably, the Nārasiṃhī mantra in this context exhibits unmistakable structural parallels to that of Pratyaṅgirā.

A third Pāñcarātra text, the *Ahirbudhnya Saṃhitā*, deals extensively with rituals designed to counter and hurl back hostile *kṛtyās*, yet it simultaneously exalts the base mantra from which the famed *kṛtyāstra* is derived. It is not without good reason, then, that certain practitioners/yoginI-s combine Sudarśana and Pratyaṅgirā, a pairing documented across several regional tantric traditions.

Having said that, such a combination requires caution and proper direction from an experienced teacher. Another female practitioner in our circle who has some affinity to both, and occasionally maintains dual practice of Bhadrakālī and Sudarśana noted an interesting practical observation: despite giving equal commitment to both, only one current tends to dominate during daily *anuṣṭhāna*. They seem to alternate as the primary *tejas* or operative presence of the day, driven by subtle factors that are difficult to isolate.

It is equally noteworthy that the designation **Aparājita / Aparājitā** recurs across the invocations of all three i.e. Durgā, Pratyaṅgirā, and Sudarśana (as in the mantra ‘bhagavān sarva-vijaya… aparājita…’). Rather than being an incidental epithet, this shared title highlights their common function as infallible, counter-offensive forces. Each represents a distinct facet of an unconquerable Vaiṣṇava protective apparatus designed to neutralize and turn back hostile sorcery. This aspect is hardly surprising when one considers that certain mantras address Pratyaṅgirā explicitly as *Kṛṣṇa-bhaginī* (*Kṛṣṇa*‘s sister).

#### The Vaikhānasa Correlate: The Six-Armed, Reddish Dhātrī

Turning to the Vaikhānasa stream, one encounters the obscure figure of **Dhātrī**. While the name commonly evokes either the gentle maternal sustainer (identified with Bhū-devī) or the sacred Āmalakī tree in the context of the Kārttika *vana-bhojana*, the Vaikhānasa saṃhitās preserve an entirely different, esoteric stratum. Here, Dhātrī appears as a terrifying, raudra-śakti bearing a striking morphological and functional resemblance to Pratyaṅgirā and Kṛtya, and in certain ritual lineages, her mantra is combined with mantra-s of Pratyaṅgirā.

In these texts, Dhātrī is visualized as completely blood-red:

- **Varṇa and Attire:** Her complexion is blazing coral-red
  (*rakta-varṇā*), she is draped exclusively in crimson garments
  (*rakta-vastra-saṃvītā*), anointed with red sandal paste, and even her
  disheveled locks of hair (*mukta-keśī* / *vikeśī*) flame with a
  coppery, reddish-tawny hue.
- **Āyudhas:** She is envisioned as six-armed (*ṣaḍ-bhujā*), prominently
  brandishing the triśūla and kapāla alongside the khaḍga, kheṭaka
  (shield), bāṇa, and cāpa.Interestingly, her textually prescribed
  signifier is the **piśāca-dhvaja** (‘piśāca-dhvaja-vāhanā’),
  underscoring her sovereign command over the spectral classes and
  reinforcing her role as an apotropaic sentinel stationed at the outer
  limits of the sacred enclosure.
- **Character:** Far from benign, she is invoked in the *Vaikhānasa
  Mantra Praśna* with epithets emphasizing her ferocious,
  consumer-of-essence aspect:*caṇḍī harī bhīṣaṇī rudra-rūpī dhātrī
  dharitrī rasa-hārī ca loke \|śūlī jaṭī mukta-keśī vikeśī svāhā \|\|*

#### Her Utsava Prior to the Brahmotsava

What makes this red Dhātrī particularly significant in Vaikhānasa ritual mechanics is the specific **Dhātrī-utsava** prescribed prior to the main festival of Viṣṇu. Before the *dhvajārohaṇa* (flag-hoisting) of the Lord’s Mahotsava can be undertaken, an apotropaic festival dedicated to this goddess must be performed at the periphery of the temple settlement (*grāma-sīmā* / *prākāra-bāhya*):

1.  **Purpose:** The rite is fundamentally one of *dik-bandhana* and
    *vighnotsāraṇa*—pacifying and neutralizing the hosts of bhūtas,
    pretas, vetālas, and malevolent planetary forces (*grahas*) so that
    the sanctified space remains immune to disturbance during the Lord’s
    principal ceremonies. 2.  **The Bali Rite:** Outside the settlement, an altar is prepared
    using five-colored powders dominated by vermilion. Unlike standard
    Vaiṣṇava offerings of śuddhānna, payasam, and tulasī, Dhātrī is
    propitiated with *rakta-bali*—boiled rice saturated with turmeric
    and lime to produce a vivid blood-red hue (*kṣārodaka-rakta-odana*),
    combined with parched grains, red oleander/hibiscus flowers, and
    sesame-oil wicks. 3.  **Circumambulation and Banishment:** To the accompaniment of harsh,
    martial percussion instruments (*ḍhakkā*, *bherī*, and *kāhala*),
    her bali is carried along the boundaries of the town in a strict
    pradakṣiṇa. The offerings are cast down at the four-way crossroads
    (*catuṣpatha*) to satisfy and bind the outer spirit retinues.

Only after Dhātrī’s wrathful aspect is fully propitiated and the protective perimeter established do the arcakas perform the *puṇyāhavācana* within the temple, paving the way for the auspicious festival of Mahāviṣṇu to commence undisturbed.

In this rare Vaikhānasa rite, we find another clear instance of the broader Vaiṣṇava āgamic pattern: a fierce, red-hued, skull-and-trident-bearing female divinity—whether designated as Kṛtyā, Pratyaṅgirā, or Dhātrī—stationed on the dangerous periphery to absorb pollution, execute destructive sorcery against inimical forces, and secure the ritual boundaries of the Supreme Deity.
