viśadavaak

Today is the 21st of June, 2016. We are celebrating the **International Yoga Day**. It is a matter of pride that we represent a country that has contributed extensively to the wellness of mankind at large.I am personally not a great fanof such ***day*** celebrations. However, this World yoga day is something that I strongly support and agree with.

Even as we are celebrating this day, we see many programmes organized by all yoga schools at various locations (either individually or as a group of institutions coming together for a common event). In most cases, the practitioners and students of yoga tend to focus more on the asanas and pranayama (if at all). But that is not all of the ‘yoga’ that India has known for all these days.

I have personally seen in many instances how the trainers themselves judge a person on theiryogic practices purely based on their body’s flexibility. There are so many competitions held for ‘asanas’, and I have seen people going so much judgemental on some mean factors at such places!There are also many schools of yoga such as power yoga, dance yoga, etc which are actually not***yoga*** in the true sense at all! People are being royally misguided by these catchy words and flashy ads of building flexibility and health through their respective schools of yoga.

There’s more salt to this misery. There are umpteen number of schools which are named after sage**Patanjali**, such as Patanjali yoga kendra, etc. At the least, most schools boast of teaching ‘pure patanjali yoga’, while they are actually teaching acrobatics inside! All this makes me wonder how people get carried away easily by flashy ads alone. Of course, the beginners have no idea about yoga, and are made to believe all thatthey are taught to be the sole truth.

In another aspect, it is popularly believed that yoga is not for the Sri Vaishnavas, because they are – after all –***prapannas***. But is this all true?

This post (series) aims at educating the masses about the true purport of yoga, and its connection with*sampradaya* or tradition.

The word**yoga**comes from the sanskrit root***yuj***, meaning ‘to unite’ (***yuji samaadhau***). Inshort, this practice is called ‘yoga’ because something is united with it (***yujyate anena iti yogah***). The current day practitioners popularly take this definition alone and start interpreting it in the wrong way. They say, ‘yoga is the union of body, mind and soul’. But that is not all.

India isthe seat of philosophy – represented by the six darshanas viz., *nyaya*, *vishesha*, *sankhya*, *yoga*, *mimamsa* and *vedanta*. All these philosophies have a common goal of awakeningthe practitioner spiritually. Of these, the Vedanta philosophy is considered to be*apaurusheya*, meaning – it is not ‘composed’ by anyone. Also, Vedanta is considered to be the ultimate source of spiritual knowledge because it crisply teaches us the purpose of life, our true nature as souls, and our relation with the supreme godhead; whereas the other schools contextually touch upon various other aspects – some being mundane in nature as well. So it is a universally accepted norm that the preachings of all darshanas are accepted to the extent that they conform to the teachings of Vedanta. The parts that are deviating from the teachings of Vedanta are ignored.

Yoga is one of the six darshanas that India has presented to the world, making us proud. This is a class apart from all regular*exercises* in the sense that it has an additional aspect of *spirituality* attached to it. Whereas all other categories of exercises focus on bodily development and maintenance, yoga is equipped to develop a person physically, mentally as well as spiritually. This is shown by sage Patanjali in his yoga sutras, where he clearly shows how the practitioner develops spiritually by practicing yoga. It actually becomes evident from the fact that he never discussesthe numerous asanas that we popularly know today. Rather, he only suggests the characteristics of an ‘asana’ that the pupilmay practice. The context of such exposition clearly indicates that he is more focussed on*meditative*postures rather than all the standing, sitting, lying and supine postures. He clearly says*sthirasukham aasanam*, which means an asana is characterized by a posture that is both firm and convinient to the practitioner. There is practically nothing more to the asanas expounded by Patanjali, apart from this characteristic.
