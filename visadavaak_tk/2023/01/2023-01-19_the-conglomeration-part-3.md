viśadavaak

In the previous post, we last saw about jeevAtmas taking birth as piece of rock, log of wood etc. are these births purely due to curse? Or does every piece of rock, a log of wood, a pot, cloth etc. have a jeevatma? We shall examine this now. This post is more of purvapaksham, or the first side of argument.

Some people opine that the jeevAtmas taking birth as a rock, wood etc. occurs purely due to a special event such as a curse. Therefore, the probability of accepting a jeevAtma within every instance of such bodies is a rare event. For example, Himavaan, Vindhya, Meru, Samudrarajan, Ganga, Yamuna et all are some specific instances where some jeevatmas identify themselves with those insentient entities. Therefore, we cannot claim that every mountain or every waterbody has a jeevatma. In their opinion, the meaning of “Sthavara”, is limited to plants, trees, herbs. The abhimaani jeeva, identifying with the other pieces of rock, wood etc., is a common jeevatma called “Abhimani Devata”. Therefore, when the shastras say that, the jeevatmas are born in 4 different types of births called deva, manushya, tiryak and sthavara, the things like stone, wood, mud and all other objects created out of these lack indwelling jeevatmas, as they are generally outside the definition of ‘sthaavara’. But, Emberumanar clearly says that, even the mud, wood, pot, cloth etc. are also identified with a jeevatma. Then how do they reconcile this? They say that, the “Abhimanai devata” identifies itself with all such instances. In specific cases such as Ahalya, Rambha et all, their birth as a stone is an exception. Only in such exceptional cases, the abhimani jeevatma indwells that particular body. Whereas, in general, the abhimani jeeva never indwells such things, but resides separately. It just identifies itself with those things.

The pramanam they take for this claim is from the Sri Bhashya, Chapter 2- Vilakshanatvaadhikaranam.  
“अहल्यादीनां कर्मनिमित्तशिलाकाष्ठादिशरीरेषु इन्द्रियाश्रयत्वं सुखदुःखहेतुत्वं च अव्याप्तम् ॥”  
While inquiring into what is the real meaning of ‘aatmaa’ and ‘shareera’, Emberumanar dwells deeply upon the definition of shareera or body. He says, body cannot be called as just the supporting framework for the senses, or as the abode of experiencing joy and sorrow. This is because, these attributes of being a framework for the senses or enjoying joy and sorrow cannot be seen in the insentient bodies such as a stone or wood when they become bodies for Ahalya and others due to their own actions.

The phrase to be highlighted here is “कर्मनिमित्तशिलाकाष्ठादिशरीरेषु” (within the stone/wooden/other bodies taken due to one’s own action). Their interpretation for this statement is that Ahalya and others got their bodies of rock, wood etc. out of their karma (which led them to be cursed). But that is not the case with other rocks, woods, pots etc. Therefore, we cannot claim that those things have an indwelling jeeva.

In another adhikaranam called Tadantarapratipatyadhikaranam, there is an inquiry into how the prana is treated, and what happens to a person (and the prana) when he dies. Emberumanar’s words in that context are as follows:  
‘ओषधीर्लोमानि वनस्पतीन् केशाः’ इत्यनपियद्भिर्लोमादिभिस्सह श्रवणात् । अतः चक्षुराद्यप्यय श्रुतिरधिष्ठातृदेवतापक्रमणपरा ॥ ३-१-४  
Here, he introduces us to this concept of ‘adhishtaatr devataa’ or the governing devata. He quotes the Brihadaranyaka upanishat to show how prana is stated on par with hair, eyes and other parts of the body, and therefore, by context, it doesn’t essentially mean that prana is limited to the commonly known ‘air’ but a special entity that is called ‘life force’. In that context, Emberumanar says that prana cannot be limited to air or vaayu just because the brihadaranyaka upanishat says that the prana of a dead person merges with air or vaayu. The brihadaranyaka upanishat also speaks about hair of that dead person merging with herbs and plants. But that is much against what we see in reality. Therefore, the meaning of this statement of brihadaranyaka is not about those parts of body merging physically with those said entities, but more to do with the governing devatas.

The purvapaksha interpretation of this statement of Emberumanar is thus: the governing devatas or abhimani/adhishtatr devatas take over the abhimana of those parts. For example, the person was claiming the hair, eyes, ears, etc as his own when he was alive. But after his death, those parts of the body are being identified by those devatas. Therefore, they are called abhimani devatas for those respective things.

This clearly goes on to prove that not every single insentient entity is indwelled with a jeevatma, but just identified with an abhimani jeevatma (usually a devata).

Is this the right interpretation? Is this consistent with all other scriptures and works of our Acharyas? Let us see in the forthcoming posts.
