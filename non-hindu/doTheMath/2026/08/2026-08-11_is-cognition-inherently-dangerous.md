Is Cognition Inherently Dangerous?

[![](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Billiards_balls2.jpg)](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Billiards_balls2.jpg)

By Andrzej Barabasz ([Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Billiards_balls2.jpg))

Has anyone noticed the blasphemous tendency in my posts to belittle cognition? The position is that brains are imperfect and tiny subsets of the universe that are not remotely up to the task of supplanting time-tested ecologies with cognitive constructs involving agriculture, writing, money, and legal systems. Paraphrasing [Frank Egler](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/1999599-nature-is-not-more-complex-than-we-think-but-more), it’s not that nature is more complex than we think, but more complex than we are *capable* of thinking. Brains can’t be expected to fool Mother Nature, in the end.

Does that mean cognition as a whole is ecologically worthless and inherently destructive? Of course not! Lots of creatures—even crickets—rub neurons together to make thought. Moreover, non-neural forms of cognition take place among plants, fungi and microbes to help figure out how to cope with varied situations. These neuron-free beings even *learn* (see examples of [amoebas](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11677-w) and [plants](https://www.sci.news/biology/science-mimosa-plants-memory-01695.html)). Clearly cognition *itself* is not ecologically disastrous—demonstrating compatible utility over eons. Humans used top-shelf brains for millions of years without precipitating ecological disaster. Yet human brains are also key players in staging the current disastrous path.

How can we tell what sorts of human cognition are safe, then? I propose a metaphorical framing that helps identify when cognition “goes rogue” that I hope will also be useful for others.

# Let the Chips Fall

A hint comes from the animist sentiment found in *[Earth Abides](https://www.resilience.org/stories/2026-04-09/human-nature-odyssey-episode-21-earth-abides-part-1-life-after-civilization/)*, when the main character, Ish, asks his great-grandson if he’s happy. “Things are as they are, and I am part of them” was the response. It speaks to a contentment living in the hands of the gods, as [Daniel Quinn would put it](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/2025/03/ishmael-overview/).

I spent several days visiting a friend who has a pool table, and in between sessions to work on constructing a cooperative billiards game, we had plenty of discussion about the limits and appropriateness of human cognition. Swirling the two together suggested a productive framing—motivated by the sense that authentic ecological human lifeways tend to *accept the universe as it is*, working with what’s given—or again, living in the hands of the gods.

What do “the gods” have to do with pool/billiards (I tend to use the two interchangeably, even though “billiards” can refer to a carom-based game without pockets)? In most games, the opening shot is a “break” of a tight pack of balls. It’s a randomizing event to initialize the game, where “the gods” set the stage for the rest of the game: no cognition involved. Thereafter, one accepts and deals with the outcomes, however inconvenient or fortuitous.

The skill is in working *within* the constraints: taking shots at designated balls that are not blocked. This is how cognition fits into ecology: figuring out ways to “make the shot”—in the world *as it is presented*.

The example image below represents a “tough” distribution, in the context of a game like 8-ball, whereby one must only strike either solid or striped balls with the white cue ball—the goal being to sink balls of that same type into the pockets.

[![](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/pool-table-1024x588.png)](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/pool-table.png)

In this example, no direct/legal shots are feasible, leaving only shots that either involve combinations of ball strikes or the cue ball first hitting a rail. I myself would not expect success, but might attempt something like the solid red ball at left into the lower left pocket after striking the upper rail with the cue ball. I’d give myself a 5–10% chance of making such a shot, while I’m guessing that a master might have better-then-even odds of success. If I practiced that *exact* shot hundreds of times, I would expect marked improvement as I honed the requisite skill.

# The Modernity Way

For contrast, modernity has used cognition to effectively bend or break longstanding ecological rules to human advantage. How much would the pool game change if one player assumed license to move the cue ball and any other ball to any position they like before taking each shot? The other player(s) would immediately cry foul, of course.

Now consider that the game of Life is played alongside many other species, all trying to sink their own balls in their own specialized ways. It isn’t too big a stretch to imagine the [dung beetle](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xNjymt6oCcQ) trying to roll a brown ball to its preferred pocket. Crucially important is the fact that the success of one species hinges on the mutual success of many others. Not all shots belong to humans. If we tried to take all the turns/balls for ourselves, ecology would collapse and chuck us into the pile of losers alongside our victims. It’s no way to play the game: what looks like outrageous “winning” turns out to be colossal losing (e.g., [low infant mortality](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/2026/06/accepting-loss-as-fair/) is an [inseparable part](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/2024/08/mm-10-ditch-the-bad/) of a [sixth mass extinction](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/2025/09/is-the-6me-hyperbole/)). Modernity has effectively jacked up one corner of the table to funnel all the goodies toward our pocket, to the systematic detriment of all other players on the planet. It’s not very sporting of us, and is terminating the game rather quickly—making losers of us all.

In a similar vein, I guarantee that I can deliver six sixes every time when rolling six dice, provided I bestow upon myself the “right” to rotate each one to my liking before declaring my turn over. How many people do you think would want to play with me if I did that? Why would the Community of Life tolerate our shameless manipulation of the ancient game? Isn’t it more admirable to accept what the gods provide, and strive to work within the emerging relationships and constraints? It’s certainly less dangerous!

# How Hunter-Gatherers Play

In the billiards analogy, the hunter-gatherer doesn’t manipulate the rules, but is still capable of making impressive shots: leaning on cognitive processes to anticipate collisions, angles, friction, inertia, etc. Dung beetles can’t do that, so that our long-range shots—cognitively contrived—look like magic.

So, that’s where cognition can fit. Humans have little choice but to use powers of observation and cognition to devise clever ways to get what we need to survive *within an ecological context*. Different places on Earth require different “shots,” which we work out in our brains. It’s just always going to be a part of what we *do*.

Here’s another wrinkle that perhaps better captures ecological reality. After a shot attempt, the balls are returned to (at least approximately) the same positions as before the shot, so that the same set of opportunities and difficulties persist. Each location on Earth has a different number and arrangement of balls on the table, and a different cast of players—all settling into their own preferred “shots.” The image above represents a difficult setting…like a desert or polar region, perhaps: no easy shots available to the cue-stick-wielding humans. Or maybe such a challenging location would simply have fewer balls on the table. In any case, one can imagine people becoming expert enough at a particular *possible* shot in that place, making a living off one or a few available opportunities, even if requiring tremendous, specialized skill.

# Dealing with Cheaters

The problem enters when we try to cheat. Cheaters loudly protest any accusation of cheating, and we would be foolish to expect practitioners of modernity to be any different. “Agriculture isn’t cheating: it’s being clever!” “Use of fossil fuels isn’t cheating: it’s fulfilling our destiny as the most powerful species on the planet!”

If a modality is novel on [ecologically-relevant timescales](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/2024/02/sustainable-timescales/)—and especially if it effects [signs of distress](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/2026/03/ecological-deviation-application/), then maybe it earns the “cheating” label. Also, why pay attention to the cheater’s protests? Isn’t it the perspective of the other (cheated) players that matters more? Let’s ask the Community of Life if agriculture is cheating. When the answer comes back as the unambiguous unfurling of a sixth mass extinction, maybe we should attend to that as being far more authoritative than whatever the cheaters make up to say. Practicing proponents of agriculture are essentially disqualified from rendering unbiased judgment, right?

What can prevent the use of human cognition to unfair advantage? Well, one way is simple failure: go ahead and practice your “cleverness,” but prepare to die as a species—alongside countless others. The other is deliberate restraint, which is sometimes called wisdom. Respect for rules and norms that are aligned to ecological relationships keep hunter-gatherers on safe paths over vast tracts of time. This respect applies most strongly to the more-than-human world, of which humans are one small part. Modernity, by contrast, treats the non-human world as a set of commodities—a resource-rich canvas on which to paint a glorious, ascendant humanity.

# Inexplicit Ambiguity

This is no place for lawyer-types to squabble over definitions, for two reasons.

First, when it comes to the appropriate “rules” of the game, it’s no use pretending there’s a bright, sharp line here. When does the universe ever work like that? Every living being manipulates its environment in some way. It’s in the substantive manipulation of (inexplicit) *rules* that one asks for trouble. Insisting on a disproportionate number of turns, depriving others of their resources, tilting the table to our advantage, moving the balls around willy-nilly—these are the actions that break ecological health. Daniel Quinn’s [Law of Limited Competition](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/2025/04/ishmael-chapter-8/) parallels this idea.

Second, the rules can’t easily be codified into rigid words. Attempts in that direction might signal the wrong path…like attempting to love someone by following an algorithm. The universe provides no recipe for safe operation. It only smacks down those who deviate too far, using a glacial hand: slow, but powerful. One time-tested method is to watch carefully the methods of other members of the Community of Life and adopt/emulate aspects of their proven patterns. If we don’t see other living beings using money or written laws, then maybe the wise choice is to refrain from such foibles ourselves. It isn’t hard to observe other species using brains to figure out their next “shot,” but none of them write it down or pay for legal advice.

Even more direct is to learn what *actual* humans have done to live in right-relationship with the Community of Life for very long stretches. For this, immediate-return hunter-gatherers are far-and-away the best template. It’s no mistake that many similarities link these lifestyles to those of countless other species.

# Cautious Cognition

In the end, humans have always used cognition and will always rely on it as inevitably as we avail ourselves of fingers. The question is what we employ cognition *to do*. For many thousands of generations, it worked well to apply our mental powers to function within the conditions provided by the universe: accepting the “breaks” just as we accept the pool table configuration. Trouble came quickly when brains invented novel modalities that rejected the distribution set out by the gods and began concocting redistributions that seemed highly favorable in the short-term. Thinking we can do better than what ecology has tested over deep time is where the demons lie. Some cultures in human history understood this well, and carefully refrained from angering the gods with hubris.
