The Banking Firm of Mani Rām & Lakshmī Chand

By the time eighteenth century dawned on India, the polity was about to be starkly different than the preceding two centuries. The infamous Mughal Emperor Aurangzéb (r. 1658-1707 CE), the last one to truly embody the title, left in his wake a tottering empire, merely a shadow of its prime due to a war of attrition between the Mughals and the Marāṭhās lasting 27 years. Unsurprisingly, it exasperated the struggling finances of the empire even more which anyway had been in a difficult situation by the late 1680s. This resulted in emergence of some financial entities from the background to the forefront: the great banking firms.

**Henceforth, these banking firms gradually became indispensable for various polities pan-India. Whether it was the Mughals, the Marāṭhās, the Nawābs or the East India Companies, all of them extensively used services of these banking houses in various degrees.**

Only one such great banking firm is usually discussed, the House of Jagat Seṭh, the famous banking firm of the eighteenth century Bengal. Its dealings with the Mughals, the Bengal Nawābs and the East India Company became a topic of serious discussion. But there were many other lesser known and yet equally significant firms from this period onwards that played an important part in the Indian economy and polity. Though covering most of these firms and their economic activities is a rather impossible task, limited both by the authors own ability and requisite space in one blogpost, therefore the topic of this post is one such firm: the House of Mani Rām & Lakshmī Chand (spelled variously as Lakhmī Chand, Laxmī Chand or Lachhmī Chand).

This great banking firm is mentioned as having ‘few rivals in the whole of India’ by F. S. Growse, a British civil servant who served as the Collector in the district of Mathurā till 1876-1877. Mathurā being one of the most significant cities in India, primarily famous as the birthplace of Bhagavān Kṛṣṇa, it had been commercially just as important throughout Indian history (Chandra, 1977); Mathurā was the city out of which the firm was based. Growse published his Mathurá: A District Memoir in 1880 and therefore is the primary (often singular) and most important source for details regarding the firm and will be quoted frequently in this write up. The firm had its “branch establishments in Delhi, Calcutta, Bombay, and all the other great centres of commerce”… it was “known everywhere, and from the Himālayas to Capo Comorin a security for any amount endorsed by the Mathurā Seṭh^(**1**) … was “as readily convertible into cash as a Bank of England Note in London or Paris (Growse, 1882/1979; 14).”

**Moneylenders & Bankers**

Before proceeding to know about this particular firm, it will be apt to learn of the background in which these banking firms rose to prominence and the importance they gradually came to acquire in various polities of India from the late 17th and early 18th century. First and foremost, it is important to note the distinction between a typical moneylender and a banking firm in this period. Though both of these businesses had existed in the Indian economy since ancient times, the latter gathered more power in the period of our concern due to the precarious political situation since late 17th century. The moneylenders (individuals) and money lending firms habitually made loans while the banking firms not only made loans but also received deposits and/or dealt in huṇḍīs (huṇḍīs were written orders for payment transmitted throughout India) (Leonard, 1979: 155). This is the same instrument of payment aforementioned with regard to the firm of Mani Rām & Lakshmī Chand whereby it is told that ‘a security for any amount endorsed by the Mathurā Seṭh is as readily convertible into cash’ across the length and breadth of India. More importantly, while moneylenders dealt customarily with agriculturalists; bankers rather had primary dealings with the empire i.e. the Mughal government and its functionaries (Leonard, 1979: 154-155). Leonard ascribes this last distinction to ‘the extensive development of credit facilities, not those oriented towards the production of agricultural or other goods, but those oriented towards investment and profit through transactions with the Mughal government and its functionaries (ibid.).’ Gradually, these firms extended their business to a wide variety of enterprises, with several branches, often based on one household (Leonard, 1979: 154).

**Bankers as the State Treasurers**

However, as mentioned above, what caused the emergence of such firms from acting in a supportive role from the background to an ever increasing active role even in the polity was the dwindling finances of the Mughals from the long stretched Deccan War against the Marāṭhās for until then the empire was self-financing and did not depend on the private sector for the capital (Richards, 1981: 292).

**With the uncertain and delayed nature of land revenue, the Mughal government and many other regional polities increasingly took the services of these banking firms, even appointing them as state treasurers. This was in order “to provide cash and credit for the payment of salaries and other expenses on a regular basis. Thus the delays and irregularities consequent upon sole dependence on the seasonally delivered land revenues to the capital could be avoided.”**

There are indeed many examples of such appointments. The Jagat Seṭh firm was one of the earliest of this type that gained fame in its treasurer role in mid-eighteenth-century Bengal (Leonard, 1979: 155). In all likelihood, these firms had been used by the Mughal Empire even before 18th century — though not to the extent of being state treasurers — however, it is difficult to learn about exact depth of their involvement within the state apparatus or as a support to that apparatus in their earlier period for “the official and quasi-official Mughal chronicles and memoirs pre-occupied with centralized authority and power and with military success in the cause of Islam might have preferred to ignore an unpalatable dependence of the Indo-Muslim State upon non-Muslim Hindu and Jain Bankers (Richards, 1981: 291).” Leonard explains yet another aspect which became frequent in this period: the dependence of many Mughal nobles and officials in securing loans and credits from these bankers, mostly by using the jāgīrs (land-assignments) given as security. The practise was so common that these nobles even preffered the jāgīrs to cash salary despite the high interest rated for this very reason. Akbar had tried to avoid this by establishing a royal treasury advancing loans at interest rates below what the bankers asked. But by the time of Aurangzéb, the practise was so widespread that “the state officials served as intermediaries to recover debts for bankers from nobles” for which officials usually claimed one-fourth of the debt as service charge. Aurangzéb even tried to abolish the practise.

**§** **Jagat Seṭh**

As just mentioned, the House of Jagat Seṭh was certainly the first to get famous in this regard; though Jagat Seṭh was not the actual name rather an honourific. The firm was founded by Hirānand Sāho belonging to the Osawāl subcaste of Jain traders who migrated from Nagar in Mārwār, Rājasthān to Paṭnā in Bihār (Richards, 1981: 287). After some time, the firm moved from Paṭnā to Dhākā (Dāccā) and Murshīdābād with the Mughal governors of Bengāl (Leonard, 1979: 156).

Its role in the Mughal Empire became so entrenched that Aurangzéb personally honoured the firm’s head Mānek Chand for his large loans to the government (Leonard, 1979: 156). From here on, the more the Mughal finances worsened, the more these banking firms became prominent and influential within the state administration. Mānek Chand’s nephew i.e. his adopted son and successor Fatéh Chand was appointed ‘Imperial Treasurer’ and was thus awarded the title of Jagat Seṭh (distinguished merchant of the world) by the Mughal ruler Farukhsiyār (ibid.). Jagat Seṭh was even accorded mint privileges by 1717, and after 1728 the imperial tribute from Bengal was sent to Delhi by draft on this banking house (ibid.). This was in stark contrast to the financial administration of the Mughals in their heydays where the empire centrally controlled such significant fiscal administrative decisions through its various officials and functionaries.**²** The influence of Jagat Seṭh in Bengal was even used in the political games between the Mughal centre and the Bengal province. When the faction backed by Murshid Qulī Khān, the Bengal governor did not win in yet another sucecssion struggle for the Mughal throne, he asked Jagat Seṭh to spread the rumour of the victory of the side he had backed in order to buy time so that he could secure his position meanwhile.

> and Āgrā, represented East India Company in Delhi Court between

**§ The Banking Firms**

With each passing year, these firms were in controlling position regarding accessing the actual collection of land revenue through provisions of credit or cash (Leonard, 1979: 158) to various rulers in India. “The urban bankers made loans to rulers for military and other purposes and were in return authorized to collect the revenues” (Leonard, 1979: 156). There are many examples of such firms: “Kallidaikurichy Brāhmin firms were bankers to the Rājās of Cochin and Travancore. Branches of a single Mārwārī firm served as bankers to the Nawāb of Fatéhpur, the Piṇḍārī Nawābs, and Raṇjīt Singh. Particular firms were named as bankers to the Nawāb of Arcot and to the Nizām of Hyderābād (Leonard, 1979: 156). The Paṭawardhan firm of Puṇé (Poonā) was banker to the Péshwās and served the Marāṭhā state through their branches in Nāsik, Mumbai (Bombay), Aurangābād, Bījāpur; the founders of this firm began as money lenders in Nāsik in the first decade of the eighteenth century before moving to Puṇé (Poonā) (Richards, 1981: 286).

There is an argument which suggests that as the later Mughals, both in policy and in practise, did not place enough importance in retaining the confidence of the great banking firms, this error cost them the empire (Leonard, 1979: 154) but it is rather how firms increasingly shifted away from the Mughals to other avenues, be it the emerging regional polities or the foreign merchant companies, providing essential fiscal services to the new provincial rulers of the early decades of the eighteenth century that indirectly contributed to the rise of these relatively decentralized regional polities (Richards, 1981: 286) and the downfall of the empire (ibid. p. 289). The weakening of the Mughal Empire affected the eventual redirection by these firms of their investments from the central Mughal administration to regional and local political powers (Leonard, 1979: 159) as the empire was unable to provide protection to its commercially important centres.

Arguably, the most prominent event regarding this was the raids of Śivājī in Sūrat, the wealthiest port of India during the Mughal period (ibid.). Since then, the bankers increasingly started redirecting their financial investments from the central Mughal administration to regional and local political powers. Earlier, the heads of these firms, especially in Sūrat had access to the erstwhile Mughal Emperors who responded favourably to their requests (Leonard, 1979: 160). But failure of Aurangzéb in providing adequate protection from raids resulted in migration of many merchants and bankers out of Sūrat to other avenues, one of which was Poonā, capital of the Péshwās (ibid.). In fact, in 1702 when Aurangzéb attempted to secure interest-free loans to pay troop arrears in the Deccan, he was turned down by banking firms as the demand must have been perceived as contrary to the banker’s interest at the time (ibid.). And this was the contextual politico-economic environment in which was founded the firm of Mani Rām & Lakshmī Chand.

**The Firm of Mani Rām & Lakshmī Chand**

**§ Gokul Dās ‘Pārikh Jī’**

The firm was actually founded by a Vaiṣṇava Gujrātī Brāhmin of Vallabhācārya persuasion (Growse, 1882/1979; 14) named Gokul Dās. He held the significant post of being Treasurer to the Scindia’s state of Gwālior and due to this position, he was usually called Pārikh Jī. It is said that in a dharmaśālā (alms-house), a man named Mani Rām, a Khanḍelwāl Jain from Jaipur State once saved a sick man who was none other than Parīkh Jī. He therefore took Mani Rām under his wing, setting him up in Gwālior, in a clothing business. Mani Rām also helped him in the official matters as a office subordinate. Pārikh Jī set up a banking firm in Mathurā and Mani Rām helped him in its administration. He was unfortunately childless and at bad terms with his only brother; but since he had great affection for Mani Rām, he on his death bed in 1826, bequeathed the whole of his immense wealth to Mani Rām (ibid.). However, as per the laws of the time, due to the difference in the religion between him and Mani Rām, he could not formally adopt him as his son and so the will was ‘fiercely disputed’ by his brother —the litigation running for several years — but eventually the highest Court of appeal confirmed the property in Mani Rām’s possession (ibid.). Mani Rām held the reins of the firm till 1836 when after his death, the eldest of his three sons, Lakshmī Chand inherited the firm, the other two sons were named Gobind Dās and Rādhā Krishan.

Seṭh Lakshmī Chand — many a times simply called the Mathurā Seṭh — handled the business that as previously stated, had its “branch establishments in Delhi, Calcutta, Bombay, and all the other great centres of commerce. However, the firm even though headed by Lakshmī Chand, was managed along with his two brothers and all three of them were collectively called Mathura Seṭhs.

**As mentioned above, it was ‘known everywhere’… ‘from the Himālayas to Cape Comorin a security for any amount endorsed by the Mathurā Seṭh’ was as readily convertible into cash as a Bank of England Note in London or Paris (Growse, 1882/1979** **: 14).”**

The firm of Lakshmī Chand thus had extensive influence across various facets of nineteenth century northern India, be it politico-economical, social and even religious. The goal of this post is to analyse these facets separately; the political question being the most curious and complex, will be dealt with firstly.

**Seṭh Lakshmī Chand** **&** **1857**

The question involving the political role and loyalties of Lakshmī Chand is primarily related to the events of 1857. There are three main sides which we find the Mathurā Seṭh dealing with around this time: the British, the Mughals and the Marāṭhā Scindias, particularly the erstwhile Scindia regent Baijā Bāī or Baizā Bāī, wife of Scindia ruler of Gwālior, Daulat Rāo Scindia.

**§ The Conspicuous Loyalty**

By all accounts, the firm’s relations with the East India Company were indeed very strong; Growse in his memoirs regarding 1857 calls Lakshmī Chand’s loyalty to British as the most conspicuous (Growse, 1882/1979: 15). There were even complaints that though the Seṭhs were ‘loyal to the core… many of the other large merchants were only “watchers of the atmosphere,” (Malleson Ed. 1899: 97). It is told that even in the past, there had been many occasions when they (the Mathurā Seṭhs) advanced large sums of money to the British ‘for Government purposes…when other wealthy firms had positively refused to give any assistance (Growse, 1882/1979: 15).’

It is also told that they warned the then Collector of Mathurā, a Mr. Thornhill, of the impending outbreak (of 1857) a day before it actually took place (ibid.) and post-the outbreak as well, ‘they sent such immediate information to the authorities at Āgrā as enabled them to disarm and thus anticipate the mutiny of the other companies of the same Native Regiments, the 44th and the 67th, which wore quartered there (ibid.).’ The Collector and the other Europeans were even provided shelter in the Seṭh’s own house in the city^(**3**) (Growse, 1882/1979: 47) ‘till the 5th of July, when, on the approach of the Nīmach force, they took boat and dropped down the river to Āgrā. After their departure, the Seṭhs even took charge of the Government treasure and maintained public order (Growse, 1882/1979: 15). ’

Amidst all this, yet another eventful situation arose when the guards posted over the Seṭhs’ house attempted to murder the Englishmen (Malleson Ed. 1899: 98) & Mr. Thornhill only ‘by apparent accident escaped being murdered just before he left Mathurā (Mallesons 1899: 102). “When we entered the Seṭhs’ house in the afternoon,” Thornhill writes, “their Muhammadan guards proposed to murder us all as we sat on the terrace. The manager (Seṭh Mangī Lal) heard of the plot; he ran and informed his masters. They were terrified, half stupefied. He implored them to exert themselves, warning them that if we fell the English Government would take a terrible vengeance, and that utter destruction would fall on them and their house. Urged thus by their manager, the Seṭhs went down to their guards, and by threats, persuasions, and some display of force, they succeeded at length in inducing the men to abandon their design. The Muhammadans did not renounce their design, but proposed to kill the two Englishmen as they left the house. But in this they were equally baffled by the generous Seṭhs (Malleson Ed. 1899: 102).” It would not be wrong to assume that the guards were most likely loyal to the Mughals and the rebel forces in Delhi and were thus, either out of their own volition or as part of the larger plan wanted to kill the Englishmen. 

As mentioned before, after the arrival of the Nīmach force in Mathurā, Mr. Thornhill and company decided to go to Āgrā and the Seṭh provided requisite escort for the journey, though most of them except the Jamādār took fright and decamped (Growse, 1882/1979: 48), an act for which the Jāmadār received a reward of a small piece of land on the “Brindāban road, just outside Mathurā, called after the name of a Bairāgi who had once lived there, Dudhadhāri (ibid.).” “Though the rebels stayed two days in Mathurā before they passed on to Delhi, the city was not given up to general plunder, partly in consequence of the prudent management of Seṭh Mangī Lāl (the manager), who levied a contribution, according to their means, on all the principal inhabitants (ibid.).”

**Even though, by this time Seṭh Lakhmī Chand was at Ḍīg**…**“the greater part of his estblishment remained behind and rendered Government the most valuable assistance by the despatch of intelligence (ibid.).”**

Thus, providing intelligence seems to have been a constant contribution by the Seṭhs because it is told that “so long as the disturbances lasted, they kept up at great expense, for which they never made any claim to reimbursement, a very large establishment for the purpose of procuring information and maintaining communication between Delhi and Āgrā (Growse, 1882/1979: 15).” In acknowledgment of these services, the title of Rāo Bahādur was conferred upon Seṭh Lakhmī Chand, with a khilat of Rs. 3,000. A grant was also made him of certain confiscated estates, yielding an annual revenue of Rs. 16,125, rent free for his own life and at half rates for another life (Growse, 1882/1979: 15).

**§ The Covert Plan**

This account leaves no doubt on whom the Mathurā Seṭh had placed his bet in political power plays of mid-nineteenth century India, though it was hardly surprising given the tide of the East India Company had been perennially on the rise ever since they snatched the hegemony of India from the Marāṭhās post-the Third Anglo-Marāṭhā War (1817-1819). The Mughals now remained confined to Delhi, the regional Moslem polities like Bengal, and Hyderābād having far more power than the de jure Mughal ‘Emperor’. In the aftermath of 1818, despite the loss of Péshwā overlordship, the Marāṭhā regional powers remained significant. One of these powers was the Scindia state of Gwālior.

**§ Baijā Bāī**

Interestingly, under the surface of this rather simple story of a rich banker’s support to the foreign powers, there are indeed a lot more complex layers that remain understudied. It seems Lakshmī Chand had placed his bet on yet another side i.e. the aforementioned Scindias or to put it more precisely, the erstwhile regent of the state Baijā Bāī and through her, on the faction of Nānā Sāheb and Tātyā Ṭopé. Baijā Bāī was the wife of Daulat Rāo Shindé (Scindia is merely an anglicized version of the original Marāṭhā surname), ruler of Gwālior in 1798. She was the daughter of Sarjé Rāo Ghaṭgé who had been a part of Daulat Rāo’s court and ‘fiercely opposed to the English rule’ (Topé, 2010).^(**4**) Baijā Bāī — taught by her father to be a skilful horserider and to use both a spear and a sword — also had a decisive advisory role to Daulat Rāo. She seemed to have inherited her views on the British from her father “and even accompanied him in some of his campaigns, including the Anglo-Marāṭhā wars, at the battle of Assaye in 1803 (ibid.)…. She was also a steadfast supporter of the Péshwai. “The English Resident in the court of the Shindées later observed that Baijā Bāī did all in her power to uphold Bājī Rāo II” after the second Anglo-Marāṭhā War (1803-1805).

This small introduction of Baijā Bāī shows that she had political acumen and interest which became more pronounced when Daulat Rāo passed away and she became regent to the adopted son and successor of Daulat Rāo, Jānkojī Rāo II in June 1827. Regarding her relations with the English, the tensions continued for she seemed resolute to not allow them to dominate the state affairs. Soon enough, the English East India Company “asked for a ‘loan’ of Rs 1 Crore (10 million) from Baijā Bāī. The English often depended on such local credit for its military and commercial activities in India. Implicit in this borrowing was the precondition to recognise her as a Regent of Gwālior” (Ṭopé, 2010). Baijā Bāī stalled it for some time, fully aware of the extortion and the likelihood that the English had no intention to ever pay the amount back. She brought the amount to Rs 80 lakhs (8 million) but the English kept evading the repayment, even asking her to prove that the funds were her personal wealth and not from the Gwālior Treasury (ibid.).

**Eventually, “Baijā Bāī made sure that the amount was paid into her banking firm in Vārāṇasī, not into the treasury at Gwālior, which the English were slowly attempting to control.”**

Here in lies one of the most interesting details i.e. her connection to the banking firms, not just in Vārāṇasī but in Mālwā as well; she owned a large network of banking firms which operated under various names, from Vārāṇasī to Ujjain. In Ujjain, she was reportedly at the head of the banking firms: Nāthjī Kishan Dās and Nāthjī Bhagwān Dās (Topé, 2010).

There are a few very pertinent points in the aforementioned events. The English clearly did not just depend on financial backing of the banking firms alone. They also extorted or coaxed finance from such states that were not in a position to mount a strong resistance to their demands like in the case of the Hyderābād Contingent (Majūmdār Ed., 1963/2002: 88-89). It is however, noteworthy that since Baijā Bāī was at the head of these banking firms, in theory this must have been helpful in dealing with the British. But in reality, this did not prove much helpful for the court politics divided the Scindias in two factions: Baijā Bāī on one side, Jānkojī II with the English on the other. Possibly due to the English maneuverings, a pliable Jānkojī urged the army to rise against Baijā Bāī who, fearing a civil war, left the palace in July 1833.

**§ The Mathurā Connection**

Important to note that after leaving the Gwālior palace, the former regent ‘changed residences frequently in the years to come. She proceeded to Dholpur, then to Āgrā, Mathurā and Fatéhgarh.’ The Mathurā connection is the key as Baijā Bāī stayed for quite sometime in the city which was as is known, the centre of the firm of Lakshmī Chand. The fascinating detail which readers must have understood by now, is that the connection was visibly not limited to this. As previously mentioned, the founder of the firm Pārikh Jī (Gokul Dās) was a treasurer of the Gwālior State. In fact, Daulat R**ā**o Scindia provided heavy funds for the construction of Dvārakādhīsh Temple in Mathurā in 1814 with the treasurer Pārikh Jī along with Mani Rām overseeing the work. This was the business aforementioned due to which both of them stayed in Mathurā and Pārikh Jī established his banking firm. Thus, Scindias and the Banking firm of Mathurā Seṭhs had a deep personal connection, with them being banker to Baijā Bāī even after Daulat Rāo passed away. Infact, the connection becomes deeper when we learn that Mathurā was one of the favourite residences of Mahādjī Scindia (Growse, 1882/1979: 43).

**“In 1840, she (Baijā Bāī**) **moved to Nāsik and stayed there for about eight years. Subsequently she did return to Gw**ā**lior at a time that could not be accidental.”**

What happened during the time she was in Nāsik is another fascinating detail. The erstwhile regent used this time to establish fresh communications with ‘like-minded people’ using an emissary named Krishna Shāstrī. The earliest one was with the Holkars but while Holkar attempted to guage the resolute, capability and strength of those involved, time was not in favour. However, the tide turned once Bājī Rāo II passed away in 1851 and Nānā Sāheb was in a position to act. Here in, yet again, enters Seṭh Lakshmī Chand.

**“Baijā Bāī got the reply she was expecting from Biṭhūr, thirteen years after her first communiqué to Bājī Rāo II in 1838. Sometime in 1851, Nānā Sāheb sent his trusted priest to Baijā Bāī, who at that time was in Ujjain. Baijā Bāī was ready, and she responded with her ideas and a plan was in place. Nānā Sāheb began communicating with various Indian leaders and financiers. Amongst the first to respond was Baijā Bāī’s personal banker from Mathurā, Lakshmīchand.”**

**§ The Yajña**

It seems likely that Baijā Bāī had already communicated her ideas to the Mathurā Seṭh while she was staying in Mathurā after leaving her regency of Gw**ā**lior but the two may have been waiting for a favourable time and response. However, it does not seem that she stayed still until that time because the English suspected her involvement in the Poonā and Asīrgarh rebellions in early 1840s. After a long time, her efforts were now bearing fruit and Lakshmī Chand became key to the finances of the latest attempt in ousting the English, its execution being controlled by Nānā Sāheb and Tātyā Ṭopé. Baijā Bāī finally returned to Gw**ā**liar in 1856. There is a very fascinating eye-witness account titled ‘Māzā Pravāsa (My Journey)’ for the events of 1857 by a Brāhmin named Viṣṇubhaṭ Goḍsé who was on a visit from his hometown Varsāī in Mahārāshṭra to Mathurā. Baijā Bāī had invited him and many other priests from all over India for a grand sacrifice called the Sarvatomukha Yajña to be organized in Mathurā. She had arranged the yājña and the financier for this grand sacrifice was none other than Lakshmī Chand (Ṭopé, 2010). The grandeur of this yajña can be understood from the amount that was spent on it, a figure of more than seven lakh rupees. One might wonder what could be the purpose of this yajña and how it may have helped the cause. Parāg Ṭopé explains it as follows:

**‘…In this flurry of movements of priests, priestly messengers, carried secret messages, could travel undetected (Ṭopé, 2010).’**

**§ An Emissary: Sītārām Bāwā / Bābā**

The testimony of Sītārām Bāwā / Bābā — a native priest and emissary — to the Judicial Commissioner of Mysore, H. B. Devereux, in January 1858 provides corroboration for these plans. He was the one who mentioned that ‘a prominent financier of the War in the early phases was Seṭh Lakshmīchand…’ The connection between Lakshmī Chand and the Scindias had already been mentioned before in this post. Sītārām Bāwā was interrogated for about a week. “After the testimony, the English concluded that Sītārām Bābā, was ‘if not an actor’, he was ‘at any rate to have been in the confidence of the chief conspirators, and early initiated into their designs (Ṭopé, 2010)’. In addition, they concluded that Sītārām Bābā appears ‘remarkably clear and consistent in all his statements’, and he was ‘inclined to attach a considerable credit to them’ (ibid.).

Sītārām Bābā initially refused to divulge any information; however, he later seems to have been ‘persuaded’ after being taken ‘back to the confinement.’ As per his testimony, primarily six ruling princes were part of Nānā’s ‘conspiracy’: two Marāṭhā Mahārājās, Holkar and Scindia, two Rājpūt rulers, Jaipur and Jodhpur, the Ḍogrā Rājput Mahārājā of Jammu & Kashmīr and lastly, the Nizām of Hyderābād. However, it is conspicuous that none of these rulers openly rebelled, probably because too much was at stake. It is also interesting that as Sītārām Bābā mentioned, the actual spark to these ‘conspiracies’ was provided by the annexation of Oudh (Awadh) in 1856. It is perfectly reasonable to assume that Nānā got the initial support from such rulers, however limited, simply because they thought that their state could be the next one after Awadh. But, if the British got the whiff of their support, in whatever capacity, the result would be the same that they had been apprehensive about in the first place i.e. annexation of their territories. This theory about princely support gets more traction when we notice that many of these state rulers were unable to prevent their own troops from rebelling; the British indeed suspected and monitored them throughout this period for this very reason.

Without digressing, it is important to understand from this very short explanation is that Lakshmī Chand was not the only entity part of the plan to oust the British covertly, while being a loyal supporter of the EIC overtly. These princes had adopted a similar tactic. It is also likely that these parties, the princes, the financiers, the zamindārs etc. would have come out in the forefront had the rebels gotten the upperhand but as war went on, it had started to become clearer that however fierce and hopeful the initial period was, the tables had turned against their plans. Thus this helps us to understand the behavior of the Mathurā Seṭh where he, being one of richest banker in India at the time had just as much to lose as these rulers did, had the British confirmed their suspicions, so he showed utmost loyalty to the British on one hand, while providing support to Baijā Bāī and Nānā Sāheb’s plan (at least in the early period) on the other. Important to note that Lakshmī Chand was not the only financier, though it seems like he was the most prominent one.

**It is also learnt from Sītārām that “Golāb Singh, of Jummoo, was the first to send an answer. He said that he was ready with men, money and arms, and he sent money to Nānā Sahib, through one of the Lucknow Soukārs” (Majūmdār** **Ed., 1963/2002: 606)**

**§ The Mughals & the Bankers**

Now, one might naturally wonder where exactly did the Mughals fit into this narrative? Interestingly, the last Mughal titular ‘Emperor’ Bahādur Shāh II ‘Zafar’ (r. 1837-1857), ‘is also said to have written two letters to Baizā Baī but she sent no reply…and (he) got no favourable response from Nānā with whom he communicated through a confidential agent about two months after the outbreak of the Mutiny (Majūmdār Ed., 1963/2002: 513). This lack of support between Nānā and Bahādur Shāh is also seen from Lakshmī Chand. While the Mathurā Seṭh was overtly supporting the British while reportedly financing the early efforts of Nānā Saheb secretly, he was also extorted out of his money and forced to pay an amount to the rebels camping in Delhi at the time.

**§ The Extortion**

The Delhi rebels were facing severe shortage of foodstocks and the water situation was ‘equally dire’ (Dalrymple, 2006). The probem arose mainly due to their failure in establishing “a well-governed ‘liberated area’ or Mughal realm from which they could draw tax revenue, manpower and, most of all, food supplies” and this ultimately proved “the Delhi rebels’ single most disastrous failure” …having “not attempted to collect one single rupee of tax revenue (ibid.)… so much so that ‘the intense hunger on the streets of the city had become a prominent theme of the spies’ reports.” The rebels complained that ‘there are no moneylenders who will give them loans….By 14 August, the newly arrived Nīmach brigade were openly threatening to desert if they were not fed (ibid.)’ The Court of Administration, led by Mirzā Mughal, had indeed tried borrowing money from the city’s moneylenders but the amount (6,000 rupees) could only provide supplies for a few days; the efforts in ‘extracting the money from the bankers and baniyās’ in Delhi went on for long without any results as the money lenders kept on evading with various excuses. Ultimately, Mirzā Mughal decided to up the ante and suggested “a more vigorous approach:

**‘Proclaim an order’, suggested the Prince, ‘…that if these moneylenders remain hidden you will blow them from a cannon (ibid.)’**

As far as the Mughals were concerned this was certainly not the first time that the moneylenders were forced to ‘lend loans’, it actually had a rather long tradition behind it; even back in 1600 moneylenders suffered from the same perils. In his autobiography, Ardha Kathānak (The Half-Story) (1641), a jewel trader from Āgrā named Banārasīdās tells that “his father, a prosperous trader of jewels and pearls in Jaunpur had his entire fortune extorted by beatings and confinement by a man titled Qilīch Khān, the Mughal governor during Akbar in 1600 and the family, now destitute had to move to Allāhabād till Qilīch Khān was recalled from Jaunpur (Richards, 1981: 291-292).” The memoir conveys “a profound suspicion and wariness of capricious imperial power and officials” (Richards, 1981:292).

Then there are examples from various succession wars for the Mughal throne during which warring factions financed their claims through extorting the moneylenders. Murād Baksh had forced a large loan from merchants of Sūrat in 1656 to fight for his succession against Aurangzéb (Richards, 1981: 291). More than half a century later, Farrukhsiyār did the same by forcing traders (including English and Dutch merchants) in Paṭnā, Bihār to pay an amount totaling to two to three lakhs of rupees (200-300,000 rupees). Bihār was the chosen target because his main supporter to the claim, Husain Ali Khān (one of the king-making brother duo Sayyid brothers) was a governor of Azīmābad, Bihār. However, the plan regarding the foreign merchants was thwarted by the governor himself who, probably wanting to keep an upper hand, had his own dealings with the Europeans (Leonard, 1979: 160).

**In fact, Husain Ali had “himself financed his rebel army, indeed the coup, by seizing the annual treasure shipment from Bengal to Delhi valuing at over ten million rupees. He apparently seized two such shipments to finance the coup (Richards, 1981: 288).”**

These facts are in context of the politico-economic environment in which. from 1650 to 1750, many indigenous bankers were changing their economic and political orientation from Mughal urban centers to those of other powers, and the banker’s extension of trade and credit transactions to newcomers, the Dutch and the English, in contrast to their earlier policies (Leonard, 1979: 160). Leonard especially mentions that once the new commercial relationships have been noted, there was usually no attempt to reconstruct the commerical networks of the banking firms prior to their connection with the European traders (Leonard, 1979: 160). However, despite the early enthusiasm concurrent on the new prospects of business with these foreigners in the beginning, it only went on till about 1772 when the East India Company took various policy decisions that emphatically ended the role of indigenous bankers in various capacity of intermediaries, including Jagat Seṭh’s (Leonard, 1979: 163).

Added to these is the example from Hyderābād where due to various reasons including the weak financial management by the regional states and them trying to walk a tight rope of maintaining independence and relations with the East India Company at the same time, all the causes combined with the policy decisions of the British and resulted in the bankruptcy of most of its famous banking firms like the Panch Bhāi by the year 1851 (Leonard, 1981: 179-180).

**§ Taking the Gumāshtā as Hostage**

And so in this fashion, Lakshmī Chand was also sent a message by the Delhi rebels demanding a hefty payment. Since, those demanding the payment were not really in a strong position themselves, at first Lakshmī Chand was enticed with “the position of Fotadār (Treasurer) in return for a loan of 5 lakh rupees” though Lakshmī Chand replied that he was still unable to help (Dalrymple, 2006). 

Ahsānullāh, the physician and a close confidant of Bahādur Shāh notes in his memoir: “The Khazānchī represented that very little money was left in the Treasury, not even 10,000 rupees and that the expenses of the powder magazine etc., etc., were great. Some arrangement must be made. A parwānāh (official letter) was addressed to the Mirzā Moghul in the evening. He stated that, ‘I informed all the officers of the low state of the Treasury, that a parwānāh should be written to the officers of Neemuch army either to bring 5 lakhs from Muttra from Luchmi Chand or his gumāshtā who should accept the post of Fotedār…” (Haq Ed., 1958: 19). But Lakshmī Chand excused himself with a reply in the negative. Ahsānullāh notes: “A petition came in from Neemuch army that Luchmi Chand Seṭh had gone on a pilgrimage, that his gumāshtā (agent) had not so much money” (ibid. 20). This act of taking the gumāshtā hostage created a controversy because Lakshmī Chand was not an ordinary moneylender.

**“The members of the Court came and said, ‘the gumāshtā of Seṭh Luchmi Chand has been taken in the camp of the Bareilly Troops. Release him quickly else there will be a row.’**

Apparently, the agent was ‘ill-treated’ so the Maulvī was ordered to go at once and release him as he had already given his contributions of money (Haq Ed., 1958: 26-27).” Thus, it seems like some particular amount of money had been taken by taking the said agent of the Mathurā Seṭh a hostage.

**§ Bankers & Mercenary Troops**

This act of the Delhi rebels of taking the agent of Lakshmī Chand hostage was merely one example of such incidence. It is known that for example, in Hyderābād, mercenary troops were used to provide personal security; many Arab and Paṭhān military commanders and their troops were employed (levied) by city elites (Leonard, 1981: 190-191). In previous sections it was mentioned how Lakshmī Chand sent armed men in to provide security for the British officer, the Collector of Mathurā, Mr. Thornhill. There were even instances of direct violent disputes between the state and the bankers (Leonard, 1981: 184-190). Pestonjī Viccajī’s (a prominent 19th-century Pārsī banker) land assignments were reclaimed and he levied Rohillā troops to fight the state troops. He even asked the British East India Company’s help in securing repayment; his request was denied and the firm went bankrupt in 1848 (Leonard, 1981: 187-188). Even here there had been instances of use of violence (kidnappings) by the troops to demand their payments in arrears as ransom (Leonard, 1981: 190-191).

**The Heirs of the Firm**

Soon enough, when it became clearer that the British had won by the end of 1857, Lakshmī Chand continued his business as usual. Growse states in his memoirs: “During the more than 20 years of peace which have now elapsed since those eventful days (of 1857), the Seṭhs, whenever occasion required, have shown themselves equally liberal and public spirited (Growse, 1882/1979: 15).” Seṭh Lakshmī Chand’s only son named Raghunātha Dās is told as not to have ‘inherited his father’s talent for business’ and therefore, the management which was anyway under his two uncles previously mentioned — brothers of Lakshmī Chand — Seṭh Rādhā Krishan Dās and Seṭh Gobind Dās (Growse, 1882/1979: 14). However, Rādhā Krishan had died in 1859 followed by the death of Lakshmī Chand in 1866. And thus, the sole surviving brother Gobind Dās, became the recognized head of the family (ibid.) and was awarded with ‘A Companion of the Order of the Star of India’^(**5**) (Growse, 1882/1979: 14) on the 1st of January, 1877, when the British Queen Victoria assumed the Imperial title Kaisar-e-Hind. The award was ‘in acknowledgment of his many distinguished public services.’ Gobind Dās died merely a year after this, ‘leaving as his joint heirs his two nephews, Raghunāth Dās, the son of Lakshmī Chand, and Lachhman Dās (the one in the portrait posted at the start of the blogpost), the son of Rādhā Krishan (Growse, 1882/1979: 15).’

**Religious Establishments**

However, there is and was a lot more to workings to such elite businesses besides the politics of the time, especially their contributions to public welfare. Being one of the richest business entities in India, the firm since the early period of its existence had contributed to the religious causes in the city of Mathurā. For an ancient city that had been largely made barren of its religious heritage due to the prolonged period of Islamic invasions and Moslem rulers’ sword, it meant much more than what may seem on the surface.

**§ Dvārakādhīsha Temple**

As mentioned above, the temple was built in 1815 by Gokul Dās Pārikh Jī, the treasurer of the Gwalior state at the cost of Rs. 20,000 with donations from Daulat Rāo Scindia (Growse, 1882/1979: 176). The temple, since its establishment has been under the Puṣṭimārga tradition of the Vaiṣṇava Vallabhācārya sect, the sect to which Pārikh Jī himself belonged. The temple due to this reason was for a long time simply known as the Seṭh’s temple. At first, it was administered by Gosāin, who was ‘the hereditary lord of the much older and yet wealthier shrine of the same name in Udaypur (Growse, 1882/1979: 156).’ However after some time, “the expenses for the temple in Mathurā were…defrayed by annual grants from the Seṭh’s estate…the firm made an absolute transfer to the Gosāin of the temple with a landed property yielding an income of Rs. 25,000.”

**§ Jain Jambusvāmī** **Temple**

Mani Rām, the heir to Pārikh Jī, as aforementioned, was a Jain. He built a Jain temple that remains to this day known as the Jain Chaurāsī. Mani Rām enshrined in it ‘a figure of Chandra Prabhu, the second of Tirthankaras.’ However, years later, Seṭh Raghunāth Dās, son of Seṭh Lakshmī Das ‘brought from a ruined temple at Gwālior, a large marble statue of Ajitnāth and enshrined it’(Growse, 1882/1979: 13). The descendants of the family are still to this day members of the trust that handles its management.

**§ Ranganāth Jī Temple**

The two brothers of Seṭh Lakshmī Chand: Seṭh Rādhā Krishna and Seṭh Gobind Dās became converts to Vaiṣṇavism under the influence of the learned scholar, Swāmī Rangācarya. The two brothers founded ‘at enormous cost the great temple of Rang Jī or Sri Ranga Nāth at Brindāban…the only large establishment in all Upper India that is owned by the followers of Rāmānuja (Growse, 1882/1979: 14).’ Svāmī Rangācārya (Śrī Rangadeshika Svāmī Jī) was placed at the head of the great temple of Rang Jī. The temple is built in Dravidian style of Hindu temple architecture. The work was commenced in 1845 and completed in 1851, at an enormous cost of 45 lakhs (Growse, 1882/1979: 260).

> **The temple included (still does to this day) also an alms-house for
> the relief of the indigent and a college where students are trained in
> Sanskrit literature and philosophy.**
>
> — **Growse, 1882/1979. p. 16**

**Public Welfare**

The family supported many projects of public welfare as well. They supported William Muir’s Central College at Allāhabād (Prayāga) with a subscription of Rs. 2,500 (Growse, 1882/1979: 16). When the terrible famine of 1874 was raging, ‘before the Government had put forth any appeal to the public, they spontaneously called a relief meeting and headed the list with a donation of Rs. 7,100.

They also contributed in the construction of the Mathurā and Hāthras Light Railway when it was made conditional on its receiving a certain amount of local support…at once they “took shares to the extent of a lakh and-a-half of rupees, even though the money had to be withdrawn from other investments, where it was yielding a much higher rate of interest (Growse, 1882/1979: 16)” and here it was not more than 12 % which at the time, as Growse pointed out was “the lowest return which Indian capitalists ordinarily receive for their money.” Though this was explained by Growse as due to ‘highly commendable public spirit and a desire to support the local European authorities, who had shown themselves personally interested in the matter (Growse, 1882/1979: 159)’, it nonetheless is a good example of contribution of indigenous elites in construction of basic infrastructure at that time.

There are also examples of many beautifying cenotaphs (chhatrīs), even major bridges constructed due to their funds (Growse, 1882/1979: 16, 133). The city of Mathurā at that time had ‘become one of the handsomest in all Upper India,’ largely due to various buildings constructed by the Seṭhs and there were many other works which it is told that ‘do not so conspicuously bear their names, they have been among the largest contributors, and it would be scarcely possible to find a single deserving institution in the neighbourhood, to which they have not given a helping hand.’ To this was also added the Catholic Church built from their donation of Rs, 1,100 (Growse, 1882/1979: 16).

**The Later Heirs**

The working of the firm was mostly managed by Seṭh Mangi Lāl (mentioned above), assisted by his two sons, Nārāyaṇa Dās and Śrīnivāsa Dās. The latter, who was in charge of its Delhi branch, was an author as well and even published ‘a Hindī drama entitled Raṇdhīr and Prem-Mohinī.’ Nārāyaṇa Dās managed the Rangjī Temple estate, and stayed an active member of the Municipal Committee of Vṛndāvan and Mathurā. Growse also states that, Nārāyaṇa Dās superintended the relief operations during the late severe famine of 1874 and for this he was awarded a khilat of honour from the Lieutenant Governor in a public Darbār held at Āgrā in the year 1880 (Growse, 1882/1979: 15). Seṭh Lachhman Dās (son of Seṭh Rādhā Krishan) had two heirs: Dāmodar Dās and Dwārkā Dās; the following generation were Mathurā Dās (son of Dāmodar Dās) and Gopāl Dās (son of Dwārkā Dās). However, there were no heirs from the last two male members, and so the Seṭh’s Estate was administered by the Court of the Wards of the British Government in India. The Estate then passed to Seṭh Bhagwān Dās, the adopted son of Gopāl Dās, and who took its control from the Court of Wards in 1944. Seṭh Bhagwān Dās was survived by Seṭh Vijay Kumār Jain, the only son from his first marriage, who is based between Kānpur and the ancestral home in Mathurā.

**Conclusion**

One might wonder why the British after the end of the war of 1857 continued their dealings with the entities like the princes, the zamindārs, the financiers like Lakshmī Chand etc., despite the suspicions that Sītārām Bābā’s testimony casted on them? It is quite reasonable to assume that the British Crown that had in 1858 officialy proclaimed India as its colony, was not in the mood exasperate even further the already tense situation, especially given the fact that the EIC’s own high handedness through the policies like the Doctrine of Lapse had resulted in antagonizing the princes and alienating the factions that were once counted as reliable allies. Thus, it is quite natural why in the aftermath of these revelations, the relations were handled cautiously by the British Crown. The case of Lakshmī Chand yet again proves that there were many in India at the time whose role cannot be categorically confined in clear distinctions of being either the supporter or the rebel to the British Rule. In fact, there were many who embodied the role of being either of these at a time, or even both at the same time, acting differently in covert and overt. Therefore, it is important to study such examples in the context of the political environment of the period where both motives and actions many a times navigated by crossing the threshold between practical realities on one side and ideals on the other.
