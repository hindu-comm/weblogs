When Did the Mahabharata War Happen? Debunking Mr Nilesh Oak

In the late Vedic texts, we find mention of important historic figures that become major characters in the epic. However, the main protagonists and antagonists, namely the five Pandava brothers and their 100 Kaurava cousins aren’t mentioned anywhere in the Vedic texts. The Vedic texts mention their ancestors (such as Pratisutvan, Praitpa, Bahlika, Shantanu, Devapi, Vichitravirya, Dhritarashtra, Baka Dalbhya, Somaka, Nagnajit of Gandhara and Bhima of Vidarbha). There is also a mention of some of the important contemporaries, such as Shikhandi (as son of Yajnasena) and Krishna (as son of Devaki). And further more, their descendants (Parikshit and Janamejaya) figure prominently in late Vedic texts. Yet, the Vedic texts evade any reference to the five Pandava brothers and the 100 Kaurava brothers, nor is there any mention of a great war in Kurukshetra.

The Ashvalayana Grihya Sutra, which one may count among late Vedic literature, mentions Mahabharata as a text associated with Vaishampayana. The Prashna Upanishad may give a clude about the dating of the Ashvalayana Grihya Sutra. The Prashna Upanishad places the Kosala king Hiraṇyanābha as a contemporary of Âśvalāyana Kausalya, who may have been the author of the Âśvalāyana sutras. These sutras may therefore be dated ~880 BCE. This is not too long after the reign of Janamejaya (~920-900 BCE), during which Vaishyampayana would have composed an early version of the Mahabharata. The Âśvalāyana Grihya sutra proclaims Vaishampayana as a teacher of Mahabharata. If the above inferences are correct, then it would imply that the Mahabharata was composed some 60 years after the Kurukshetra war, and the earliest reference to Mahabharata comes from a text dated to about 40 years later than the composition of the epic.

This lonesome reference to the Mahabharata in a late Vedic text, however, does not give us any idea about the contents of the Mahabharata at this early stage. It does give us some confidence that at least some version of Mahabharata was indeed composed by Vaishampayana.

While Vaishampayana may have been an early reciter of the Mahabharata, the setting in which he recites the epic (Janamejaya’s sarpasattra) is clearly the innovation of a later redactor.

The Sarpasattra of the Mahabharata is very likely inspired from the Sarpasattra of Panchavimsa Brahmana, a late Vedic text. The Vedic Sarpasattra does not involve casting snakes into the fire. The Vedic Sarpasattra is in fact performed by the humanoid Naga race in order to gain strength. The Panchavimsa Brahmana names one of the officiating priests as ‘Janamejaya’. But this is Janamejaya the Naga, not Janamejaya the son of Parikshit. He is named along with other famous Nagas such as Dhritarashtra Airavata.

The next important literary source is Jataka tales. These tales are certainly post-Vedic, and were probably composed centuries after the Buddha. Yet, they probably draw from popular myths of their time which in turn would be based on older material. The Jatakas may be dated ~300 BCE. Many important details of the lives of Krishna and the Pandava brothers can be found in Jataka stories. The story of Krishna as mentioned in the Jatakas closely parallels the version found in the Mahabharata and Harivansha, from the circumstances of his birth to the end of the Yadavas. The story of Pandava brothers, however, differs far more. Let us look at what the Jataka references tell us:

- Yudhisthira, Arjuna, Bhima, Nakula and Sahadeva were five sons of
  Pandu, and they had a wife named Kanhā (Krishnā, another name for
  Draupadi) \[Kunāla Jātaka\]
- There was a King Dhañanjaya, of the Yudhishthira-gotra, who ruled from
  Indraprastha \[Sambhava Jātaka, Vidhurapandita Jātaka, Dhūmakāri
  Jātaka\]
- Vidura and Sanjaya were the advisors of Dhananjaya \[Sambhava Jātaka\]
- Dhananjaya was fond of dice and was defeated in a game of dice by a
  Yaksha named Punnaka \[Vidhurapandita Jātaka\]
- Devagabbhā (Devaki) was the sister of King Kamsa, and it was foretold
  that one of her sons would destroy the lineage of Kamsa \[Ghata
  Jātaka\]
- Devagabbhā was married to the king of Mathura
- She had ten sons, among whom were Vasudeva, Baladeva and Arjuna, and
  they were known as Andhakavenhus
- Her sons were entrusted to the care of Nandagopā
- They (the sons) became famous as highway robbers, and when Kamsa found
  out about their parentage, conspired to kill them
- Kamsa arranged two famous wrestlers, Cānura and Mutthika, to have a
  match with them, and Baladeva killed both the wrestlers
- Vāsudeva threw a wheel which cut off the head of Kamsa
- The ten brothers conquered the city of Dvāravatī, with advice from
  Kanhadīpayana (Krishna Dvaipayana)
- They settled there and had many sons and daughters in that city.
- These sons played a joke on Kanhadipānyana, by dressing up a young lad
  as a pregnant woman, and asking the sage about when the baby would be
  born
- Kanhadipānyana told them that after seven days, he would give birth to
  a piece of wood that would bring about the end of the Andhakavenhu
  family
- Enraged, the sons killed Kanhadipānyana, but his prophecy came true,
  and the family ended by fighting each other
- Vasudeva was killed by a hunter named Jara, who pierced his foot by
  his arrow
- Another reason for the demise of Andhakavenhus is said to be their
  habit of drinking \[Kumbha Jātaka\]
- Vasudeva may have given spiritual teachings to Arjuna (although in the
  Jataka version, Ghatapandita, a brother of Vasudeva, gives spiritual
  teaching to Vasudeva; I suspect a later sectarian distortion in this)
- It is stated that Jambāvatī, of the Caṇḍāla caste, was the beloved
  queen consort of Vāsudeva Kaṇha, the king of Dvāravatī, and their son
  was named Sivi \[Mahā Ummagga Jātaka\]

The life of Krishna, as per the Jatakas, is almost exactly the same as that mentioned in Hindu epics, but with some important differences:

- In the Jatakas, Arjuna is a younger brother of Krishna. This would
  make him distinct from Arjuna, the son of Pandu. In this reference,
  however, I suspect a distortion. Arjuna, in the Hindu epic, is a
  cousin of Krishna, which the Jataka authors have distorted into a
  younger brother, although they also make reference to Arjuna the son
  of Pandu in a different Jataka.
- In the Jatakas, it is Krishna who receives spiritual teachings from
  Ghatapandita, his younger brother. In this scenario, Arjuna has no
  role to play, even though he finds mention in the Jataka tale. This is
  clearly a sectarian distortion. Krishna is known as a spiritual
  teacher in the Upanishads, and he most likely did pass on some
  spiritual teaching to Arjuna. Panini’s Ashtadhyayi (4.3.98) mentions
  the veneration of Vâsudeva and Arjuna, while Ghatapandita is a figure
  unknown outside of one particular Jataka tale. The Jataka, motivated
  by sectarian competition, attempts to create a counter-narrative by
  making Arjuna a silent spectator, while Krishna becomes the student,
  and the role of the teacher is assigned to a past birth of Buddha,
  named Ghatapandita.
- The third most important difference is that there is no mention of
  Krishna participating in the Kurukshetra war as far as the Jatakas are
  concerned.

In other words, the points at which Krishna of the Jatakas differs from the Krishna of Hindu epics are mainly the points at which Krishna’s story interacts with that of the Pandavas. Almost everything else about Krishna’s life can be found in the Jatakas. This tells us that the story of Krishna pretty much reached its final form long before the story of the Pandavas was finalized. Interestingly, there is a cursory mention of Akrura’s jewel in a late Vedic period text (Yaska’s Nirukta ~600 BCE), which is another episode from Krishna’s life as mentioned in Harivansha.

The story of the Pandavas is hardly as well developed in the Jatakas. The characters are familiar: the five brothers, their father Pandu, their wife Krishnā, and their advisor Vidura. The details vary too much. There is no mention of Dhritarashtra or his 100 sons. No Kurukshetra war. No exile. No house of lac. The Jataka story is quite unflattering towards Draupadi. It goes as follows:

Krishnā was the daughter of the king of Kosala. When her mother was pregnant with Krishnā, Brahmadatta, king of Benares, killed her father and carried off her mother. When the child was born, Brahmadatta adopted her as his own daughter; she is, therefore, called dvepitikā (=having two fathers). The king promised to grant her a boon, and she held a svayamvara, at which she chose as her husbands all the five sons of King Pandu; Ajjuna, Bhīma, Nakula, Yuddhitthila, and Sahadeva.

By her strong passions she won the love of them all. Not satisfied with them, she also made love to a hump-backed slave who was in her service. One day, when she was sick, all her husbands were gathered round her, and she made signs to each of them to show that she loved him best. Ajjuna, however, was suspicious, and by questioning the hump-back, learnt the truth. The five brothers left her and retired to the Himalaya, where they became ascetics.

Apart from this, there is a mention of a King Dhananjaya of the Yudhishthira gotra, ruler of Indraprastha. In the Jatakas, Dhananjaya is fond of dice, which is supposed to be a characteristic of Yudhisthira in the Mahabharata. His story in the Vidhurapandita Jātaka is a completely different story from the Hindu epic. Here, Dhananjaya loses a dice game with a yaksha, and has to give up his advisor Vidura. The Yaksha takes Vidura to the Naga world, in an attempt to win the Naga princess’s hand in marriage. Vidura, on the other hand, wins the princess for himself while impressing the Naga king with his wisdom, and then returns to Dhananjaya.

Even an indirect allusion to the Kurukshetra war (along with a mention of Duryodhana) is found only in a text as late as Arthashastra (~300 BCE). The Arthashastra tells us that there was a gambling match between Yudhishthira and Duryodhana, which the latter won. In another reference, the Arthashastra tells us that Duryodhana perished for his unwillingness to part with a portion of his kingdom. Another reference from the same text says that the Vrishnis attacked sage Dvaipayana, and perished as a result. (Which interestingly is closer to the Jataka version than the Mahabharata version). Yet, there is no mention of a great battle involving many kingdoms.

The historicity of the war cannot be denied, but it appears far more likely that it was a minor internal Kuru affair, and did not hold much importance even in the Vedic tradition, until a collegium of bards (sutas) and Brahmins (mostly Shaunakas), produced a volumnous, embellished account of the war, with the purpose of using the story to illustrate the Kuru-dharma, in an attempt to answer the challenges posed by Buddhism. One may expect at least some influence of Greek epics on the Mahabharata (as well as Ramayana), on account of the Indo-Greek (Yavana) kingdoms. The Mahabharata must have attained its present form by 200 BCE – 200 CE, since the later Kavya literature is clearly post-epic in both style and content.
