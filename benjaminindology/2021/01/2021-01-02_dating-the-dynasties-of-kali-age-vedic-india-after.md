History of India from Manu to Mahabharata: From the earliest sources

The epic Mahabharata is supposed to give us the history of Kuru kingdom till the reign of King Janamejaya. The record of kings and dynasties that followed Janamejaya are recorded in the Puranas. In this post, I will attempt to date them, from Janamejaya to the founding of Mauryan Empire.

###### Kuru/Vatsa kingdom

The Vishnu Purana lists 26 kings of Kuru after Janamejaya:

1.  S’atanika 2.  Aswamedhadatta 3.  Asima-krishna 4.  Nichakra 5.  Ushna 6.  Chitraratha 7.  Vrishnimat 8.  Sushena 9.  Sunitha 10. Richa 11. Nrichakshu 12. Sukhihala 13. Pariplava 14. Sunaya 15. Medhavin 16. Nripanjaya 17. Mridu 18. Tigma 19. Vrihadratha 20. Vasudana 21. S’atanika 22. Udayana 23. Ahinara 24. Khandapani 25. Niramitra 26. Kshemaka

Nichakra is said to have shifted his capital to Kaushambi, due to the flooding of Hasthinapura (Kuru capital). Kaushambi is known to be the capital of Vatsa kingdom. Perhaps Nichakra either founded or annexed Vatsa.

In any case, it is clear that the kings after Nichakra in this list are Vatsa kings. In fact, the 22nd in our list, Udayana, is known from Buddhist suttas to be a Vatsa king, and a contemporary of the Buddha and the Magadha king Bimbisara. The Purana says that the Kuru/Vatsa dynasty ended with Kshemaka.

###### The Kosala kingdom

After Vrihadbala of Kosala was slain in the Bharata war, the Purana informs us that the following kings succeeded him:

1.  Vrihatkshana 2.  Urukshepa 3.  Vatsa 4.  Vatsavyuha 5.  Prativyoman 6.  Divakara 7.  Sahadeva 8.  Vrihadas’wa 9.  Bhanuratha 10. Supratitha 11. Marudeva 12. Sunakshatra 13. Kinnara 14. Antariksha 15. Suvarna 16. Amitrajit 17. Vrihadraja 18. Dharman 19. Kritanjaya 20. Rananjaya 21. Sanjaya 22. Prasenajit 23. Kshudraka 24. Kundaka 25. Suratha 26. Sumitra

The Purana also inserts three more kings in between Sanjaya and Prasenajit: S’akya, S’uddhodana, and Rahula. I have removed them from our list because these are clearly Sakya kings; Sakya being the eponymous founder, Suddhodana being Buddha’s father, and Rahul being Buddha’s son. Sakya was a province under the Kosala kingdom, but Sakyans were never rulers of Kosala. However, these names are not arbitrarily placed. Prasenajit was indeed the king of Kosala during Buddha’s time, as the Buddhist suttas inform us.

###### The Magadha kingdom

The Magadha kings Jarasandha and his successor Sahadeva were slain in the epic. The kings of Magadha after Sahadeva (the Barhadratha dynasty), according to Vishnu Purana, go as follows:

1.  Somapi 2.  S’rutavat 3.  Ayutayus 4.  Niramitra 5.  Sukshatra 6.  Vrihatkarman 7.  Senajit 8.  S’rutanjaya 9.  Vipra 10. S’uchi 11. Kshemya 12. Suvrata 13. Dharma 14. Sus’uma 15. Dridhasena 16. Sumati 17. Suvala 18. Sunita 19. Satyajit 20. Vis’wajit 21. Ripunjaya

The ruling dynasty of Magadha is said to have ended with Ripunjaya. The Vishnu Purana then erroneously claims that Ripunjaya was succeeded by Pradyota. It is very clear from Buddhist texts that Pradyota was the ruler of Avanti and a contemporary of the Magadha king Bimbisara. The Purana mentions five kings (of Avanti) after Ripunjaya: Pradyota, Palaka, Vis’akhayupa, Janaka, Nandivarddhana.

The Purana states that Shishunagas succeeded the Pradyotas. This, however, does not mean that Pradyotas ruled in Magadha. Sisunaga took over Magadha and then annexed Avanti. The following is the king list of Shishunagas according to Vishnu Purana:

1.  S’is’unaga 2.  Kakavarna 3.  Kshemadharman 4.  Kshatraujas 5.  Vidmisara 6.  Ajatas’atru 7.  Dharbaka 8.  Udayas’wa 9.  Nandivarddhana 10. Mahanandi

The Vidmisara of this list corresponds to the Magadha king Bimbisara known to Buddhist texts. His sons/descendants Ajatashatru and Udayin/Udayashwa are also known to Buddhist texts. King Pradyota of Avanti would have been contemporary to Vidmisara/Bimbisara.

This seems to be a chronological error in the Purana, because Bimbisara and his descendants were from a different dynasty that reigned in Magadha after the Barhadrathas and before the Sisunagas. The Purana, however, not only includes them in Sisunaga dynasty, but erroneously places them after Sisunagas.

But the Purana isn’t entirely wrong. Pradyota was indeed the contemporary of the king of Magadha (Bimbisara) who succeeded the Barhadrathas, and in that sense Pradyota did “inherit the earth” after the Barhadrathas. And indeed, just as the Purana says, the Pradyotas were overthrown by Sisunagas. The only mistake of the Purana is to not realize that Bimbisara/Vidmisara and his descendants were actually contemporaries of Pradyota.  
  
According to Buddhist texts, the kings from Bimbisara to Udayin preceded the Sishunagas. Udayin was the last king in Bimbisara’s dynasty, who was dethroned by the citizens for his wickedness, and his minister Sisunaga was elected as the monarch. (Note: It is possible that Buddhist texts portray Udayin in a negative light because he was a devout Jain, according to Jain texts).

The Buddhist version tallies with the Puranic accounts of other dynasties. For instance, Prasenajit of Kosala was the 22nd king (after Vrihadbala), while Udayana would be the 22nd king after Janamejaya in Kuru/Vatsa. Bimbisara was certainly contemporary to both. Since Ripunjaya is the 21st king in Magadha, it follows that Bimbisara would have succeeded Ripunjaya (the last Barhadratha) in Magadha.

According to the Puranas, the dynasties of Prasenajit, Udayana, Bimbisara and Pradyota (all contemporaries), came to an end after the next 3-4 generations. All these were most likely conquered by Sisunaga dynasty, originating in Magadha.

Therefore, it was Sisunaga, rather than Mahapadma Nanda, who laid the foundations of imperial Magadha. The reason Puranas call Nanda the ‘destroyer of Kshatriyas’ is because while Sisunaga was regarded as a Kshatriya, Nanda and all the kings that followed him were all born of Sudra mothers.

The Buddhist text Mahavamsa, lists the following kings of Magadha:

Bimbisara (reigned c. 546–494 BCE) 52 years reign  
Ajatashatru (c. 494–462 BCE) 32 years reign  
Udayin (c. 462–446 BCE) 16 years reign  
Anuruddha and Munda (c. 446–438 BCE) 8 years reign  
Nagadasaka (c. 438–414 BCE) 24 years reign  
Shishunaga (c. 414–396 BCE) 18 years reign  
Kalashoka (c. 396–368 BCE) 28 years reign  
Ten sons of Kalashoka (c. 368–346 BCE) 22 years reign  
Nanda Dynasty (c. 346–324 BCE) 22 years reign  
Candagutta (324-300 BCE) 24 years reign  
Bindusara (300-272 BCE) 28 years reign

The text, of course, does not mention the dates but only the length of the reigns. But the dates may be assigned to them on the basis of the identification Chandragupta (Candagutta) with the Sandrocottus of Greek historians.

Some have tried to identify Sandrocottus with Samudragupta, but the accounts of Sandrocottus do not match those of Samudragupta. Sandrocottus is said to have overthrown the then ruler of Gangaridai (Magadha). Samudragupta, on the other hand, was a legitimate heir to the Gupta Empire.

The Mahavamsa states that Bimbisara was fifteen years old when he became king, and Buddha was five years elder to him. From this we may estimate that Buddha was born in 566 BCE, began teaching in 531 BCE, at the age of 35, and passed away in 482 BCE, at the age of 84.

Since the Puranas list 22 generations between the reigns of Janamejaya and Bimbisara, it follows that Janamejaya would have reigned somewhere in the interval of 1200-1000 BCE. \[The Puranas are more consistent about the number of generations during this period than they are about the lengths of reigns.\]
