Dating the Dynasties of Kali Age – Vedic India After Janamejaya

There has been a lot of buzz surrounding Mr Nilesh Oak’s theories of the antiquity of Indian civilization. He has proposed a revolutionary model for India’s timeline, which may be described roughly as a ‘rule of 7s’: The Mahabharata war happened 7000 years ago, the Rama-Ravana Yuddha 14,000 years ago, and Rigveda (earliest parts) was composed 21,000 years ago.

In this post, I will focus exclusively on the claims made by Mr Oak in his book, *When did the Mahabharata War Happen*. When reading his book, I identified several instances of textual re-interpretation (or misinterpretation) and bad inferences used to force-fit the textual evidence with the theory and proposed date.

\[Note: The reference numbers are the same as those from his book\]

**Re-defining the word ‘near’ (समीप)**

Reference no 6 for the first day of war goes as follows: विशाखयोः समीपस्थौ बृहस्पतिशनैश्चरौ \|  
The reference clearly states that Saturn and Jupiter were situated near (समीप) the nakshatra Vishakha.

In Mr Oak’s proposed first day of war (16 Oct 5561 bce), Saturn and Jupiter were in two different nakshatras. Jupiter is in Mula and Saturn in Hasta. These are far from Vishakha. In fact, there are two Nakshatras in between Vishakha and Mula and also two Nakshatras between Vishakha and Hasta. By no definition, does this count as being ‘near’ Vishakha.

**‘Vakri’ motion means oblique crossing of ecliptic?**

The usual, standard, well-attested definition of the word ‘Vakri’ in Indian astronomy is ‘retrograde motion’. It is a loop traced by the path of a planet as it moves across the sky. The phenomenon is quite spectacular and hard to miss for any astronomer.

In references 11, 13, 14 of the book, it is described how Mars was seen going ‘Vakri’ near Magha, and again, near Jyeshtha/Anuradha. Admittedly, Mars does not go retrograde two times in a year. But that’s not my problem. I’m not making the claim that all astronomy references in Mahabharata are visual observations of Mahabharata times. That claim is not yet established.

Mr Oak, in order to corroborate his date, redefines the very word ‘Vakri’. According to him, Vakri is when a planet obliquely crosses the ecliptic. It is to be noted that the ecliptic itself is not visible in the sky. In other words, Mr Oak’s vakri motion is hardly a noticeable event. To illustrate the same, I’ll be borrowing images from [Astronomy of India](https://astronomyofindia.wordpress.com/2019/09/08/a-complete-rebuttal-of-oaks-revolutionary-theory-of-vakra-motion/) blog.

![](https://benjaminindology.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/image.png?w=768)

In the above picture, the green line is the ecliptic, the red line is the path traced by Mars. The point marked by the yellow arrow ‘A’ shows the real Vakri motion of Mars, while the point marked by red arrow ‘B’ shows the ‘Vakri’ as defined by Nilesh Oak. Remember, the green line is not visible in the sky. While ‘A’ is a spectacular, well-known event in astronomy, ‘B’ is a meaningless, hardly noticeable event that isn’t given special importance in any astronomy text. Even taken literally, the word ‘Vakri’ means ‘curved’ and never simply ‘oblique’.

To quote [Astronomy of India](https://astronomyofindia.wordpress.com/2020/02/17/the-blunder-inherent-in-shri-oaks-vakri-motion-theory/) blog on this:

***Here is the glaring blunder in this theory: The Moon crosses the ecliptic several times a month. Does the Moon become “Vakri”? Can the Moon ever be Vakri? According to Shri Oak, the answer is yes. Yet, can anybody point out in history, a single instance when the Moon has been called Vakri?***

At the end of the day, there isn’t a single reference in any astronomy text that would define ‘Vakri’ in the manner of Mr Oak. And he acknowledges this much. But his argument simply amounts to saying: ***My date is corroborated because Vakri means oblique crossing, and Vakri means oblique crossing because on my proposed dates, Mars was doing oblique crossing.*** This is called ‘circular reasoning’ or begging the question.

**Changing the definition of ‘afflicted’ (पीडित)**

Varahamihira defines affliction in the following way:  
रवि रविसुत भोगमागतं क्षितिसुत भेदनवक्रदूषितम्।  
ग्रहणगतम् अथोल्क्याहतं नियतम् उषाकर पीडितञ्च तत्॥  
A nakshtra is said to be afflicted (pidita) when  
(1) It is tenanted by Sun(ravi) or Saturn (ravisuta)  
(2) It is spoilt by Mars(kshitisuta)by either cutting through or by vakra motion  
(3) When it is involved in an eclipse  
(4) When it is stuck by a meteor  
(5) When it is manifestly crushed by moon  
(6) Or when something unnatural happens to it.  
\[Source: [Astronomy of India](https://astronomyofindia.wordpress.com/2019/09/08/a-complete-rebuttal-of-oaks-revolutionary-theory-of-vakra-motion/)\]

According to the standard definition, in order to afflict a Nakshatra, a planet must be near the Nakshatra. But according to Mr Oak’s definition, a planet can ‘afflict’ a Nakshatra if one of them (either the planet or the nakshatra) is in the eastern part of the sky and the other is in the western side, making an obtuse angle. Once again, this definition is not attested anywhere. It is used because it corroborates Mr Oak’s dates.

**Making unfounded claims about history**

Many of Mr Oak’s ‘corroborations’ depend upon the ability of astronomers of Mahabharata times to be able to see Neptune, Uranus and Pluto. The reader should remember that these planets aren’t visible to the naked eye, and can only be seen using a telescope. Mr Oak, therefore, conjectures that astronomers of Mahabharata times had some device by which they could observe astronomical bodies otherwise invisible to the naked eye. Once again, he uses a conjecture to ‘corroborate’ a conjecture (i.e, his theory).

**Blatant misinterpretation of reference no. 83**

Reference 83:

**चतुर्दशी पञ्चदशी कृतेयं राहुणा पुनः** \[The 14th and 15th tithis have been devoured by Rahu\]**  
तदा च भारते युद्धे प्राप्ता चाद्य क्षयाय नः** \[This happened at the time of the Bharata war and has again occurred for our destruction.\]

As can been seen, the reference states that two eclipses occurred on the 14th and 15th tithis. *This has not been corroborated for Mr Nilesh Oak’s dates.* The next line states that this (i.e., untimely eclipses) had also happened at the time of Bharata war. He has, however, found two eclipses in the year 5525 bce, that occur in the interval of 13 days, similar to *his interpretation* of what happened during the Bharata war. But as we can clearly see, the verse states that two eclipses occurred on the 14th and 15th lunar tithis, which Mr Oak hasn’t been able to corroborate.

**Half-moon or gibbous moon can be ‘visualized’ as a full moon, and references to eclipses can be ignored or explained away**

Reference 47 (10th day of war) goes as follows:

**दुःशासनं ततः क्रुद्धः पीडयाम् आस पाण्डवः** \[Angered, the Pandava then attacked Dushasan\]**  
पर्वणीव सुसंक्रुद्धो राहुर् उग्रो निशाकरम्** \[Like the Full Moon attacked by angry Rahu\]

According to Mr Oak’s dates, it was Amavasya on the first day of war. Starting from Amavasya, the phases of the moon progress as follows:

![](https://benjaminindology.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/image-1.png?w=800)

Take a good look at how the moon looks like in the 10th through 14th days of the war. Does it look like a full moon? It does not. Which means Mr Nilesh Oak’s dates fail to corroborate all the full moon references made from the 10th through the 14th days of the war.

It is curious why any poet would insert full moon references for 9 days continuously. Obviously, full moon can only be thought of as ‘full’ on the true full moon day, or at best one day before or after. Why would any sensible poet, with even basic common sense, would make a reference to full moon for 9 continuous days?

The answer is simple, but Mr Oak misses it completely. The poet’s intent isn’t to talk about what’s happening in the sky at all, but to glorify the fallen warriors by comparing their pale faces with the full moon. In any case, these references fail to corroborate Mr Oak’s theory.

Furthermore, reference 47 also makes a reference to Rahu attacking the moon. This indicates a lunar eclipse on the 10th day of war. Nilesh Oak’s dates fail to corroborate this as well.

**Misinterpreting the word ‘Vishakha’**

Reference no. 57 states that the Moon was situated between two stars of the Vishakha nakshatra on the 16th day of war. For reference, I am showing below the order of nakshatras:

![](https://i.redd.it/nm6950mmqpr41.jpg)

The problem with this reference is that on the 16th day of war proposed by Mr Oak, the Moon happens to be in Punarvasu. He conjectures that the word “Vishakha” over here does not refer to the nakshatra of that name. Instead it means “branch”. So reference no. 57 can be taken to mean that Moon was between two branches. What does that mean?

He conjectures that these are the branches (ie. stars) of Punarvasu. Reference no. 59 does mention Punarvasu stars ‘behind’ the Moon, but regardless of how this is interpreted, it still does not establish that ‘Vishakha’ refers to ‘branches’ of Punarvasu.

To conclude, I would say that while Mr Nilesh Oak has done a commendable job extracting all the astronomical references from the Mahabharata, his inferences aren’t very sound. Most of the important astronomical references in the Mahabharata text do not corroborate his proposed date, at least not without taking support of a large number of unlikely conjectures.
