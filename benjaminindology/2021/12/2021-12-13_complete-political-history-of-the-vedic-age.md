Where was Airyanan Vaejah? Identifying the Indo-Iranian homeland

**Introduction**

The Vedas are the earliest literature of the Indian subcontinent, and there is hardly any dispute over this in the academic community. Furthermore, linguists who have studied the Vedas declare that the language of the Vedas is more archaic than the classical Sanskrit, and admit that the Vedas have been preserved very rigorously, almost like a tape recording from thousands of years ago.

The more or less ‘unchanging’ nature of the Vedic texts means that Vedic texts are highly valuable and reliable sources for the history of the centuries during which the Vedas were being composed. This time period is known as the Vedic age.

Linguists determine which Vedic texts are earlier and which are later, by comparing the language (vocabulary, grammar and style) of these texts. Vedic age is the earliest known historic age. Everything that happened before the Vedic age is by definition prehistoric.

The Vedic texts describe historic events and name historic personalities. In this manner, they give glimpses of the history of the Vedic age. Vedic texts are further used to deduce the lifestyle of the Vedic people, and how it evolved from the early, to the middle, to the late Vedic age.

Yet, the Vedic texts do not contain a systematic political history of the Vedic age. It has often been alleged that no history pertaining to this period has been passed down in India. Yet, this isn’t entirely true. Tradition regards 20 books – 2 Itihasas and 18 Puranas – as authentic sources of history of the Vedic age.

Itihasa-Purana is a genre of texts that is frequently referenced in the Vedic texts, where they are often portrayed as an extension of the Vedas.

The Vishnu Purana claims that the sage Krishna Dvaipayana Vyasa composed the original Purana and that four disciples of Vyasa composed four different Puranas. The original Purana is likely to be the Purana incorporated into the Harivamsha, and the four Puranas composed by Vyasa’s disciples are likely to be Brahma, Brahmanda, Matsya and Vayu. The genealogical content of these Puranas is estimated to be the oldest.

The Vishnu Purana further states that the Vishnu Purana itself was composed on the basis of these five original Puranas. Other Puranas were composed at an even later date, and gradually, the number of Puranas multiplied to 18.

Despite accumulating material from later dates, these five (or six) early Puranas can be said to contain ‘core’ portions going back to the Vedic age. What are these core portions? These core portions are enumerated by the Puranas themselves.

The following are the five distinguishing marks (pancha lakshana) of a Purana:

\(1\) Sarga (creation)  
(2) Upasarga (secondary creation)  
(3) Vamsha (royal genealogies)  
(4) Manvantaras (an ennumeration of the original progenitors, principal sages and gods of each aeon)  
(5) Vamshanucharita (short biographies of kings that are usually embedded within the genealogies).

The Vamshanucharitas (biographies) of the important kings of Ikshvakus, Kurus and Vrishnis probably evolved into the epics Ramayana, Mahabharata and Harivamsha. These epics were known as Itihasa. Thus, the core parts of Itihasa-Purana come down to us from the Vedic age. We must, therefore, abandon the notion that no history of the Vedic age has been passed on.

Perhaps the most famous attempt to reconstruct a correct historical account of the Vedic age has been that of Pargiter. His work, however, suffers from two major defects. Firstly, he only considered 20 or so synchronisms. The synchronisms that did not fit with his model were dismissed as Brahminical inventions. On the other hand, I have successfully explained 67 synchronisms, including those mentioned by Pargiter. I have so far made the most comprehensive collection of these synchronisms.

The second major defect in Pargiter’s work was his over-reliance on genealogical gaps. While a few gaps in the genealogies are inevitable, assuming many such gaps dilutes the significance of his synchronisms. His approach was basically to “distribute” the names of all other dynasties at different points along the longest known dynasty – the Solar dynasty. My approach, however, was to begin by breaking down the genealogies into segments consisting of 10 generations each and treating each segment as a potentially independent dynasty. As I began rearranging the segments according to the synchronisms, some of these segments quite naturally fit together in the order described in the Puranas – bolstering my confidence in the Puranic sources. My approach allows me to explain 67 synchronisms with little to no genealogical gaps.

The Puranas give Purukutsa as the successor of Māndhātri, but this is an error. It is known that some Kshatriyas became Brahmanas because their ancestors were composers of Vedic hymns. Those Kshatriyas that joined the Angiras group were known as Kevala Angirasas. The descendants of Purukutsa are a Kevala Angiras gotra called Vishnuvriddhas. The descendants of Mandhatri are a Kevala Angiras gotra called Haritas. The dharmashastras do not forbid intermarriage between Vishnuvriddhas and Haritas. Generally, when even one common ancestor is known, the gotras do not intermarry. The fact that dharmashastras have not forbidden intermarriage among Vishnuvriddhas and Haritas is a strong indication that these two gotras did not share a common ancestor.

The Pravara lists mention a son/descendant of Mandhatri called Kutsa. It seems that the Puranas have mistakenly identified Kutsa with Purukutsa. Therefore, the sons of Mandhatri would be Kutsa, Ambarisha, and Mucukunda. It seems that after Mandhatri was killed by Lavana, the prince of Mathura, the descendants of Mandhatri adopted the Brahminical occupation. This would place Mandhatri about the same time as the events of the Ramayana epic.

A few more synchronisms of this lineage deserve mention. Drishadvati, the daughter of Samhitashva, had many husbands. She was a mother to king Prasenajit, but also to four other kings, namely, Vasumanas (s/o Haryashva), Pratardana (s/o Divodasa), Shivi Aushinara, and Ashtaka (s/o Vishvamitra). It is to be noted that this Shivi is not the first of his name. ‘Shivi Aushinara’ was a title used by the kings of the Aushinara tribe, and even the Aushinara king of Mahabharata times was called by his title ‘Shivi’. In a later myth, Drishadvati was equated to the daughter of the fallen Yayati, but this is clearly a myth.

Regarding king Prasenajit, there is an interesting synchronism. Prasenajit married Matinara’s daughter Gauri. Jamadagni married Renuka, daughter of Prasenajit. Once, Renuka saw the Yadu king Citraratha enjoying with his wives in a pond and had a sinful thought. Citraratha’s son was Shashabindu, and Shashabindu’s daughter was known as Bindumati Caitrarathi, who married Mandhatri.

**Bharata 2- Kashi dynasty**

Why was the Vidarbha line joined to the Shashabindu line? It’s a long story. The Shashabindus ruled in the Malwa region. Malwa region later on came under the control of Satvatas, especially after Babhru Daivavriddha of Satvata dynasty. The Satvatas themselves originate in the dynasty of Sahasrada, but they merged into the line of Dasharha of Vidarbha dynasty. As a result, when the Satvatas, who now styled themselves as Dasharhas, took over the Malwa kingdom, they adopted the former kings of Malwa as their ancestors.

**Bharata 3 – Panchala dynasty**

According to the Mahabharata, Bharata’s son was Bhumanyu, his son was Suhotra, and his son was Ajamidha. One of his wives was Nilini, and she had two sons – Dushanta and Parameshti. It is stated that these two were founders of Panchala.

In the Rig Veda, Sarnjayas (descendants of Srinjaya) and Maudgalyas (descendants of Mudgala) are the two major branches of Bharatas (Panchalas). It stands to reason that these two descend from Dushanta and Parameshti.

The Puranas give a slightly different version. Dushanta and Parameshti are not found in any Purana. Ajamidha’s son Shanti is mentioned. One may identify Shanti with Dushanta. But what about Parameshti?

It seems clear that the Puranas have merged the lines of Dushanta and Parameshti. Due to this merger, Parameshti (Purujanu) has been mentioned as a grandson of Shanti.

**The Future Kings**

The Purana names the ‘future’ kings of Magadha, Kosala and Kuru (Kaushambi). Given that the narration of the Purana is set in the reign of the Kuru king Adhisimakrsna, the Ayodhya king Divakara and the Magadha king Senajit, the so-called ‘future’ kings are their descendants and successors. Their names were added later, but in the future tense, framed as a prophecy, so as to not disrupt the narrative flow of the Purana. That the Puranas were updated regularly is a feature rather than a bug.

For the sake of showing the synchronisms, I have shown the lines of Shakyas and Pradyotas alongside the Magadha line. The Puranas state unanimously that Ripunjaya was the last in the line of Brihadratha. The Puranas also make it clear that Pradyotas rose to power right after the decline of Brihadrathas.

There is however, some confusion over their location. The Vishnu Purana makes Pradyotas the rulers of Magadha. However, the Vayu states that the Pradyotas rose to power by overthrowing ‘Vitahotras’ (traditional rulers of Avanti). Vayu Purana is generally held to be older than Vishnu Purana. The Buddhist texts also make Pradyotas the rulers of Avanti. It is, therefore, far more likely that Pradyotas ruled in Avanti, right after the decline of Brihadrathas in Magadha.

The Puranas also state that the Magadha king Shishunaga annexed Avanti and destroyed the Pradyotas. The Puranas, however, make no mention of who ruled in Magadha during the time of the Pradyotas. This mystery is solved by the Buddhist texts, that mention the ‘Haryanka’ dynasty that ruled in Magadha. From Bhatiya to Nagadasaka are the Haryankas.

The Buddhist texts state that the citizens of Magadha removed Nagadasaka and instated the minister Shishunaga as the king. Shishunaga’s son Kalashoka (or Kakavarna) is said to have 10 sons, many of whom succeeded Kalashoka in quick succession. Even though Shishunagas succeeded the Haryankas, the Puranas have done something strange. The Puranas have included some of the Haryanka names among the sons/successors of Kalashoka.

The Purana mentions that the Magadha king Shishunaga annexed Avanti ruled by Pradyotas. It seems that the dynasties of Kosala and Kaushambi-Kurus also came to end during the reign of Shishunaga. The Shishunagas were ultimately replaced by the Nanda dynasty. Nandas further extended the dominance of Magadha across the Ganga-Yamuna doab. This period marks the end of traditional Kshatriya dynasties, especially in the Ganga-Yamuna doab.

**Dating the Dynasties**

What remains is to assign proper dates to the reigns of these kings. Mainstream historians (with good reason) have identified Chandragupta Maurya with the Sandrocoptus of Greco-Roman sources. According to Greco-Roman sources, Sandrocoptus met Alexander (‘the Great’) during the latter’s Indian campaign. The suggestion that Sandrocoptus be identified with the Gupta ruler Samudragupta is, unfortunately, entirely devoid of any merit.

Accordingly, we may date the beginning of Mauryan empire in the ball park 330-320 bce. Based on the data gathered from the Mahavamsha chronicle, we may date the founding of Nanda Empire to 380 bce, and further deduce that king Bimbisara of Magadha, a contemporary of Gautama Buddha, reigned 8 generations before Mahapadma Nanda. Considering an average reign of 20 years, we may date Bimbisara’s reign to 540-520 bce.  
  
Gautama Buddha was a senior contemporary to the Kuru-Kaushambi King Udayana, as per Buddhist suttas. The funeral rites of Gautama Buddha were carried out during the reign of Udayana. His reign may be fixed to 480-460 bce, considering that Buddha is said to have lived well into his eighties.

King Nichakra would be (as per the Puranas) 18 generations prior to Udayana. Taking 20 years per regin, we may date Nichakra’s reign to 840-820 bce. The Puranas state that Hasthinapur was washed away by Ganges during his reign, and he established his capital at Kaushambi.

The archeologist B.B. Lal discovered that Hasthinapur was indeed destroyed by a Gangetic flood around 800 bce, and at the same time he found the beginning of settlement in Kaushambi. The dates predicted by my model, therefore, match the dates arrived at by archeology.

Following the rule of 20 years per dynasty, the reign of Purukutsa, the earliest king mentioned in Rigveda, would be dated to around 1400 bce. King Bharata, who is mentioned in the Rigveda only as a distant memory, would have reigned around 1500 bce. The famous Bharata dynasty kings mentioned in Rigveda are Divodasa (~1300 bce) and Sudas (~1240 bce).

King Shri Rama of Ayodhya would have reigned around (~1240 bce). During this time period, the Dasa-Rakshasa communities were headed by Shambara and Ravana. The Kuru King Dhritarashtra would have reigned around 1000 bce, and the battle of Kurukshetra would have happened at the end of his reign, around 980 bce.

\[59\] Jaiminiya Brahmana (3.312) mentions how the Panchala king Kesin, son of Darbha, stayed with Kabandha Ātharvana, who taught him the twelve-day ritual with its metres transposed. Kabandha was the disciple of Vyāsa’s disciple Sumantu. (Sumantu was to contemporary to Jaimini, Vaishampayana and Paila).  
\[60\] Ucchaihshravas, the brother of Parikshit, ruled the Kuru kingdom after Parikshit’s death, until Janamejaya came of age; Keshin, son of Darbha, was his nephew  
\[61\] The Kuru king Adhisimakrsna, the Ayodhya king Divakara and the Magadha king Senajit were contemporaries, and during their reign a sattra was organized by Rishis, and the Vayu Purana was recited to the Rishis  
\[62\] Bhāṭiya of Magadha was defeated and slain by Brahmadatta of Anga  
\[63\] Prasenajit of Kosala, Bimbisara of Magadha, Pradyota of Avanti, and Siddhartha Gautama were contemporaries of the same age group. King Bimbisāra sent his physician Jīvaka to King Pradyota to heal his jaundice. Bimbisara and Prasenajit were married to each other’s sister. Siddhartha Gautama was five years older than Bimbisara.  
\[64\] Udayana of Kaushambi and Ajatashatru of Magadha were younger contemporaries of Siddhartha Gautama.

Trivial (they are easy to synchronize but not discussed above):  
\[65\] Yayati’s eldest brother Yati married Go, daughter of Kākutstha.  
\[66\] Druhyu king Aruddha/Angara was slain by Yauvanasva.  
\[67\] Turvasha king Marutta adopted Dushyanta Paurava

Unexplained and rejected as spurious:  
\[68\] Shakuntala (mother of King Bharata) is said to be the daughter of Vishvamitra, but this is untenable. According to Mahabharata, Vishvamitra was born in the Bharata lineage, and is even called ‘Bharatarshabha’ (Best of Bharatas) in Vedic texts. Vedic texts speak of Shakuntala as an ‘Apsara’ without mentioning her parentage.  
\[69\] According to a statement in a late Vedic text, Dirghatamas coronated King Bharata. This does not accord with other synchronicities, nor does it make sense, historically. Dirghatamas is a Rigvedic rishi, whereas Bharata was already a distant memory in the Rigveda.
