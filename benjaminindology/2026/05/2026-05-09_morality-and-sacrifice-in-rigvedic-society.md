How many Vishvamitras? The Key To Solving the Puzzle of Ikshvaku Genealogy

Early Indo-Aryan society, rests on three fundamental rules, each associated with a principal Aditya: Varuna, Mitra, and Aryaman. Together, they form the moral backbone of a world that was otherwise war-like, unstable, and lacking in formal law.

The first rule belongs to Varuna: speak the truth and honor your word. In a society without strong institutions, where violence and rivalry could easily dominate, social order depends on trust. If speech cannot be relied upon, then promises mean nothing, agreements collapse, and human life becomes unpredictable. Varuna therefore stands for the sacred binding force of truthfulness. To break one’s word is not merely a social fault; it is a rupture in the very fabric of order.

The second rule belongs to Mitra: a friend or ally must not be harmed. In a stateless and conflict-ridden environment, there may be no universal legal protection against injury, robbery, enslavement, or killing. Life may otherwise be governed by force. But once a Mitra-relation is established, once a bond of alliance or friendship is sealed, that relationship becomes inviolable. The ally is no longer just another person in a dangerous world; he enters a protected moral space. This is a crucial step toward peace, because it creates trust where there was once only hostility. Civilization begins, not with abstract law, but with the sanctity of alliance.

The third rule belongs to Aryaman: the harmless traveler seeking temporary refuge must not be harmed. Wherever possible, he should be given food and lodging. This too is a civilizing principle. In a world without stable public institutions, the traveler is vulnerable. He arrives with no local ties, no power, and no guarantee of protection. Aryaman therefore governs hospitality, the duty to shelter the stranger and ensure that even a passing guest is not treated with suspicion or cruelty. Through this rule, society extends a measure of order beyond kinship and immediate alliance.

Aryaman’s domain also includes the ethics of food sharing. The noble Arya does not eat selfishly, but first offers food to the gods, the ancestors, travelers, the needy, animals, and one’s own family and loved ones. This is not merely generosity in a modern sense. It is a sacred act of distribution, a recognition that what one possesses is never wholly one’s own. Before the self may consume, the world around it must be acknowledged and honored. Food becomes part of a larger moral economy.

Seen in this light, sacrifice embodies all three principles at once. Truth is spoken in the form of inspired hymns that praise and invite the gods to the offering. The gods are honored before eating, which turns food into a shared sacred gift rather than private consumption. And through this very act of sharing, a Mitra-relation is created: a bond of alliance with the divine, one that secures favor, reciprocity, and protection.

Sacrifice, then, is not trade. It is not an exchange between equal parties in a marketplace. It is something deeper and more personal: alliance, friendship, and sacred reciprocity. In this vision, the earliest order of society is built not by contracts alone, but by truthfulness, protected friendship, and generous hospitality. These are the foundations upon which peace begins to emerge from chaos.
