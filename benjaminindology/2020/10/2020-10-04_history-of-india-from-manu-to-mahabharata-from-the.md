Critiquing Talageri (Part 2) – Who were “Vedic Aryans”?

The Vishnu Purana (3.6.15) speaks about the origins of the Puranas. It discusses how Vyasa compiled the Purana in a single volume, and then taught it to Romaharshańa. Romaharshańa was the father of Ugrashrava Sauti who recited the Mahabharata to rishis at the Naimisha forest. Romaharshańa in turn passed on the Purana to six of his disciples. Romaharshana composed his own version of the Purana, and his disciples also composed three other Puranas.

Vishnu Purana claims to embody the collected substance of those four Puranas. But surprisingly, the Vishnu Purana declares Brahma Purana to be the first Purana. As far as the setting of its narration goes, Brahma Purana does appear to be the Purana compiled by Romaharshana and narrated to sages at the Naimisha. It is also mentioned in the Mahabharata by the sages of Naimisha that Romaharshana used to recite Pauranik stories to them, and this is precisely how the Brahma Purana (BP) begins as well.

Regardless of the fact that BP contains some fairly late material, most of its early material on the histories of dynasties, especially in the early chapters, appears to be based on a genuine ancient tradition that – in many ways – differs from later Puranas but parallels the Mahabharata. So I decided to base my research on ancient Indian history on Brahma Purana. The older the source material, the less distortions, and the more reliable it is likely to be.

In this post my agenda will be to shine light on the pre-Mahabharata histories of the most important dynasties, using some of the earliest historical material available to us, including the Vedic texts and the Brahma Purana.

Note: I have tried my best to always make a conservative estimate of the generation gap between two figures, wherever possible. I do not feel the need to unnecessarily create a “long chronology”. If this approach doesn’t suit you, this post will still be of value to you. Feel free to add as many “gaps” in the genealogies as you like. I would prefer to be a bit on the conservative side.

The Rigveda mentions that Mānavas – the community of descendants of Manu – are composed of five separate groups (RV VIII.9.2). Elsewhere (I.108.8), these five groups are identified as Yadu, Turvaśa, Druhyu, Anu, and Pūru. These five peoples are called ‘Ārya’, in contrast with Dasyu:

O Aśvins, wondrous ones, scattering barley by a “wolf”, milking out refreshment for the race of Manu, blasting sound at the Dasyu with a bagpipe, you made wide light for the Ārya. (RV I.117.21)

Rigveda (X.22.8) describes Dasyu as akarmā (non-performers of Vedic sacrifice), amantu (devoid of Vedic insights), anyavrata (followers of non-Vedic commandments), and amānuṣa (non-descendant of Manu). It is clear that Rigvedic people distinguished the Ārya/Mānuṣa from Dasyu based on cultural differences. The followers of Vedic practices self-identified as Ārya or Mānuṣa, and claimed common descent from Manu.

The origin of Mānuṣas is described in the Satapatha Brahmana (8th adhyaya, 1st Brahmana). Manu survived a great flooding event with the help of a giant fish, and then created the goddess Idâ. He offered libations of clarified butter, sour milk, whey, and curds. These offerings transformed into his daughter, Idâ. The Brahmana goes on to say: “Through her (Idâ) he generated this race, which is this race of Manu”.

The Rigveda mentions Purūravas as the son of Iḍā (X.95.18). Therefore, Purūravas was originally thought of as a universal ancestor of the five peoples, the Āryas or Mānuṣas. The Purana includes a sixth people (Ikṣvāku), who do not trace their descent from Purūravas or Iḍā. But this belief could not have existed in Vedic times. The Vedas proclaim clearly that the race of Manu, consisting of five peoples, descended from Iḍā. The so-called Ikṣvāku, therefore, was originally used as a synonym for Paurava (descendant of Puru). Descendants of Puru are alternatively referred to as Aikṣvāka. [I’ve made a separate post on this.](https://benjaminindology.wordpress.com/2020/07/25/the-solar-dynasty-origin-of-the-ikshvakus/) More on this later.

Let us turn our attention to the Pauravas. Rigveda mentions a genealogy of Bhāratas. From Bharata descended Devavāta (III.23.2), also known as Devavant (VII.18.22). From him descended Sr̥ñjaya (IV. 15.4; VI.27.7), from him Vadhryaśva (VI.61), from him Divodāsa (VI.47.25), from him Pijavana and from him Sudās (VII.18). These Bhāratas were a branch of Pūru (I.63.7). But they had rivalry with other Bharatas and Pūrus (VII.33.6, VII.8.4, VII.18.13).

Sudās fought a Battle of Ten Kings (RV, VII.18), and defeated many enemies at the Paruṣṇi river, such as Śimyu, Paktha, Bhalānas, Alina, Viṣānin, Śiva, Vaikarṇa, Pr̥śnigu, as well as Turvaśa, Yakṣu (Yadu) and Druhyu. Bheda, Ajas, Śighras, and Yakṣus, who attacked Sudās at Yamunā were also defeated. Sudās also defeated a branch of Pūrus, who may be identified as the defeated Bharata-branch. I have made a [separate post](https://benjaminindology.wordpress.com/2020/08/16/origin-of-kurus-a-tale-of-double-revenge/) on how these defeated Bharatas took the help of Vasistha and revived under the name of Kuru. After the fall of Sudās at the hands of the Kurus, his dynasty remained in obscurity for two or three generations, and was revived by again by Somaka, son of Sahadeva (RV, IV.15). It is also said in the Brahma Purana: “When the family declined in prosperity, Ajamīḍha’s son (descendant) Somaka was born.”

Let us look at what Brahma Purana says about Pauravas. The BP genealogy inserts 5 names between Puru and Raudrāśva whereas in the Mahabharata there are none. Nothing can be said with certainty about these 5 names. What can be said with some confidence is that Raudrāśva, Ṛceyu, Matināra, Taṃsu, and Duṣyanta were important pre-Bharata Pauravas from whom the Bharatas claim descent. The establishment of Kuru likely happened with the overthrow of Sudās. It is, however, uncertain how much generation gap exists between Kuru/Sudās and King Bharata, as King Bharata is already a legend by Rigvedic age. Bharata is therefore a pre-Vedic king.

There is a gap of two or three generations between Sudās and Somaka. Once the Saudāsas had declined, it is Somaka who revived the dynasty again. The first Kaurava King (who would have succeeded Sudās as the ruler of Madhyadeśa) is likely to have been Parīkṣit I. His successors were Bhīmasena, followed by Pratīpa, followed by Śantanu, who would have been contemporary to Somaka. The Brahma Purana also mentions a few names between Parīkṣit I and Bhīmasena, but these result from a confusion of timelines. [I have written a separate post on this.](https://benjaminindology.wordpress.com/2020/07/22/dating-the-mahabharata-war-a-tale-of-two-janamejayas/)

The king lists of Amāvasu, Turvasu, Anu, and Druhyu in the Brahma Purana are exceedingly short (less than 20 kings each), very poorly documented, and very little can be said about them historically. Later Puranas add a host of tribes under the Ānavas. They divide the Ānavas into two branches. The western branch, founded by Uśīnara, consists of Vṛṣadarbhas, Suvīras, Kaikeyas, Madrakas, Śibis, Yaudheyas, Navarāṣṭra, Kṛmi and Ambaṣṭhas. The eastern Ānava branch is called Bāleya, and it was founded by Uśīnara’s brother Titikṣu. This branch includes Aṅga, Vaṅga, Suhma, Puṇḍra and Kaliṅga.

In the Brahma Purana, however, the Ānavas are a very small and insignificant dynasty. The Uśīnaras and Bāleyas, however, are described in the Brahma Purana as Pauravas descended from from Kakṣeyu, Ṛceyu’s brother. Aṅga’s genealogy (in the BP) ends with Vikarṇa, who is said to be the founder of the Vaikarṇas. Sudās defeated the Vaikarṇas in the Battle of Ten Kings. If these are the same Vaikarṇas, then it may be said that these dynasties were founded in pre-Vedic times. Whether they were originally Pauravas or Anavas can only be speculation on the part of Puranic authors. Despite having pre-Vedic and obscure origins, these polities of the far west and far east continued to play an important role till much later in history.

For the Kasi kings, two alternate genealogies are mentioned. One descends from Anenas, which makes the Kasis a branch descended from Āyu, but separate from that of Yayati. This one has 43 kings (from Manu). Alternatively, the same Kasis are said to descend from Suhotra of Bharatas. This one has 35 kings (from Manu).

Eight key figures are common to both these lists: Dīrghatapas, Dhanvantari, Ketumān, Bhīmaratha, Divodāsa, Pratardana, Vatsa, and Alarka. Alarka is said to have been defeated by the Haiheya King Durdama. These 8 Kasi kings were pre-Vedic to early Rigvedic kings. Apart from these 8, the Kasis do not appear to have been documented very well. Their association to either Anenas or Bharata is purely a result of speculation by Puranic authors.

In the BP, Haihaya tribe is associated with Yādavas, although in the Mahabharata they have also been associated with Śāryatas (Dana Dharma Parva, adhyaya 31). An early king of Haihaya was Bhadraśreṇya, who was defeated by Kasi King Divodasa, but the kingdom was retaken by Bhadraśreṇya’s son (or grandson) Durdama. The Purana says that Divodasa spared Durdama because he was a little boy. Later, Durdama defeated Divodasa’s great grandson Alarka and restored Varanasi to Haihayas.

Kṛtavīrya is another early Haihaya king. His son was Arjuna, an imperialist who was killed by Bhārgava Rāma. He would have lived in the early-middle period of Rigveda (given that he was killed by Bhārgava Rāma). The Puranas say that after Arjuna, the Haihayas split into various branches such as Vītihotras, Suvratas, Bhojas, Avantis, Tauṇḍikeras, Tālajaṅghas, Bharatas and Sujātas.

Let us now turn our attention to the Ikṣvākus. As has been discussed already, ‘Aikṣvāka’ was the designation given to Paurava kings. This designation became common during the late Vedic period of Brahmanas, Upanishads and Aranyakas. Puranic authors came accross several Paurava lineages that were labelled by late Vedic texts and Gathas as ‘Aikṣvāka’.

They couldn’t figure out who these Aikṣvākas were, so they created a chronologically inaccurate collection of these names, and attributed the whole to a separate dynasty. This collection was gradually increased over time. For instance, in the Brahma Purana, there are 58 Ikṣvākus up to Rama. In the Ramayana, however, there are only 34. The number is nearly doubled even in the earliest Purana.

It can be demonstrated decisively that the order of Ikṣvāku kings as mentioned in Puranas is chronologically inaccurate. In the Aitareya Brahmana, section 7.13 it says:

*Harischandra, the son of Vedhas, of the Iksvaku race, was a king who had no son. Though he had a hundred wives, they did not give birth to a son. In his house there lived the Risis, Parvata and Narada.*

In the same text, in section 7.34, it says Parvata and Narada were contemporary to Somaka, and they performed a sacrifice for him. Hence, it naturally implies that Harischandra could not have been much older than Somaka. At the maximum, he could have been 2 or 3 generations older. This would make him a near-contemporary of Sudās. And this makes sense, since Harischandra is referred to in the Brahmanas as a contemporary of Vishvamitra, as Vishvamitra adopted the boy Śunaḥśepa who was about to be sacrificed by Harischandra.

A few generations later, we find a mention of Bhageratha. In the Jaiminiya Upanisad Brahmana, section IV.6, we read that King “Bhageratha Aiksvaka” was about to perform a sacrifice. So the Brahmans of “Kuru-panchala” decided to visit him. To quote:  
  
*IV. 6. 1. King Bhageratha Aiksvaka was about to sacrifice with a wish-fulfilling sacrifice.  
IV. 6. 2. Then the Brahmans of the Kuru-pancalas said : “Verily this king Bhageratha Aiksvaka is about to sacrifice with a wish-fulfilling sacrifice. With him we will have a talk.”*  
*(Jaiminiya Upanisad Brahmana)*

Bhageratha then asked those Brahmanas five questions. One of those Brahmans was… Baka Dalbhya!  
  
*IV. 7. 1. These five questions he \[Bhageratha\] asked of them.  
IV. 7. 2. Of these Kuru-pancalas Baka Dalbhya was learned.*  
*(Jaiminiya Upanisad Brahmana)*

Baka Dalbhya was a contemporary of Dhritarashtra (Vaichitravirya). In the Mahabharata, Chapter 40, Tirtha-yatra parva, a dispute is mentioned between Dhritarashtra and Baka Dalbhya. A similar dispute is also mentioned in Yajurveda, Kathaka Samhita, 10.6! So if Harishchandra was contemporary of Somaka, and Bhageratha was a contemporary of Dhritarashtra, just when exactly was Rama Dasarathi born? Rama was born in the Raghava dynasty, which is a separate dynasty much older than even Hariscandra and Bhageratha.

So just when exactly did Rama Dasarathi reign? It is tempting to connect the eras of Arjuna Kartavirya, Sudās Paijavana and Rama Dasarathi. Arjuna was killed by Bhārgava Rāma, who was son of Jamadagni. Jamadagni was a contemporary of Viśvāmitra, the priest of Sudās. Vasistha was also the priest of Sudās. Vasistha, Viśvāmitra and Bhārgava Rāma are all in turn contemporaries of Rāma Dasarathi. Furthermore, Arjuna defeated Ravana, another contemporary of Rama. This would imply that Arjuna Kartavirya, Sudās Paijavana and Rama Dasarathi were all contemporaries.

For this we can also find an independent corroboration. The Vamsha Brahmana of the Kauthuma Shakha mentions a total of 11 generations of teachers from Rishyashringa (who performed a sacrifice for Dasaratha Raghava) to Indrota Daivāpa Śaunaka, the priest of Janamejaya. And from Sudās Paijavana to Janamejaya is also 11 generations: Sudās(1), 2 intervening generations, Sahadeva(4), Somaka(5), Prsata(6), Drupada(7), Draupadi(8), Abhimanyu(9), Parikshit(10), and Janamejaya(11).

Mandhātar Yauvanaśva was a late Rigvedic king, and according to the Gopatha Brahmana (I.2.10), he was a great conqueror who thrived after the establishment of Kuru, and hence after the fall of Sudās. In the Purana, he is known for defeating Druhyus. The Anus and Druhyus had previously been pushed out of the Punjab region to the Northwest by Sudās. Mandhātar would have further pushed them to the Northwestern-most borders of Aryavarta. Druhyus established Gandhara shortly after their conflict with Mandhātar. In light of all this, it seems highly unlikely that Mandhātar would have been the ancestor of Purukutsa.

Purukutsa was an early Rigvedic king, and a contemporary of Divodāsa (RV VI.18.13). His son was Trasadasyu, who was probably contemporary to Pijavana and Sudās. Two lines descending from Trasadasyu are mentioned in Rigveda. From Trasadasyu descended Trivr̥ṣan, whose son was Tryaruṇa (RV V:27). From Trasadasyu also descended Mitrāthiti, whose son was Kuruśravaṇa, whose son was Upamaśravas (RV X.33). This other branch of Trāsadasyavas possibly assimilated into Kuru, as suggested by the name ‘Kuruśravaṇa’ (‘glory of Kuru’). The above mentioned descendants of Trasadasyu likely reigned in the interval between the fall of Sudās and the rise of Somaka.

Puranic authors have also speculated about the ancestry of Viśvāmitra. Two alternative ancestries are given in the Brahma Purana. In one case, he descends from Amāvasu. In another case, he descends from Ajamīḍha of Bharatas. These are all speculations by Puranic authors. We can only say with confidence that Viśvāmitra was a Kauśika, while Devarāta (Śunaḥśepa) and Aṣṭaka were his sons, and he had some association with Jamadagni. Perhaps Jahnu and Gadhi were his ancestors.

Finally, there is the line of Yādavas descended from Kṛoṣṭā. Most of these names, especially the early ones, cannot be relied upon. Like the Ikshvaku king lists, these are all later reconstructions and are rife with chronological errors. Very likely, king lists from several independent dynasties have been placed together serially, one after another, and the names in the final list do not necessarily appear in the correct chronological order.

For instance, according to Aitereya Brahmana (7.34), Babhru Daivavrddha and Bhima Vaidarbhi would be contemporaries or near contemporaries to Sahadeva and Somaka of the Panchalas. Yet, in the Puranic list, Bhima is an ancestor of Babhru separated by 17 generations. The explanation likely to be correct is that Vrsni, Bhima, and Babhru were contemporaries of Somaka. Vrsni ruled over Mathura, Bhima over Vidarbha and Babhru founded the Bhoja dynasty of Mṛttikāvatī. Mathura was inhabited by Satvat tribe, and Vrsni and Andhaka were two prominent leaders of Satvats around the same time as Somaka. Varsneyas and Andhakas probably had tense relations, and this is reflected a few generations later in the conflict between Vasudeva and Kamsa.

With this, I shall wrap up this post. It seems like I have mostly solved the puzzle of Indian history from Manu to Mahabharata era history, although more research will surely shed more light.
