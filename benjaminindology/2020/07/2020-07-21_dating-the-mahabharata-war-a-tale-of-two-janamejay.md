The Solar Dynasty – Origin of the Ikshvakus

In this post I will deal with the relative dating of Mahabharata war with respect to Vedic texts. The only mention of Kurus in the Vedic hymns is a late Atharvan hymn (AV, xx, 127, 7-10) celebrating the kingship of Parikshit. In contrast, the Kurus figure quite prominently in later Vedic texts such as the Brahmanas. For instance, in the Shatapatha Brahmana, XIII.5.4, we read that Indrota Daivāpa Śaunaka performed a sacrifice for Janamejaya Pārikṣita. Janamejaya’s capital is said to be Āsandīvat (modern-day Assandh in Haryana). Indrota also sacrificed for three brothers of Janamejaya – Bhīmasena, Ugrasena and Śrutasena.

The Mahabharata mentions the same three brothers of Janamejaya in Poushya Parva. This is the same Janamejaya who performed a snake-sacrifice, who was son of Parikshit who succeeded Yudhishthira. Since Shatapatha Brahmana apparently mentions the same Janamejaya, does this mean the Brahmana was composed after the Mahabharata war? The case is not so simple to make. Apparently, there is also another earlier Janamejaya, who was also the son of Parikshit, and who also had brothers who were similarly named. They were Kakshasena, Ugrasena, Chitrasena, Indrasena, Sushena and Bhimasena.

Some, including Pargiter, insist that the Janamejaya of the Brahmanas must be the earlier Janamejaya. The traditionalists would certainly agree. After all, the entire Shruti corpus – along with the Mahabharata and 18 Puranas – were compiled by Vyasa. So how could the later Janamejaya be mentioned in the Brahmanas (Shruti), that too in the past tense? But this traditionalist assumption is precisely what we must challenge in order to arrive at the truth.

In the Sambhava Parva, Vaishampayana narrates the genealogy of the Kurus to Janamejaya. He does so twice, at the insistence of Janamejaya. Not only do the two genealogies differ from one another, they differ even more significantly from the Puranic accounts. The second recitation makes no reference to an earlier pre-war Janamejaya who was also son of Parikshit. The first recitation does mention Janamejaya as a son of an earlier Parikshit, but in a very awkward manner. We read:

> Abhishyanta \[son of Kuru\] begot Parikshit, the powerful Shabalashva,
> Abhiraja, Viraja, the immensely strong Shalmali, Uchchaishrava,
> Bhadrakara and Jitari as the eighth. Seven more immensely strong sons
> were born in this lineage—Janamejaya and the others. They were learned
> and famous for the qualities of their deeds. Parikshit had sons who
> were learned in dharma and artha— Kakshasena, Ugrasena, the immensely
> valorous Chitrasena, Indrasena, Sushena and Bhimasena. Janamejaya’s
> sons were famous on earth…
>
> Sambhava Parva, Mahabharata

The account then continues with Janamejaya’s lineage. From the above, it is not immediately clear if Janamejaya is one of Parikshit’s sons. But if you count the number of sons of Parikshit mentioned (i.e. six), and tally it with the preceding statement (that 7 more sons were born in the lineage), then one may infer that Janamejaya is Parikshit’s son. This seems like a very confusing way of saying that Parikshit had seven sons. Why are they not all listed in the same line? This seems to be a case of interpolation.

The preceding line – (Seven more immensely strong sons… famous for the qualities of their deeds) – was probably absent in the original text. The next line, which only lists six sons of Parikshit, was part of the original text, and its intent was to communicate that Parikshit had only six sons, and Janamejaya was not among them. Then, at a later date, the bards decided that Janamejaya had to be introduced as a son of an earlier Parikshit. But inserting a seventh son in the same line might have disrupted the poetic meter. So, they added another line above it, claming that *seven* more sons were born in the lineage, Janamejaya & others. In this way, the six sons listed in the next line could be interpreted as siblings of Janamejaya, even though the text says no such thing in a clear manner.

The account then continues with the lineage of Janamejaya, listing out his sons and grandsons. Interestingly, the genealogy seems to break at this point. The account begins to recount the lineage of Pratipa, without mentioning who Pratipa is, or how he is related to Janamejaya or his sons, or his grandsons!

This break, in my view, is further evidence of the interpolation. As the second recitation of Vaishampayana would tell us, Pratipa was the son (or descendant) of Bhimasena, one of the six sons of Parikshit. And it is Bhimasena and his descendants who succeed Parikshit. So whoever this earlier Janamejaya was (or wasn’t), he definitely did not succeed Parikshit. On the other hand, the Vedic Janamejaya is obviously the successor of Parikshit. The original text probably made no mention of Janamejaya or his lineage. Instead, it would have mentioned Bhimasena’s lineage, which in the current text appears abruptly after Janamejaya’s sons and grandsons, and begins directly with Pratipa.

But a question naturally arises – what could have been the motivation behind such an interpolation? The Brahmanas were compiled as commentaries to help make sense of the hymns. With the passage of time, however, the Brahmanas, Aranyakas and Upanishads came to be accepted as “Sruti”, and a belief developed that the entire corpus of Sruti, including the Brahmanas, Aranyakas and Upanishads, were compiled by Vyasa. Hence, according to the later consensus, the Janamejaya that came after Vyasa could not have been the Janamejaya mentioned in the Brahmanas. Therefore, the bards postulated the existence of another earlier Janamejaya, and interpolated him into the epic.

From the above discussion, we may now more comfortably date the Mahabharata war with respect to Vedic texts. The rise of Kuru power took place in the latter half of the period of composition of Rigvedic hymns, while the Sama, Yajur and latest parts of Rigveda were completed only a couple of generations before the Bharata war. In fact, the Kathaka Samhita of Yajurveda mentions Dhritarashtra, son of Vichitravirya. And there is a mention of a legend about Shantanu and Devapi in the Xth mandala of Rigveda.

Atharvaveda was also likely to have been completed around this time. There is a mention of Parikshit in a late Atharvan hymn (AV, xx, 127, 7-10), and I do believe this is the later Parikshit who succeeds Yudhishthira. However, the verse belongs to Book 20 which is held to be a much later addition to the Atharvan hymns. On the other hand, later Vedic texts such as Brahmanas, Aranyakas and Upanishads were most likely composed long after the Bharata war. In fact, even Janamejaya had become a legend by that time.

There can be no doubt that Mahabharata has undergone serious revisionism throughout history. To get a glimpse of the extent of this revisionism, one may consider the Vaishnava theology for instance, which is given much importance in both Mahabharata and Ramayana, but is nowhere even hinted at in the Vedas, Vedantas, and even the Srauta and Grihya sutras. If the events of Mahabharata and Ramayana were contemporary to (and even preceded) some portions of the Vedas, then it is clear that they lived in a world that was culturally and politically a lot different from the world portrayed in the current versions of those epics.
