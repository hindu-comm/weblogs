Dating the Mahabharata War – A Tale of Two Janamejayas

From Raja Harishchandra, to Shri Rama to Buddha, the Solar dynasty (*Sūryavaṁśa*) has produced many prominent cultural icons of Indic civilization. But what are its origins? According to most Puranas, the dynasty was started by Ikshvaku, the eldest son of Manu, who was the son of the Sun-god Vivasvant. Hence it is known as the Solar dynasty, and is contrasted with the Lunar dynasty supposedly born from the Moon-god Soma. But is this narrative historically accurate?

As with anything about Indic civilization, if we want to know its roots, we must first look for it in the Rigveda. There is only one mention of Ikṣvāku in Rigveda (X.60), and he is described as residing in heaven, radiant and possessing a great wealth of grains. There seems to be no indication that ‘Ikṣvāku’ in Rigveda refers to the name of a king or a tribe. It is more likely used as a synonym for the Sun itself. Knowing this, it does not come as a surprise that the Puranic authors decided to name the founder of Solar dynasty as ‘Ikṣvāku’.

While the Rigveda does not use ‘Ikṣvāku’ in the sense of a tribal identity, it has its own version of the Solar dynasty. Manu, the first man to install Agni for sacrifice (RV I.13.4), and the first ancestor of the Ārya clans (RV I.68.7), was the son of the Sun-god Vivasvant (RV VIII.52.1). He obtained progeny by means of sacrifice (RV I.96.2). Iḍā is the goddess of sacrifice-personified. She was Manu’s instructor in performing sacrifices. (RV X.110.8).

At this early Rigvedic stage, there is no evidence of Iḍā having a dual gender, or having any relation with Budha (son of Soma). Instead, she helped Manu in performing sacrifices and thereby attaining a son, Purūravas. Because he was born from sacrifice, Iḍā was considered as his mother and hence he was known by the matronym Aiḍā.

Āyu was the son of Purūravas from Urvaśī (RV X.95.10), and Nahuṣa was his son (RV I.31.11). Thus, the Āryas are known as the clans descended from Āyu and from Nahuṣa (RV I.58.3, I.100.16, I.117.25, V.12.6, V.73.3, VI.46.7, VIII.6.24, IX.88.2, IX.91.2). The son of Nahuṣa was Yayāti, and like Manu, he was the ancestor of Āryas (RV X.63.1). Five clans descended from Yayāti (RV VIII.9.2) – Yadu, Turvaśa, Druhyu, Anu, and Pūru (RV I.108.8).

We notice that according to the Rigveda, all the five Arya clans descended from the Sun-god, and are therefore ‘Solar clans’. However, Pūru became the most powerful among them all, and later Vedic texts were composed in the realm of Kurus (a branch of Pūru). Therefore, Yayāti was considered as the universal monarch of his time, and Pūrus were held to be the legitimate successors of Yayāti.

For this reason, especially in later Vedic texts, the word ‘Ikṣvāku’ (a synonym for Sun) increasingly began to be used as a synonym for Pūru. A branch of these Pūru/Ikṣvāku migrated eastward and established Kosala-Videha. In later Puranic usage, the word ‘Ikṣvāku’ was reserved for the eastern-most branch of Pūrus. Over time, however, historians forgot that the (eastern) Ikṣvākus had originated from Pūrus, and therefore ascribed a different origin to the two.

To give evidence for this hypothesis, I will first turn to Rigveda. The dynasty of Purukutsa, which is regarded as ‘Ikṣvāku’ in both the Puranas and the later Vedic texts (Satapatha Brahmana, 13.5.4.5), is considered as a branch of Pūru in many verses of the Rigveda (I.63.7, VII.19.3, VI.20.10, IV.38.1, I.59.6, I.130.7). This is best illustrated by IV.38.1, where Trasadasyu, the son of Purukutsa (IV.42.9), is quite clearly indicated to be a Pūru king:

*And since the earlier gifts were from you two \[Mitra and Varuṇa\], the ones that Trasadasyu was to send spilling down for the Pūrus, you have (also) given the one \[horse\] who wins dwelling places, who wins meadows, a bane for the Dasyus, overwhelming, powerful.*

In the above verse, it is clear that Trasadasyu is a Pūru king, because he has won wealth for the Pūrus by raiding other tribes. Let us now turn our attention to the origin of Kosala and Videha according to Shatapatha Brahmana (1.4.1.14-17):

*Videgha Māthava was at that time on the (river) Sarasvatī. He (Agni) thence went burning along this earth towards the east; and Gotama Rāhūgaṇa and the Videgha Māthava followed after him as he was burning along. He burnt over all these rivers. Now that (river), which is called ‘Sadānīrā,’ flows from the northern (Himālaya) mountain: that one he did not burn over. \[…\] Videgha Māthava then said (to Agni), ‘Where am I to abide?’ ‘To the east of this (river) be thy abode!’ said he. Even now this (river) forms the boundary of the Kosalas and Videhas; for these are the Māthavas (or descendants of Māthava).*

From the above reference it should be clear that the Kosalas and Videhas (who are regarded as ‘Ikṣvāku’) have descended from Videgha Māthava, a king from the Sarasvatī valley. This represents the clearest evidence that Māthava was a Pūru, because the Sarasvatī valley in Vedic times belonged to the Pūrus.

The final piece of evidence, however, is going to be most convincing. From the Rigveda, we know that Bharatas were a branch of Pūru. The most prominent Bharata faction in the Rigveda is the one led by Sudās Paijavana, who, with the priestly help of Vasiṣṭha, won the Battle of Ten Kings (RV VII.18). I have found a reference in Jaiminiya Brahmana, III. 23, where Sudās Paijavana is called an ‘Ikṣvāku’ king, with Vasiṣṭha as Purohita. This reference should clear up all doubts regarding the origin of Iskṣvākus: they were none other than the Pūrus.
