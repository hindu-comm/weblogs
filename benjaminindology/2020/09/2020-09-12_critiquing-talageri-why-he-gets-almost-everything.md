Layers of Interpolation in Mahabharata Epic

In this post, I will attempt a brutal, honest, factual critique of Shrikant Talageri, who is unarguably one of the most important authors in the field of Indology. Despite making important contributions, he makes fatal errors in his reasoning that lead to faulty conclusions.

In Chapter 3 of his book “Rig Veda A Historical Analysis”, we read the following statement:

***The Rigveda consists of ten MaNDalas or Books. And, excepting likely interpolations, these MaNDalas represent different epochs of history.***

Unfortunately, instead of giving actual evidence in favor of this hypothesis, Talageri opts for a selective reading of both the Rigveda and the Anukramani in order to support his absurd thesis. At the very outset, this statement seems illogical. If each mandala is written by a particular rishi family, and each mandala also corresponds to an ‘epoch’ of history, then it would imply that each ‘epoch’ was dominated by one and only one rishi family. It would imply that in each epoch, one rishi family held a near monopoly on composing Rigvedic hymns. At the very outset, this doesn’t seem very likely.

Further, we read:

***The chronological position of MaNDala IX is equally beyond doubt: it is definitely much earlier than MaNDala X, but equally definitely later than the other eight MaNDalas.  
MaNDala IX was meant to be a kind of appendix in which hymns to Soma, ascribed to RSis belonging to all the ten families, were brought together.***

Notice how these two statements contradict each other. If the IXth mandala consists of hymns composed by all the ten families, and each family composed hymns during a specific ‘epoch’, then it implies that IXth mandala should contain hymns from all the epochs. So while it may be true that the IXth mandala was organized at a later date, some of its hymns could be much earlier. By no means can IXth mandala be considered a ‘late’ mandala.

Further, we read:

***The family MaNDalas can be divided into Early Family MaNDalas (VI, III, VII) and Later Family MaNDalas (IV, II, V). The Later Family MaNDalas have full hymns composed by direct descendants of RSis from the Early Family MaNDalas.***

The reader is left to presume that the second statement is supposed to be ‘evidence’ in favor of the the first statement. Unfortunately, the evidence is very flimsy. The fourth mandala is attributed to Vamadeva Gautama, and he bears no known relationship to the authors of mandalas VI (Bharadvaja), III (Visvamitra) or VII (Vasistha). However, there is one hymn in mandala IV, hymn 43, which is ascribed to the rishis Purumīḷha Sauhotra and Ajamīḷha Sauhotra. Supposedly, they descend from Suhotra Bharadvaja, and this is supposed to be ‘evidence’ that the entire IVth mandala is later than the VIth! But even if we accept the premises, the only thing we can safely conclude is that this one particular hymn is later to (most hymns of) VIth mandala. Just because one hymn in the IVth mandala was composed by a descendant of rishi Bharadvaja doesn’t mean the entire mandala is later than VIth. This unfounded conclusion could only be based on his unfounded hypothesis that each mandala is supposed to be an ‘epoch’!

He futher presents a series of similar arguments in which he uses the Anukramani to “logically deduce” the relative chronology of mandalas. I do not need to demonstrate in detail that all of those arguments are based on equally unfounded and flimsy premises. Let me just point out how he has selectively employed the Anukramani data. In mandala I, there are several hymns (166-191) attributed to Agastya Maitrāvaruṇi. As we know from hymn VII.33, Agastya was a contemporary of Vasistha, and was regarded as his brother. In fact, it was Agastya (according to VII.33), who introduced Vasistha to Sudas. This would imply that major portions of mandala I actually belong to the earliest period. But Talageri asserts that even the earliest parts of mandala I are later than VII. This is a serious contradiction, because according to Talageri, mandalas represent ‘epochs’ and therefore Vasistha and Agastya must be separated by ‘epochs’ (which can’t be true).

I can give another example. Hymns VIII.101, IX.62, IX.65, X.110, X.167 are attributed to rishi Jamadagni. Jamadagni was a contemporary of Visvamitra, and we know this from hymn III.53, where Visvamitra attributes his poetic skill (symbolized by Sasarparī) to Palastis and Jamadagnis. However, Talageri places mandalas VIII, XI, and X to be later than mandala III.

One may raise a point in Talageri’s defense. It could be that the Agastya of mandala I was a later Agastya. Likewise, the Jamadagnis of mandalas III, VIII, IX and X could all be different personalities belonging to different epochs. But this would open a can of worms. How, then, would we know that the hymns of mandala VI that are attributed to Bharadvaja were composed by the same Bharadvaja? In fact, we don’t. In all likelihood, the mandala VI was composed by many generations of Bharadvajas, and not all of their personal names were preserved in the Anukramani. This would mean that each family mandala would have early, middle and late portions, that would be roughly concurrent with the early, middle and late portions of other family mandalas. In other words, rishi families were (in some sense) “competitors” in a market rather than monopolists of their respective epochs.

Talageri himself notices some inconsistencies in his narrative. As we further read,

***Trasadasyu is clearly the most important of these Kings, and he and Purukutsa belong to the same period (since the reference in IV.42.8-9 makes it clear that Purukutsa is the actual father, and not some remote ancestor, of Trasadasyu). And equally clearly, this period is the late period:***  
***a. Trasadasyu’s name occurs the greatest number of times in MaNDala VIII (as DivodAsa’s name does in MaNDala VI, and SudAs’ in MaNDala VII).  
b. Trasadasyu’s son (referred to only as TrAsadasyava) also clearly belongs to the period of MaNDala VIII.  
c. Trasadasyu is referred to as a patron, and therefore a contemporary, only in MaNDalas V and VIII (V.27.3; 33.8; VIII.19.32, 36).***

***And yet, we find four references to Purukutsa and Trasadasyu in the older MaNDalas (VI.20.10; VII.19.3; IV.38.1; 42.8-9), and one in the middle upa-maNDalas (I.63.7). This raises a piquant question: is there something wrong with our chronology of the Rigveda, or is there something incongruous about these five references in the older MaNDalas?***

Yes, it does appear that there is something seriously wrong with Talageri’s chronology of Rigveda. Because clearly, there are portions of mandala VIII that were composed concurrently with portions of mandalas VI, VII and IV. However, to defend his irrational hypothesis, Talageri proposes what can only be called a conspiracy theory, because it is not at all parsimonious with the given evidence. Talageri asserts:

***And, on examination, we get the following picture: the five references in the older MaNDalas and upa-maNDalas are laudatory and even adulatory references to Purukutsa and Trasadasyu. Purukutsa and Trasadasyu, although they were not even Vedic Aryans (as we shall see in our chapter on the identity of the Vedic Aryans) are accorded the highest praise in the Rigveda; and this high praise is on account of the fact that they were responsible for the victory, perhaps the very survival as a nation, of the PUrus (who were the Vedic Aryans) in a vital struggle between the PUrus and their enemies which must have taken place during the period of the Late MaNDalas.***

***As a result, the extremely grateful RSis belonging to the families intimately connected with the Bharatas (namely, the ANgirases of both the BharadvAja and Gotama groups, and the VasiSThas) recorded their tribute to Purukutsa and Trasadasyu in the form of verses. The case of Purukutsa and Trasadasyu was clearly such a special one in the eyes of these RSis that in their case, and only in their case in the whole of the Rigveda, they made a point of breaking with orthodox tradition and interpolating these verses in their praise into the older MaNDalas and upa-maNDalas connected with their families.***

There is no good enough (parsimonious) reason to presuppose that references to Purukutsa and Trasadasyu in mandalas IV, VI and VII are interpolations. This conspiracy theory would have been ridiculous enough on its own without the assertion that Purukutsa and Trasadasyu were not “Vedic Aryans”. But that topic deserves a separate post. I will probably have to write my full critique of Talageri is several parts. For now, let us focus on what “evidence” Talageri has for his assertion that Trasadasyu and Purukutsa helped the Purus in a “life and death” struggle.

We read:  
***IV.38.1, likewise, thanks Mitra and VaruNa for the services which Trasadasyu, “the winner of our fields and plough-lands, and the strong smiter who subdued the Dasyus”, rendered to the PUrus.***

The verse in question calls Trasadasyu a “bane for Dasyus” and also asserts that he won “gifts” for the Purus. But there is no evidence that this was a “life and death” struggle, nor is there any good reason to presume that Trasadasyu couldn’t have been a Puru king himself. Apparently, Puru was a huge confederation that included many independent branches. The Bharatas were one such branch (and even they had many sub-branches). This becomes apparent in the very name of Purukutsa, which most likely means “the thunderbolt (kutsa) of the Purus”.

\[Note: While it is true that Purukutsa was called an Ikshvaku in (post-Samhita) late Vedic texts (but NOT in Rigveda), it so happens that Talageri’s favorite Bharata hero Sudas is also identified as Ikshvaku king in the Jaiminiya Brahmana. So it is not entirely clear that the [late Vedic meaning of Ikshvaku](https://benjaminindology.wordpress.com/2020/07/25/the-solar-dynasty-origin-of-the-ikshvakus/) was the same as the Pauranic meaning. In any case, there is no parsimonious evidence from Rigveda that rules out the possibility that Purukutsa and Trasadasyu were Purus.\]

Talageri fruther cites VI.20.10, in which the Purus praise Purukutsa. This is easily explained by what has been discussed above. He cites I.63.7 which refers to the help rendered by Indra to the Purus, and gives Purukutsa and Sudas as examples. Once again, none of this is evidence of a life and death struggle. A more natural, parsimonious interpretation would be that Purukutsa and Sudas were fellow Purus. Further, Talageri says:

***VII.19.3 refers to Indra helping the PUrus “in winning land and slaying foemen”, once by way of Trasadasyu Paurukutsa and once by way of SudAs.***

Precisely. The two refer to different events in which Indra helped two different Purus. At any rate, it is much easier to show that Trasadasyu and Purukutsa were actually Purus, and that some portions of mandala VIII were composed concurrently with portions of mandalas VI, VII and IV. In the end, Talageri presents no convincing arguments in favor of his “interpolation” hypothesis. And his very motivation for proposing this “interpolation” hypothesis was to support the equally unfounded “epoch” hypothesis.
