The Solar Dynasty – Origin of the Ikshvakus

Kuru-Panchāla is a very important janapada in late Vedic texts such as Brahmanas, Aryanyakas and Upanisads. The Kuru-Pancālas are said to be in the middle region (Aitereya-brāhmaṇa, VIII.14), to have the best form of speech (Śatapatha-brāhmaṇa, III.2.3.15) and their method of sacrifice is given preference over others (SB I.7.2.8).

Despite the importance accorded to them in later texts, there is no mention of the term in early Vedic texts, i.e., the Samhita hymns of Rig, Yajur, Sama and Atharva. Only one Atharva hymn (xx, 127, 7-10) eulogizes the rule of Parikshit, explicitly referring to him as a Kaurava king. Other than that, there is a mention of Samtanu and Devapi in the Rigveda, as well as a reference to Dhritarashtra Vaichitravirya in Yajurveda, but in neither of these references are they associated with Kuru.

It may be noted that the above references to Kuru are possibly later additions. Book 20 of Atharva hymns and Book 10 of Rig hymns are regarded as late. Dhritarashtra is also mentioned only in one śākhā (Kāṭhaka) of Yajur but not in others.

There is a historic reason for the near absence of Kuru-Panchālas in the early Vedic texts. While the groups that would later become known as Kuru and Panchāla certainly existed from the earliest Vedic period, they weren’t called by those names. They were called Bhāratas, and the branch of Bhāratas that were more prominent in Rigvedic period were the ones that ended up becoming Panchāla, while the Kurus (whose origins are rather obscure in Vedic literature) ended up becoming more prominent.

The biggest puzzle in Vedic history is the rise of Kurus: when and how did it happen. Because while the word ‘Kuru’ (as a people/polity) is absent in the Samhitas, it is the very center stage in later Vedic texts, and Vedic texts shed little light on when and how this transition happened.

The famous Battle of Ten Kings in the Rigveda established the supremacy of Sudās, a Bharata/Pūru king whose descendants went on to rule over Panchāla. In that Battle, Sudās is said to have defeated pretty much every other known Vedic tribe, including a rival faction of Bharata/Pūru.

The Mahābhārata remembers this event from the eyes of the defeated branch of Bharatas, who went on to become the Kurus. In the Sambhava Parva, we read:

*It has been heard that when Riksha’s son Samvarana ruled the earth as king, there was a great disaster that led to the destruction of subjects. \[…\] The Panchalas soon conquered the entire earth and defeated them in battle with ten akshouhinis of soldiers. In great fear, King Samvarana fled with his wife, sons, advisers and friends. He then found shelter in the forests on the banks of the river Sindhu, where the river extends up to the mountains. One day, the illustrious rishi Vashishtha went there to the Bharatas \[…\] and the king himself then requested him to be their priest, so that they might regain their kingdom. Vashishtha agreed to this by saying “Om” to the Bharatas. We have heard that he instated the Pourava as the emperor of all the Kshatriyas, like the tusk of the entire expansive earth. \[…\] Through Tapati, the daughter of Surya, Samvarana had a son named Kuru.*

Incidentally, Vasiṣṭha was the priest of Sudās, and helped Sudās secure the aid of Indra during the Battle of Ten Kings. The alliance of Vasiṣṭha and Sudās was called “Trtsu” and it is greatly praised in the Rigveda. However, the Mahābhārata insists that Vasiṣṭha actually helped the rivals reclaim their kingdom. But why would Vasiṣṭha switch sides? Interesting clues are found in Vedic texts.

The Jaiminiya Brahmana (II, 390) mentions how the sons of Sudās (Saudāsas) threw Śakti, the son of Vasiṣṭha, into fire. Other texts mention how they also killed other sons of Vasiṣṭha. The Taittiriya Samhita (7.4.7) reads: *Vasistha, his sons slain, desired, ‘May I win offspring, and defeat the Saudasas.’ He saw this (rite) of forty-nine nights; he grasped it, and sacrificed with it. Then indeed did he win offspring and defeated the Saudasas.*

So we know from Vedic texts that Vasiṣṭha was plotting revenge against Saudasas. Ultimately, he took recourse to the “puny Bharatas” (RV 7.33.6), also called “Pūru of scornful speech” (RV 7.18.13), who were defeated by Sudās. With Vasiṣṭha on their side, the “puny” Bharatas became more and more powerful, defeated the Saudasas, and became established as Kurus. In this way, the rise of Kuru power is a story of double revenge – that of Samvarana and Vasiṣṭha.

The descendants of Sudās allied with several other minor tribes, including Turvasu, to form Panchāla (SB XIII.5.4.16). This may have reflected in the Puranic tale according to which Dushyanta (Bharata’s father) was adopted by Turvasus by which the Turvasus merged into the Bharatas. Dushyanta lived long before their merger, but the Puranic lore remembers this merger rather inaccurately, in the form of the adoption of Dushyanta.

The assault of Saudasas upon Vasiṣṭha’s sons gave rise to some interesting myths. Bṛhaddevatā, the commentary on Rigveda, says that either Sudās or Saudasas killed Vasiṣṭha’s sons because he had become a Rākṣas due to a curse. Does this sound familiar?

This is exactly the Puranic story of Mitrasaha, also known as Saudasa, who became a cannibalistic Rākṣas on account of a curse, and thereby ended up devouring Vasiṣṭha’s sons. In Puranas, however, Saudasa is **not** the descendant of Sudās Paijavana, the king who won the Battle of Ten Kings. Instead, the Puranic Saudasa belongs to the Ikṣvāku dynasty.

This confusion may have been brought about by the fact that the later Vedic texts referred to Sudās Paijavana as an Ikṣvāku king. I have shed more light on this in my [previous post](https://benjaminindology.wordpress.com/2020/07/25/the-solar-dynasty-origin-of-the-ikshvakus/).
