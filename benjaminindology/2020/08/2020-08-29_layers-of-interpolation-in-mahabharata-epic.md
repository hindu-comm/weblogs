Origin of Kurus – A Tale of Double Revenge

This post will mostly attempt to summarize the research of Madhukar RamravYardi of BORI. The Mahabharata mentions that it is an expansion of an earlier work, the Bharata Samhita, of 24,000 verses. The current Mahabharata, including the Harivamsha, adds up to about 100,000 verses. Yardi attempts to extract the original 24,000 verses narrated by Vaishampayana to Janamejaya. (See the end of the article for my revised take on this). He uses a statistical method to do so.

Yardi’s method is based on the fact that the vast majority of the epic is composed in anushtubh meter, which is very flexible. Yardi began with the hypothesis that every author had his own unique ‘style’ which reflected itself in the unconscious use of long or short syllables in places where the meter did not restrict the use of long or short syllables.

He began by dividing the epic into small groups of adhyayas. Each group dealt with a particular topic or event. He then subjected each group to a statistical analysis of the variance in the use of long or short syllables. If the variation between two groups of adhyayas was found to be statistically significant (i.e., could not be merely by chance), then the two groups were said to have a different ‘style’.

Yardi deduced that the Mahabharata consists of five distinct styles. Yardi considered the possibility that the same author could have undergone a change in his style as time went by. \[After all, the epic is said to have been composed in three years\]. However, he ruled out the possibility. These five styles are fairly well distributed in every Parva, so that it couldn’t be argued that these styles resulted from the change in style over time.

Yardi also noticed that none of these style correlate with the nature of the content (narration of events, dialogues, philosophical dialogues, etc are all found in each different style). Yardi therefore concluded that each of these styles represented a different author. One limitation of this method is that it can only detect changes in authorship across groups of adhyayas. It cannot detect mirco-interpolations of one or two verse here and there.

In the Adi Parva, adhyayas 1-3 are written by a distinct style which is rarely seen hereafter in the epic. It seems that the only purpose of this author was to add the Vyasa-Ganesha episode, and provide an index to the epic. Yardi therefore calls him ‘Parvasangrahakara’ (or the author of Parvasangraha, the index). Parvasangrahakara also added adhyayas 106-110, 135 of Anushasana Parva.

Adhyayas 4-54 are written in another distinct style. By their own admission, these verses were added by Sauti, who at the request of Kulapati Shaunaka recites a number of different episodes before getting to Vaishampayana’s composition.

Vaishampayana’s composition begins at Adhyaya 55. From this adhyaya onwards, you will not see any reference being made to Sauti (at least not until the very end). Adhyaya 55 in fact begins like an independent composition, where Vaishampayana first pays respects to his guru Sri Vyasa, and then begins by exclaiming that he is reciting the Mahabharata, and then finally begins his recitation by first giving a brief account of the events that led to the war, and then a brief genealogy beginning from King Uparichara, in order to explain the origins of the main characters. Vaishampayana style continues till adhyaya 57.

For the rest of Adi Parva (58-226), the recitation returns to the style of Sauti (without, however, Sauti making this fact explicit). However, there are certain groups of adhyayas within 58-226 that are in yet another style. According to Yardi, this style of adhyayas were added before even Sauti’s additions, and this style is frequently found throughout the epic, interlaced in Sauti’s narrations.

In other words, Sauti appears to have incorporated slokas from this earlier interpolator into his interpolation. Yardi, therefore, calls him Suta (Sauti’s father), who is also mentioned in the Mahabharata as a knower of history and a great storyteller. The adhyayas of Suta’s style in Adi Parva are 121-127, 128-138, 139-143, 144-156, 157, 158-164, 165-173, 174, 175-191, 192-199.

The entire Sabha Parva is the joint composition of Suta and Sauti. Aranyaka Parva is also mostly authored by Suta and Sauti. However, certain adhyayas of Aranyaka Parva appear to be in yet another distinct style (13-90, 141-178, 184-206, 258-276). This style is the same as that of the author of Harivamsha. Yardi, therefore, calls him ‘Harivamshakara’. The author of Harivamsha hereafter appears from the time to time to contribute adhyayas in between Suta and Sauti’s adhyayas. Virata Parva is also written by Suta and Sauti. Suta also composed first 21 adhyayas of Udyoga.

Vaishampayana style again begins (from where it left off in Adi 57) at adhyaya 22 of Udyoga. Adhyayas 22-50, 145-148 were composed by Vaishampayana. The rest by Suta and Sauti.

Bhishma, Drona, Karna, Shalya, and Sauptika Parvas are the so-called war Parvas. The adhyayas written by Vaishampayana only focus on describing the war activities. Even in these Parvas, we find interpolations by Sauti and Harivamshakara. Most of the interpolations consist of philosophical discourses, tangential episodes, description of tirthas and other detours from the main war narration.

**Incidentally, the Bhagavata Gita is written entirely in the same style, and the author appears to be Sauti.** This point will be discussed in more detail later on. Interestingly, Karna Parva is the only Parva with no interpolations. It is authored entirely by Vaishampayana. The account of Balarama’s pilgrimage was added by Harivamshakara.

Stri Parva is authored entirely by Suta. In the Shanti Parva, adhyayas, 59, 135-151 were part of Vaishampayana composition. The rest of it, including much of the discourse on Dharma was added by Sauti. In the Anushasana Parva, Vaishampayana has composed adhyayas 1-27, 125-34, 154. The rest of Anushasana is composed largely by Sauti.

Vaishampayana’s narration actually ends with adhyaya 154 of Anushasana. It ends with Bhishma on his death bed, giving these final words of advice to Yudhishthira: “O lord of men! Brahmanas, especially wise ones, preceptors and officiating priests, should always be worshipped by you.”’

Ashvamedhika Parva was written jointly by Suta and Sauti, whereas the rest of the epic (Ashramavasika, Mausala, Mahaprasthanika & Svargarohana) is authored by Suta. Overall, Suta attempts to add more graphic details to Vaishampayana’s narration, based on the short summary of Vaishampayana. On the other hand, Sauti takes up an even more ambitious task: He wants to turn a book of Itihasa (history) into a Dharma Shastra (religious, moral & spiritual treatise).

Sauti framed the entire war as an epic conflict between good and evil (Dharma Yuddha). He added the disrobing of Draupadi, in order to justify the barbaric acts of Bhima (drinking Duhshasana’s blood) and also other instances where the Pandavas break war rules. At many places, Sauti appears as an apologist for the Pandavas. For some reason, Sauti felt that it was necessary to portray the Pandavas as purely good.

Another major change brought about by Sauti is the addition of Bhargava-Yadava religious doctrines into the epic. Yardi notices that the Vaishampayana narration has a Shaiva outlook. The Krishna of Vaishampayana is an ordinary mortal, claims to be no more, and in fact worships and recommends the worship of Shiva.

But a large portion of Sauti’s compostion is dedicated to glorifying the Bhargavas. Sauti is narrating the epic to a Bhargava (Shaunaka). This might indicate that Sauti’s interpolations were made under Bhargava (and hence Yadava) influence. The Bhargava’s philosophical doctrines are incorporated in the Bhagavata Gita and other portions authored by Sauti.

Bhargavas and Yadavas were closely related kin, as is reflected in their origin myths. Bhargavas hailed Vishnu as the highest God, and due to their close connection with Yadavas, incorporated the most cherished Yadava hero Krishna Vasudeva as an Avatara of Vishnu.

According to Yardi, who also did a similar analysis of Ramayana, Sauti also made interpolations in Ramayana whereby Rama is portrayed as an Avatara of Vishnu. Thus, with the biggest amount of material added by Sauti, the Mahabharata text acquired a mostly Vaishnava color, but Vaishnavism appears to be absent in the parts composed by Vaishampayana.

I actually attempted to read in succession the adhyayas supposedly composed by Vaishampayana, only to see if they read like a coherent book on their own. Surprisingly, they do fit into coherent narrative for most part. You wouldn’t even notice that there were adhyayas missing. Despite this, there are a few apparent “jumps” in the Vaishampayana narrative. This could be because Yardi’s method hasn’t singled out individual verses that were either added or deleted.  
  
An after-thought:

I’ve been thinking about Bharata and Mahabharata as separate texts. This is what I think may have happened. Mahabharata was never a 24,000 sloka document. The 24,000 sloka version was Bharata, and it was actually a separate text narrated (or composed) by Jaimini, just like Mahabharata was narrated by Vaishampayana. The prefix “Maha” indicates that already, Mahabharata was larger than Bharata when it was first composed. We don’t know how large. But as of today, the current Mahabharata retains only 21,000 slokas of Vaishampayana.

Yardi takes this to mean that he has somehow “extracted” the Bharata from the Mahabharata, but that can’t be done because the Bharata and Mahabharata are different compositions. On the other hand, Yardi’s statistical method does not rely upon any assumption about how many verse the original Mahabharata may have contained. Therefore, his conclusions cannot be dismissed.

It is possible that Vaishampayana’s original Mahabharata had a shorter description of the pre-war events. But Suta and Sauti decided to elaborate upon those events. In this process, it is possible they may have in fact \*replaced\* many of Vaishampayana’s slokas with their own, much more detailed, descriptions.
