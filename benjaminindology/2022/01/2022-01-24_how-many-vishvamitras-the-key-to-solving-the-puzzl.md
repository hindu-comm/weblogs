Complete Political History of the Vedic Age

The historian Pargiter noted that sages like Vishvamitra seem to have been associated with many Ikshvaku kings that are (supposedly) generations apart. He proposed a theory that there were multiple Vishvamitras. Is Pargiter’s theory of multiple Vishvamitras justified? Before we get into that, we must first ask: Who was the first Vishvamitra, and how is he to be recognized?

The Mahabharata states clearly that Vishvamitra was born in the lineage of King Bharata. He was the son of King Gadhi. Here are some facts about him:  
1) His elder sister Satyavati, was married to sage Richika, and their son Jamadagni was his nephew  
2) Among his chief sons, the Puranas name Shunashepa (adopted as Devarata), Ashtaka and Galava  
3) His son Ashtaka was born of princess Drishadvati

Shunashepa was the adopted son of Vishvamitra, whom Vishvamitra had saved from Harishchandra’s sacrifice. Earlier, the same Vishvamitra also showed favor to Harishchandra’s father Trishanku, who had saved Vishvamitra’s son Galava from being sold.

All this proves that the Vishvamitra associated with Harishchandra and his father Trishanku must have been the first one. Furthermore, the same Galava who was rescued by Trishanku, when he had to pay tuition fee to his teacher/father Vishvamitra, took the help of princess Drishadvati. Drishadvati had a son Vasumanas from the Ikshvaku king Haryashva.

The Vishvamitra who accompanied Rama also stated quite explicitly that Satyavati was his elder sister (married to sage Richika), once again indicating that this was Vishvamitra the first. Vishvamitra’s nephew Jamadagni’s son Parashurama also met Rama, as mentioned in Ramayana. This would indicate that the first Vishvamitra lived in the Ramayana era, and all these Ikshvaku kings – Rama, Harishchandra, Trishanku, and Haryashva were contemporaries.

It comes as no surprise, therefore, when the Harivamsha states that Haryashva married the daughter of King Madhu, whose son Lavana was slain by Rama’s brother Shatrughna. It is quite surprising that this same Lavana had earlier slain Mandhatr, supposedly a remote ancestor of Rama. But Mandhatr’s presence in the Ramayana era is proved by the following.

Jamadagni, the son of sage Richika, married Renuka, the daughter of an Ikshvaku King Prasenajit. This Prasenajit can be none other than the grandfather of Mandhatr. Furthermore, Mandhatr married Bindumati, the granddaughter of King Chitraratha. It is this same Chitraratha who, when he was enjoying with his wives in a pond in Mrittikavati (an important Yadava city), was seen in that state by Renuka, who then had a wicked thought.

Since Jamadagni, being Vishvamitra’s nephew and the father of Parashurama, belongs to the Ramayana era, it follows all other personalities associated with him also belong to the Ramayana era, including his wife Renuka, her father Prasenajit, his grandson Mandhatr, along with Yadava King Chitraratha, whom Renuka saw, and his granddaughter Bindumati, whom Mandhatr espoused.

Lavana’s killing of Mandhatr, therefore, is well grounded in the Ramayana era, and by killing Lavana, Shatrughna does not avenge a remote ancestor, but his own cousin from a different Ikshvaku branch.

Lavana’s father Madhu was the brother-in-law of Ravana. Early in his career, Ravana had slain Rama’s ancestor Anaranya. This Ravana was captured by Sahasrabahu Arjuna, and Arjuna was later slain by Parashurama, the son of Jamadagni.

Parashurama then went on a violent campaign against all Kshatriyas, although he spared some. Rama’s father Dasharatha was one of them. Another contemporary Ikshvaku king Mulaka, born in the line of Rituparṇa Bhāṅgāśvina, saved himself from Parashurama by hiding behind women.

Thus, all these contemporary Ikshvaku kings belonged to different branches of Ikshvakus. The Puranas err in collating all these branches into one big genealogy, but this error is easily corrected by the synchronisms.

The Ikshvaku clan has three principal branches: the Yuvanashva branch, Bhaṅgāśvina branch, and the Purukutsa branch. They go as follows:

Therefore, we may now put to rest Pargiter’s theory of multiple Vishvamitras. While it may be true that a descendant of Vishvamitra could also be called Vishvamitra, when it comes to the Puranas, there is only one prominent Vishvamitra, and he was the first of his name.
