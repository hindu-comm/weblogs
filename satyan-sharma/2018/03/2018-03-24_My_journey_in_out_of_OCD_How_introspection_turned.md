It was the 2nd year of my bachelor of arts degree, when I had my first massive panic attack and was diagnosed with Obsessive Compulsive Disorder. I was 18 years old then. My obsession was with hygiene and security. I used to wash my hands repeatedly and repeatedly check if a door was locked ‘enough’ or not. My hands were full of rashes, hard to touch, and the cuts used to bleed regularly. Before going to my college, I made sure that I applied some oil or a moisturising cream on my hands to make them ‘proper’ and comfortable.

But natural my family was worried. Regarding my obsession for hygiene, my dad used to say, “if all other members of the family can live just healthy enough by not washing their hands repeatedly, you too can lessen the amount of times you wash your hands.” Pretty simple practical demonstration, right? I too understood it, but when it came to practising it, I failed repeatedly. But somewhere deep inside me, I was consciously aware that I was doing something unreasonable.

My family decided to get me a psychiatric treatment. I attended some counselling sessions and took medication. The counsellor once demonstrated to me how to diminish the OCD impulses. She told me to not attend to them, not to fulfil the desire to confirm about hygiene and security, and every next such impulse will be of a lesser force. The diagram she drew was simple, but the application of it, very difficult.

The medication which I was on, slowed down my brain. I used to spontaneously faint/fall asleep for 3–4 seconds and get up to see things fallen down from my hands and guessing what had happened. I was then told to avoid driving any vehicle. The impulses became diminished, but my experience was not good at all. The side effect of the medicine was such that I used to feel as if I was in a dream 24x7.



I was told that the symptoms of OCD, although unnoticeable, could come back, and that I should visit the hospital as soon as I feel so. Gradually, I was off the medication. It took a lot of time before I could feel normally awake. But the impulses used to come back. I was a short tempered person since long, but this time my anger was on another level. In the moment of anger, my head used to feel numb, my hands felt so numb, so painless that I could break a mountain down to pieces. To harm someone physically, wasn’t a difficult job then.

Till then, I was told, as a layman, that anger or any such impulse is caused by the brain, and it’s the brain doing all the stuff. I never felt like I could control my own thoughts. This is why I was given medicine to slow down my brain. But was it all worth it? I casually started to read the sayings of Buddha, specifically those regarding mind. His approach sounded logical because he encouraged to introspect, so that the right causes could be found. When the right cause is found, the right solution can be worked out.

I started to introspect, to find the causes of my impulses (an approach I wasn’t introduced to by my psychiatrist). Very soon I found out that it were my opinions about certain things that made me react in a certain way. Then I started to investigate the validity of my opinions and what I found out was astonishing. I found out that majority of my opinions were not based on facts, instead were based on my personal biases. I saw a gradual, yet a great fall in my emotional (over)reactions. This improvement had to stay, because it had come from within, with understanding and logic.

Since then, I witness my own thoughts, rising and falling, an approach which is based on the idea that mind/self and the brain are not identical and that the former can control the latter. As a scientist would say, “I don’t know how it works, but works pretty well.”

Though after a long time, I realised, that the actual medicine which worked, wasn’t those capsules or the counselling sessions, but the introspection. In the beginning itself, I consciously knew that I was doing something unreasonable, but did not know how to rectify it, because I was a proper physicalist, who believed that apart from matter and energy, nothing else exists. I specifically used to hold, that mind and brain are identical.

But for me, that mind-brain identity model failed. It was only when I viewed mind/self and brain as two distinct entities (where the former acts on the latter), that I gained control over myself. Introspection still continues, as it would even in the future, for there’s nothing better than to guard one’s own self, and to do that, to have a control over one’s thoughts is of utmost importance.
