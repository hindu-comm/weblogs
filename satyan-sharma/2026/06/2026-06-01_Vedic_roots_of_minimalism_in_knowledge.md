Minimalism in knowledge is a view which favours only purpose-oriented practical knowledge.

*Know what is needed, is beneficial, and leave the rest.*

This idea is not new.

See these mantras of Ṛgveda :

*i) saṃ pūṣan viduṣā naya yo añjasānuśāsati* \| *ya evedamiti bravat* \|\| — 6.54.1

O Pūṣan ! lead us to the wise who would instruct us in a direct way. Who would say, “this is it.”

*ii) akṣetravitkṣetravidaṃ hyaprāṭ sa praiti kṣetravidānuśiṣṭaḥ* \|

*etadvai bhadramanuśāsanasyota srutiṃ vindatyañjasīnām* \|\| — 10.32.7



The one who doesn’t know the destination, asks the one who knows it. Having been instructed by the knower of the destination, he goes. This is the benefit of instruction : one attains the direct path.

— — —

This is common between both mantras : purpose-oriented question leading to practical knowledge.

Mantra *i)* states directness of instruction (*añjasā anuśāsati*). Mantra *ii)* states attainment of direct path to the desired destination as a result of instruction (*añjasīnāṃ srutiṃ vindati*).

*Specific purpose → specific knowledge*

Hence there is no space for the unnecessary.

This idea can also be seen in Veda-based texts like Dharmaśāstras.

For example, Mānava Dharmaśāstra speaks against purposeless curiosity :

*na jātu syātkutūhalī* — 4.63

Minimalism in knowledge, i.e., inclination toward receiving purpose-oriented practical knowledge, is smart and Vedic.
