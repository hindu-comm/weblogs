Interpretation of Vidhurādhikaraṇa (Brahmasūtra-Śāṅkarabhāṣya) by Śrī Bharatī Tīrtha Svāmin of…  
2026-11-09  
Source: [here](https://satyan-sharma.medium.com/interpretation-of-vidhur%C4%81dhikara%E1%B9%87a-brahmas%C5%ABtra-%C5%9B%C4%81%E1%B9%85karabh%C4%81%E1%B9%A3ya-by-%C5%9Br%C4%AB-bharat%C4%AB-t%C4%ABrtha-sv%C4%81min-of-084ebffddbec)

The central text of Vedānta is Brahmasūtras, attributed to Maharṣi Bādarāyaṇa. It contains many adhikaraṇas which deal with specific issues, one of which is whether or not those persons who do not belong to any of the four āśramas (brahmacarya, gṛhastha, vānaprastha and saṃnyāsa) are competent to resort to the three means to realisation : śravaṇa, manana and nididhyāsana. In order to effectively resort to these means, one’s mind needs to be pure. Mental purity is said to be effected through the performance of one’s duties and rites as stated by the śāstras. Varṇa and āśrama provide the context, acting as per which, one is said to surely attain mental purity, making one competent for the said means. But what about those who do not belong to any āśrama?

Śaṅkarācārya has commented upon this portion of Brahmasūtras called ‘vidhurādhikaraṇa’ (Brahmasūtras 3.4.36–39). It is called so because it deals with, among others, the vidhuras (widowers) who cannot perform Vedic sacrifices, as the competence to perform them requires a living wife. The position he presents is that although āśramas are the best way to attain such competence, there are alternative means for those who do not belong to any āśrama.

Śrī Bhāratī Tīrtha Svāmin of the Śṛṅgerī Śāradā Pīṭha has presented his interpretation of Śaṅkarācārya’s commentary on the said adhikaraṇa. As is the case with every adhikaraṇa, this too contains the opponent’s view, as per which the widowers and others, who do not belong to any āśrama, and are consequently not competent to perform Vedic sacrifices and other rites, do not have the requisite competence to resort to śravaṇa, manana and nididhyāsana. Svāmin says:

“The opponent summarily rejects the eligibility of widowers and others who cannot perform *Vedic* sacrifices. However, Vyāsa and Bhagavatpāda have taken exception to this extreme position and categorically stated that despite their disqualification to engage in sacrifices, they too can definitely practise certain alternative means such as the repetition of a *mantra*, fasting and the worship of God. As these means do confer mental purity and can be resorted to by all, it is untrue that the path of knowledge and emancipation is ruled out for those ineligible for *Vedic* sacrifices.”

Based on a verse of Manusmṛti (2.87: *japyenaiva tu saṃsidhyet*..) cited by Śaṅkarācārya (on Brahmasūtra 3.4.38), Svāmin says that repeating a *mantra* with faith and steadfastness leads to mental purity, irrespective of whether or not one engages in rites. Hesays:

“When one repeats a *mantra* with faith and steadfastness, one’s mind becomes pure, regardless of whether one can and does engage in rites and such else or not. Becoming pure-minded, such a person decidedly qualifies for taking up the means to knowledge, such as hearing about the Truth. Like the repetition of a *mantra*, fasts and worship of God too are purifiers of the mind and can be universally resorted to and everyone can engage in them on one’sown.”

He further says:

“Nevertheless, as there is no restriction on widowers and such others who do not belong to any of the four *āśramas* to repeat a *mantra*, fast or engage in the worship of God, they too can, through such means, acquire purity of the mind and qualify for the path of knowledge. The scripture does not shut the door upon them; on the contrary, in keeping with one’s competence, it presents every one with spiritual practices by means of which one can cleanse one’s mind and moveahead.”

He mentions the case of Gārgī as ‘an instance met with in the scripture of a person outside the āśramas who became knowledgeable about Brahman’ (p. 44, *Emancipating Enlightenment*). The case of Raikva, who was neither in the brahmacarya āśrama, nor in the gṛhastha āśrama, and knew the truth, leads to a presumption that he must have earlier resorted to such alternative means (p. 45, *Emancipating Enlightenment*).

As per Svāmin’s interpretation, the central point is the ineligibility to perform Vedic sacrifices and other rites. As per him, Manusmṛti, a text of Dharmaśāstra, itself declares that irrespective of whether one can or does perform rites or not, one can attain mental purity by repetition of a mantra with faith and steadfastness. Fasting and worship of Bhagavān also are alternative methods which lead to mental purity. Svāmin says that such methods are widely accessible and everyone can practise them on their own.

In today’s times, when adherence to varṇāśrama-dharmas is gradually declining (even indicated by Śaṅkarācārya himself in his commentary on Brahmasūtra 1.3.33), such information based on the traditional position of śāstras (even if restricted to one sampradāya), sheds light on the methods of mental purity which the aforesaid people today can resort to.
