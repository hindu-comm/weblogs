How to slow down dissemination of knowledge? Request publishers to disseminate it on your behalf. The publisher won’t accept your request right away. Wait for a few months or years for that to happen. Let someone else decide when your work will get released for public view.

Today ample tools are available, for academicians especially, to share one’s work with either one’s peers or public at large, for free or for a fee. Academic websites (*academia*, *researchgate*, etc.) help one share one’s research quickly. Online self-publishing is a great option for books and research articles. Yet, academicians are constantly seeking validation from celebrity publishers, delaying dissemination of their own work.

Of course, academic institutions show allegiance to such publishers and the metrics they set. An academician who desires to make their position firm in an institution is forced to comply. What if the institution declares that it no longer accepts such metrics and labels, and that if the research complies with accepted quality standards, it could be published anywhere, even be self-published? Would an academician still run after such publishers?

It has been firmly accepted that one should not judge the quality of a paper by the perceived quality of a journal \[1\]. It is the same with book publishers. An academician works hard for years trying to solve a problem, and when it is time to share the solution, they have to wait again for months or years to get it published with a reputed journal (or a book publisher). Publishing in it won’t prove the quality of the research by itself. So why chase it so desperately? The author won’t even earn a penny from the sales of their own paper (which would be priced very highly by the journal). And if they want to make it available for free, they themselves have to pay a good amount of money in the name of open access. The original purpose of academic journals was easy access and dissemination of knowledge\[2\]. Has the purpose changed?

I really wonder how did academicians, who apparently think logically, accept all of this? They themselves give away the control in the hands of publishers. The quality of a publication depends on its contents and not the label. May be, academicians today value the label more than the product itself.

Several examples can be shown where low quality content was published by reputed publishers. It appears the case of replacing peer review with poor review. When such content gets a stamp of approval from reputed publishers, the reader assumes its quality. Here are a few examples.

1.  ***Indian Philosophy A Very Short Introduction*. By Sue Hamilton.
    Oxford University Press, 2001**:

The author has committed several errors in this book. One glaring example is on page 73, where the author says that in Vaiśeṣika philosophy, substances like ether, space, self and time have atoms. These substances or dravyas called respectively as ākāśa, dik, ātman and kāla in this philosophy, are categorised as vibhu, i.e., those dravyas which are omnipresent. Atoms, or rather elementary particles, called paramāṇus, are the opposite of vibhu. Only the substances called earth, water, fire, air and mind (pṛthivī, ap, tejas, vāyu, manas respectively) are atomic. These simple facts can be so easily found in any primary text of Vaiśeṣika philosophy, that there remains no room for such an error.

**2. The Metaphysics of Self in *Praśastapāda*. By Shalini Sinha. *In The Oxford Handbook of Indian Philosophy*. Ed. Jonardon Ganeri. Oxford University Press, 2018**.

On page 294, Sinha makes this statement:

“The self, Praśastapāda asserts, is itself the highest value; it is the good (*niḥśreyasa*) to be achieved as the aim of human life, a sentiment first expressed in the *Vaiśeṣika*-*sūtra*.”

For the claim that ‘Praśastapāda asserts the self to be the highest value, the good (*niḥśreyasa*), which is to be achieved as the aim of human life’, Sinha gives the following evidence in endnote number 31:

Śrīdhara, *Shoots of Reason*, ed. Dvivedin, p. 70, line 18.

The details of this publication have been given in endnote number 13:

Śrīdhara, *Shoots of Reason* (*Nyāyakandalī*), ed. V.P. Dvivedin, *The Bhāṣya of Praśastapāda together with Nyāyakandalī of Śrīdhara*, 2nd ed. (Delhi: Sri Satguru Publications, 1984).

In this publication, line 18 on page 70 reads:

यस्य तत्त्वज्ञानं निःश्रेयसाय घटते विपर्ययज्ञानं संसारहेतुर्यदर्थानि

There are two problems with this evidence.

i\) This is a statement given by Śrīdhara and not Praśastapāda.

ii\) Śrīdhara is merely pointing to the importance of the true knowledge of Ātman, which leads to niḥśreyasa, and the consequence of false knowledge of it, which is saṃsāra.

What she has cited as evidence is not the statement of Praśastapāda, and even that statement does not strengthen her assertion that as per Praśastapāda ‘self is the highest value’. In the statement cited, niḥśreyasa is contrasted with saṃsāra, which clearly tells us that what has been presented as niḥśreyasa is the liberation from the cycle of death and rebirth, and not Ātman. Also, when Ātman itself is eternal and omnipresent as per Praśastapāda himself, how can one achieve Ātman?

The other evidence presented by the author are the *Vaiśeṣika*-*sūtras* 1.1.1–1.1.4. What are these?



अथातो धर्मं व्याख्यास्यामः ॥१.१.१॥

यतोऽभ्युदयनिःश्रेयससिद्धिः स धर्मः ॥१.१.२॥

तद्वचनादाम्नायस्य प्रामाण्यम् ॥१.१.३॥

धर्मविशेषप्रसूताद्द्रव्यगुणकर्मसामान्यविशेषसमवायानां पदार्थानां साधर्म्यवैधर्म्याभ्यां तत्त्वज्ञानान्निःश्रेयसम् ॥१.१.४॥

These sūtras also do not support the author’s statement.

This is not the only case where errors appear in this chapter. Further, this is not the only such chapter in the book. **3. *From the Indian Knowledge System to the Plural Knowledge Systems of India*** by Sayan Dey and Rishiraj Sen. *Public Humanities* journal (Cambridge University Press), 2026.

In this paper, the authors have criticised the new venture named ‘Indian Knowledge System’ (IKS) being run by the current government of India on the basis of the National Education Policy 2020. The central argument is the so-called high-caste bias of IKS, due to which the knowledge generated and possessed by communities beyond the so-called high-caste people of India, is left out. The official documents of IKS published by the government of India give a completely different picture. The said documents explicitly include the traditional knowledge possessed by the tribal communities of India, and implicitly include all such traditional knowledge of various Indian communities.

Following are some excerpts:

i\) “Indian Knowledge System encompasses all of the systematized disciplines of Knowledge which were developed to a high degree of sophistication in India from ancient times and all of the traditions and practises that the various communities of India — including the tribal communities — have evolved, refined and preserved over generations.”

p 5, Guidelines for incorporating Indian knowledge in higher education curricula (published by the University Grants Commission in 2023).

ii\) “Knowledge of India” will include knowledge from ancient India and its contributions to modern India and its successes and challenges, and a clear sense of India’s future aspirations with regard to education, health, environment, etc. These elements will be incorporated in an accurate and scientific manner throughout the school curriculum wherever relevant; in particular, Indian Knowledge Systems, including tribal knowledge and indigenous and traditional ways of learning, will be covered and included in mathematics, astronomy, philosophy, yoga, architecture, medicine, agriculture, engineering, linguistics, literature, sports, games, as well as in governance, polity, conservation. Specific courses in tribal ethno-medicinal practices, forest management, traditional (organic) crop cultivation, natural farming, etc. will also be made available. An engaging course on Indian Knowledge Systems will also be available to students in secondary school as an elective. Competitions may be held in schools for learning various topics and subjects through fun and indigenous games. Video documentaries on inspirational luminaries of India, ancient and modern, in science and beyond, will be shown at appropriate points throughout the school curriculum. Students will be encouraged to visit different States as part of cultural exchange programmes.

p\. 16, National Education Policy 2020 (published by Ministry of Human Resource Development in 2020)

Here is one instance where the authors have made a completely false statement. On page 3 of the paper published in *Public Humanities*, the authors claim:

“The NEP policy, through the IKS, argues that India’s traditional knowledge systems are rooted solely in the high-caste Hindu Vedic traditions and, in the name of revival, aims to legitimize myths as realities, erase history, and promote pseudoscience as science.”

The corresponding footnote mentions the website of IKS division (Indian Knowledge System. 2020. https://iksindia.org/about.php.). Neither the policy document of NEP 2020, nor the said webpage says or argues anything like what the authors have claimed.

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

A layperson would read such content and assume that it is true because the publisher is very reputed. One major benefit of self-publishing research articles and books is that the reader will be less likely to assume the quality of the publication, because there will be no label on the basis of which a reader usually assumes it. Of course, the publication of a highly established academician will be assumed to have high quality. We cannot remove all kinds of assumptions, but we can surely try to minimise them.

I hope academicians stop being slaves to the labels and take control of their work in their own hands. There are better methods to disseminate good quality information today and academicians should be at the forefront of using them.
