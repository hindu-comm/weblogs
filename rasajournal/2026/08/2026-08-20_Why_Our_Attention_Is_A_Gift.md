# Why Our Attention Is A Gift

### If social media companies can monetise our attention to redefine the consumer economy, then why are we unable to use the same attention to our advantage?

“We live in what is, but we find a thousand ways not to face it,” is what the American playwright and novelist Thornton Wilder once wrote. While it sounds like something a Zen monk would say, he was actually referring to the importance of good theatre, presumably in contrast to the popularity of Hollywood movies. Yet I have come to realise that, at the end of the day, enduring goodness can only come out of attention, whether in art or meditation.

We live in what is defined as an attention-economy where our attention is the most dynamic billboard there is. If you study the history of modern capitalism, you will find it easily enough that mass products were never created to serve a need. They were created to manufacture a want that did not exist in you until you saw an advert for it. General Motor’s Alfred Sloan came after Henry Ford’s wildly successful Model T cars. By that time, most working class Americans had a car and enough wages left in hand to live a better life than their parents. Sloan quickly “realised that the key to mass-producing consumer goods was no longer just technology but psychology: making consumers feel comfortable spending their new wages.”

For this reason, he introduced a system of credit financing that is now common across the world. This credit could be availed for all sorts of cars, from the budget Chevrolet to the premium Cadillac, which came in a variety of colours. Not just that, he also introduced the “annual model change” policy in 1926 which also proved to be a game-changer. This policy, again much like Apple’s products today, had slight variations that made the product “new” for a while. From making the brakes a bit better to introducing a new colour, every car by General Motors excited people because of the novelty factor. Sloan was concretising personal gratification in a way that would have felt monstrous only a few decades prior. He called this strategy “dynamic obsolescence”, stating, “each year we build the best car we possibly can to satisfy the customer and then the next year we build another to make him dissatisfied.”

This was early 20th century America. Within a century, the entire world is re-enacting the same impulses in all areas of life. When we scroll infinitely on our social media platforms, we are looking for some kind of novelty. An interesting podcast, a sleek smartphone, the latest must-have accessory, who is travelling where, and so on. We are essentially being driven by the same fear or desire algorithm where either one of these dictates our conscious awareness. Are others doing better than me or am I doing better than everyone else? We never get good answers for either one of these questions so we keep on going. We never pause. We never register anything else. We never pay attention.

When we are told to pay attention in school, it is usually to focus on studies. To study harder, score higher, and be better at following instructions. But what really is attention? For one, it has something to do with silence, as I wrote [here](https://rasajournal.substack.com/p/is-silence-possible-today). What comes after silence? Awareness. And how do we use that awareness? By paying attention.

The reason why we alternate between pathologies of fear and desire is because the reality seems to resemble that duality. We see things as either good or bad. We talk of greys but subconsciously, we usually register a thing as either to be avoided (or feared) or embraced (or desired). We generally avoid disease, poverty, and insults. We generally desire health, prosperity, and praise. Yet, how could we ever perceive one without the other? If I have never known prosperity, then poverty is normal for me. If I have never known poverty, then prosperity is normal for me. Either way, it is only because of the other that one is perceived. Without the other, the one is all there is and there is no perception at all. Perception comes from a place of contrasts, never same-ness.

This is where attention becomes important. When advertisers (or social media influencers) try to capture our attention, it is done by establishing this contrast. If you buy this product, you will be the envy of people around you. If you hold this opinion, you are better than others. The implication may be implicit or explicit, but it is always there however subtle or loud it may be. For someone looking to sell you something, it is in their interest to make the messaging dramatic, aggressive, and repetitive. It is in their interest to make sure you can never really pay attention to what is, only what they want you to see. This is why capitalism succeeds so wildly in the age of social media. It has never been easier (and more profitable) to capture attention. Social media shows ***the other*** better than it has ever been perceived in the history of humanity.

But where they sell a utopia of happily ever after, endless comfort would actually lead to entropy. We would self-destruct in absence of any friction. On the other hand, we are frequently sold a chimera of doom and gloom. But if that were to be actually true, life would cease to exist quicker than I can finish writing this sentence. Whether we pay attention or not, Nature does. It constantly conjures a dance of opposites so that everything can be perceived. No wonder then that the French philosopher Simone Weil once wrote that attention is the rarest form of generosity.

But we find it exhausting to pay attention, to reconcile the apparent opposites. Charity feels meaningless in face of cruelty. But what if this is the basis of our journey as human beings? Attention not only makes us aware of the movement between the apparent dualities but also helps us make sense of them. It is not mere perception but also doing. Not just silence but also expression. Not just the calm but also the fierce. Not just the now but also the eternal.

When we are no more compulsively avoiding the bad out of fear or chasing the good out of attachment, we can finally begin to rest in the moment. We have been conditioned in the modern worldview to see this moment as emptiness but it is the very opposite in fact—an infinity full of possibilities. It is this moment that helps us shed our animal instincts and get closer to godliness where our actions, words, and perceptions are not defined by pure self-interest. We can be a part of something larger than ourselves in which we play a limited but vital role. We become a bit like a wave in the ocean.

If we were to pay attention to the ocean, we would be surprised by its complexity that is absent to us at a glance. It is not just the waves, but also the calm. The depth. The shore. The sea of blues. The glint of sun’s light. The absorption of rain and letting go of the very same raindrops next year. The life that lives beneath it but which will die if it comes to the surface for too long. The life that lives above it but which will die if it stays beneath it for too long. The pull of the phases of moon. It is the vastness that inspires. It is the vastness that terrifies. The ocean is where pure stillness meets pure movement.

It is where the dualities meet and yet, nothing is ever absurd or impossible. In a similar way, this is what attention does as well. It does not reject the real-ness of our fears or desires, it rejects their totality. Attention is not focus but a deeper and gentler discernment of the threads that intertwine everything and everyone. Attention is not separation but a coming together. Attention is what gives meaning to our subjective consciousness while also letting us into the larger collective consciousness and vice versa.   
  
But we often cannot access this attention because we live in a disembodied world. We reject the wisdom of our body for the intellect of the mind. We reject the experiences of our senses for the promise of our logic. We reject the intuitions of our soul for the survival of our ego. As a result, our attention becomes fractured and conditional. And so do our lives and our consciousness. It is upto us to redirect this gift of attention to where it belongs— to us.
