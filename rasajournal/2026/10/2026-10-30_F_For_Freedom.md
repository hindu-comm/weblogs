# F For Freedom

### Understanding what freedom really is and how to find it in our lives.

[](https://substack.com/@rasajournal)

In January of the year that was 2020, I took an Uber ride that was rather strange. I had been researching about astrology in the months prior and had published a [story](https://www.jgnt.co/why-millennials-prefer-co-star-to-prem-jyotish) about it in an American magazine that month. My understanding of the topic was superficial at best but it was fresh on my mind.

The car was dirty and noisy. The driver was an old man who spoke with a bass-effect to his voice and drove so slow that it took me twice as long to reach my destination than I would have otherwise. But somehow during the ride, I had a flash of intuition that I was being driven by none other than Saturn itself. Or more likely, an embodiment of it. The intuition arrived out of nowhere and when it did, it caught hold of me fully. I was further spooked when at the end of the ride, the driver turned back to look at me with a small smile and apologised for taking too long. I told him it was not a problem and tipped him extra. Knowing whatever I did about Saturn, I was glad to have reached my destination at all in one piece even if it left me feeling mildly annoyed.   
  
I later realised that I had started my Saturn return that month. Saturn is the slowest moving planet in astrology and associated with delays, coldness, the downtrodden, and the like. It takes roughly 30 years to come back into the same zodiac sign which is what is called a Saturn return. At that point, I did not know I was going to write a [book](https://bygarimagarg.com/heavens-and-earth) about astrology but somehow less than five months down the line, I had a book deal. It was published just about the time when my Saturn return came to an end at the age of 30. The book’s publication also took twice as long to come to fruition as it should have due to a string of unpredictable events one after one.

One of the most common questions people ask me is why did I write this book. I usually give an answer that is easier to understand but it is only a speck of the truth. I wrote about astrology because I wanted to understand fate. I have been obsessed with the idea of fate because its presence has loomed too large for comfort in my life. It has been kind and unkind to me in equal parts as it is for everyone. But perhaps it bothered me more because I wanted more than others to be in control of my life yet I felt less of it than others seemed to be. I was more inclined towards the modern impulse of “your life can be anything you want it to be”. Inevitably, we always want life to be some version of “happily ever after”.

Freedom makes life worth living because it gives us the space to enjoy the gift of existence.

In my perception, fate seemed to hand out its sentences and rewards like a mad king drunk on power. It seemed to lack purpose, reason, or even common sense. Freedom, on the other hand, makes life worth living because it gives us the space to enjoy the gifts of existence. Without it, we are mere zombies who exist as a matter of fact. It is paradoxical and a cosmic blessing, then, that in writing my book about fate, I discovered my true freewill. I realised the difference between the freedom sought by the ego versus the one experienced by the soul. I realised that fate is not only what happens to us but also how we happen to our lives. We are wonderfully complex in our inner worlds and astrology can be a handy tool to navigate them.

Fate is not only what happens to us but also how we happen to our lives. We are wonderfully complex in our inner worlds and astrology can be a handy tool to navigate them.

Unlike the modern therapy and psychiatric evaluation frameworks, which hand out clinical treatments to “solve” our perfectly valid emotional responses to life, astrology gives us a much more life-affirming way to understand our inner lives. For one, it affirms our diverse emotional landscapes. Not everyone has to be this way or that way. It prescribes no normative standards for anyone to be considered worthy of the highest truth and joys.

Secondly, it lays out that emotional landscape in a broad and structured manner. Finally, and most importantly, it gives us a positive end-goal to resolve our inner desires and conflicts. In the Indian system, this is achieved primarily by connecting our consciousness with that of universal consciousness through the philosophy of Karma. The idea is not to become better than others in any way but find peace and joy within.

I also realised in the course of writing the book how deeply we misunderstand Karma. Or to be more precise, how deviously we try to manipulate it by seeking to “remedy” it as if it was mere bureaucratic red-tape. Karma is not random. It flows for generations in families, communities, and global dynamics. It is emotional, mental and physical. It shows up in our personalities, our relationships, and our work. It works on our ego, mind, senses, and soul. It is our lived consciousness. It is not vengeance but a mirror into the human condition. It is not a one-stop destination but a river that flows seemingly endlessly for no particular reason other than a constant attempt at self-expression and self-realisation, which grows more and more profound as the journey proceeds.

Karma is not random. It works on our ego, mind, senses, and soul. It is our lived consciousness. It is not vengeance but a mirror into the human condition.

It took me nine months to complete the manuscript and throughout that duration, I was constantly taken aback by how my conception of reality was being challenged every day. It felt disturbing initially because of how sterile the modern perception of reality is in contrast. Time seemed to flow in opposite directions simultaneously— the past seemed to show up in future and the future seemed to have already revealed itself in the past. People were not just names and faces, they were patterns and influences. Events and circumstances were not unrelated, they were pieces of a puzzle that seemed to be arranging itself on its own. It forced me to hone my attention by forcing me to withdraw from my ego and resting in the following stillness that often felt like emptiness at the first brush.

There’s a quote from Holocaust that I came across many years ago, which I felt expresses deep pain and bitterness. The quote is not attributed to anyone in particular but is believed to have been found scribbled inside a Nazi concentration camp by a Jewish prisoner. It says, “*if there is a God, He will have to beg my forgiveness*”. At the same time, however, Holocaust also included people like Viktor E. Frankl who famously wrote of his travails as, “*everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms – to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances, to choose one's own way*." His book Man’s Search For Meaning, published in 1959, remains a bestseller to date.

The Holocaust is undoubtedly an extreme example for most of us but I find the contrast of approaches here rather telling. Two people can go through the same horror, yet come out of it completely different individuals. While both are perfectly valid responses, one is more life-affirming than the other because it overcomes the conflict by recognising its immutability. To want to win a conflict is to be engaged in it permanently, constantly besieged by the watchfulness of whether we are winning it or losing it. But to recognise its constant presence in our lives in one way or the other is to begin to actually deal with it without antagonism. Ironically, it becomes a sort of freedom.

This inner freedom lies in taking responsibility for our thoughts, emotions, and attitudes in response to a reality that is often not our fault. When we talk about freedom in modern discourse, we often talk about freedom in an external sense. We talk about things like what can we wear, eat, say, or who can we marry. We talk about rights. We talk about anyone who blocks that freedom as an oppressor. Freedom should be its own argument and so it should not require us to enter into any negotiation with anyone. Yet, that is not how the ancients understood it.

Our lives do not belong to us solely. They belong to those who came before us, those who exist alongside us, and those who will come after us. We are not independent of anyone or anything in this existence and therefore, no one’s freedom can come at the cost of anyone else’s. Yet it always does come at the cost of others in one way or the other. Eventually, it starts a blame game where each party stakes the rightful claim for freedom. But the chain of blame is never-ending and fruitless. Freedom, then, is to let go of it entirely. To neither take blame nor adjure it but to take responsibility for one’s words and actions after the fact because not doing so would amount to either cunning or escapism. In this responsibility, there is a self-preservation that does not come at the cost of anyone else’s happiness and dignity. In this freedom, there is a happiness and dignity that does not come at the cost of anyone’s favour or mercy.

The chain of blame is never-ending and fruitless. Freedom, then, is to let go of it entirely. To neither take blame nor adjure it but to take responsibility for one’s words and actions after the fact.

The problem is that this freedom does not feel like much initially. It feels light but like a soap bubble rather than a bird flying in the open sky. Even when we manage to lean into it sincerely, the world around us is structured to tear it down because for most part, we do not actually want true freedom and cannot bear to watch anyone else have it either. This freedom is much harder to attain because it comes from a deeply vulnerable state of being which is the antithesis of the human ego’s desire for control. This freedom requires us to undergo change inside of us, instead of asking for it outside of ourselves.

True freedom requires us to undergo change inside of us, instead of asking for it outside of ourselves.

In this change, there is the awareness that this freedom is always available to us even if the conditions outside of us remain undesirable as ever. This hard-fought change does not grant us the child-like “happily ever after” but it allows us something better. It gives us the freedom to be who we are without asking anyone to grant us the open sky. When the skies are dark with clouds or bitter with cold, freedom is the ability to take in the scenery anyway without being overcome with the fear that the sun may never come out again. Most importantly, it is the letting go of the insistence that the sun should never hide itself because if it didn’t, the existence would turn into a desert.
