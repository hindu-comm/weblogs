# The Moth And The Flame

### The interplay of our ego, soul, and the secret to a better life in this world.

Many years ago, I met a shopkeeper in the lane that used to lead to Kashi Vishwanath temple in Varanasi. It was late evening and I was just walking through the busy lane, looking to take photos along with some photographer friends. He was sleeping in his hole-in-the-wall shop where he sold miniature religious sculptures and all manner of paraphernalia. I took a picture of him and his shop because I thought it made a good photo and continued to walk ahead. But within a few minutes, our group heard him calling out for us. He had apparently woken up just as we left and felt somewhat embarrassed that he had been caught sleeping at work. I told him I had no such judgement in my mind when I took the photo and that he shouldn’t worry about it. In any case, we got into a conversation that still makes me wonder.

He told us that even though he never went to school, he knew many foreign languages and his knowledge of Spanish was attested as credible by a friend who also knew the language. He said he had learnt the languages from foreigners who stayed at his house in exchange for cheap accommodation. He then shared that he wished to be a government-licensed tour guide but couldn’t be one because it requires a college degree. As if all of these bits of information weren’t incredulous enough, what he said next stumped me the most. He said he had no regrets because he was happy to live so close to Baba (Lord Shiva). I was surprised because he seemed genuinely at peace with his life, as simple as it was, despite his potential to build a much better life. He had a contentment on his face that you would never find on Mukesh Ambani’s countenance. And it wasn’t an act; he didn’t try to sell us anything or extort anything from us, except a few minutes of our time.

I was mostly surprised by how he dealt with his ambition. He didn't lack it but he was also not consumed by it. He allowed it to manifest in whatever way fate (or Lord Shiva) wanted. This was remarkable to me, especially in this age of individualism where the personal ego is everything.

After all these years, I feel it must have had something to do with Lord Shiva and his city indeed. Kashi is the place where people go to die because the belief is that it grants them Moksha, the ultimate liberation. Yet death there is not a finality, it is a part of life. It is all around you when you move about the ghats and Godowlia Chowk market. The walls are full of graffiti art about the cycle of life and death and the streets resound with “*Ram Naam Satya Hai*” chants accompanying corpses being taken away for final rites. This is important because it gives meaning to the domain of Lord Shiva, i.e., destruction, whether it is the death of the body or the ego.

Mystics often refer to the importance of losing the ego in order to make life beautiful. One of the metaphors offered to explain the futility of ego-desire as the primary motivation for effort is that of the moth’s attraction for the flame. According to new [research](https://atmos.earth/ecological-wisdom/overview-why-moths-are-drawn-to-light/), moths need the moon’s light to navigate a stable flying path. But they cannot distinguish between the moon, a flame, or even an artificial light. So when they get fatally trapped by the flame, they are actually looking for the right way forward (and upward). They are constantly trying to survive and die due to illusion, rather than desire. But this is not where the metaphor ends, in fact this is where it begins.

Penned by the Hasan Kamal, who has written several of Bollywood’s legendary songs, there is a sentence in his last song, Jaaveda Zindagi, which hints at it nicely. It goes, “*patange ko jalne ka armaan kyun hai, isi shauk ka imtihan zindagi hai*”. I suggest listening to the [song](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P-rV6fTSSaI) in full, I find it to be a beautiful and profound rendition. The poet essentially asks why does the moth want to burn itself and then answers that life is a test of that desire. This sentence has remained stuck in my consciousness ever since I heard it more than a decade ago.

All living beings are constantly engaged in a battle for survival. But as humans, survival is something slightly more complicated for us. Unlike all non-human beings who seek only food and shelter for their survival, we tend to complicate things a lot more. For us, survival is also safety, social prestige, and accumulation of resources. But that only helps us survive at the physical level. It keeps us alive, fed, and safe from the mob. What happens when we seek to survive at a higher level? That is when we turn to knowledge, love, and meaning. We want to know why we are here and why we must struggle so hard to merely survive. We want to know if there is any goodness that redeems all of the struggle. If not, wouldn’t it be better to just eat, play, and make merry for as long as we breathe?

This is where the tiny and humble moth delivers its grand performance. In its accidental death, it exhibits its will to exist. The moth desires to be for no reason other than the desire of being. This desire goes beyond mere survival. It shows us that life is just powerful as death, perhaps slightly more even. Life wants to manifest itself not just for survival but for any expression of itself, however minute and temporary. It shows us that desire is not mere gratification but also liberation itself.

But this liberation does not come from grasping at it tightly, rather it comes from letting it be whatever it wants to be. The flame in which the moth dies is the flame in which our ego burns. It is the flame in which our consciousness is purified. Which is why, when we seek that higher survival, the ego has to take a back-seat. It is no more the most important force in the world, in fact it must die altogether. It must allow the soul to emerge so that it can express itself freely for no reason other than just contentment and bliss. It sheds the carefully and painfully curated personal self in exchange of a momentary experience of the formless divine. This is enlightenment for us, however ephemeral.

This experience is important for us because it continually teaches us that life is more than just survival. It teaches us kindness, generosity, humility, or any form of selflessness are not a zero-sum game. It allows us to contemplate setbacks, misfortune, and mistakes because life is not a marathon sprint that can be won by any one at all. It allows us to be humans rather than rats who are incapable of thought and feeling but are still engaged in a rat-race that never ends.

The flame in which the moth dies is the flame in which the ego burns. It is the flame in which our consciousness is purified.

Yet, the ego is not the enemy it is often made out to be. It is vital for our survival in this dog-eat-dog world where self-preservation is the only reality. However, once it ensures basic survival of our being, ideally it has to be discarded because we are also endowed with a soul which constantly seeks to experience its eternal reality. We go through this interplay for an infinity of lifetimes with a deeper and deeper refinement until there is no more distinction left between being and not-being. That is, when ego and soul are no more in conflict with each other and become one. For that, the ego must become adept at dying and soul must become just as good at expression. Neither of these functions come easy to us and we have to be constantly forced into both to be good at them. This comes from the friction we experience in the world in form of relationships, society, conflicts, and of course, survival. Without any of these, life would cease to exist. Without ego, life cannot manifest itself. Without soul, life cannot express its beauty. Without conflict, life cannot evolve into the bliss of nothingness.

This is not mere spiritual abstraction. We only need to pay attention to the history of modern ideas and their manifestation today to understand this in real-life terms. Until 17th-18th centuries, life around the world was organised in form of small communities with a strictly enforced code of conduct. The higher consciousness of a community was contained by — artists who were almost always insane; priests and scholars who eschewed the life of a householder; and star-crossed lovers who always met a tragic end. Rest of the community benefited from their insights but lived a safer and saner life. This life was aimed at raising a large family and working long and hard with limbs rather than the mind.

But that changed with the era of Enlightenment and the developments before and after it. The printing press made knowledge cheap and widely accessible, secularism ensured that the collective consciousness grew without the gatekeepers of orthodoxy, and industrialisation made more and more people affluent enough to afford a good lifestyle. Modern technology further makes all of these functions much more accessible. This brings us to modern day individualism where the average individual seeks to express its highest potential. But modernity lacks the emotional depth, warmth, and meaning to allow for an actual transformation.   
  
Part of the reason is our lack of spiritual anchoring. The most pertinent question for a thinker is not life but death. If all ends eventually, then why bother living a hard life? For this reason, ancient cultures developed intricate and ritualised approaches towards death. That made death a part of life, rather than something to be feared. From Mexico’s Day of the Dead (Dia de lo Muertos) to China’s Ghost Festival to India’s Pitru Paksh, there is a time and space reserved for departed ancestors along with the changing seasons of the year. It is not only a constant reminder of what life is but also why it must keep on going. This consciousness is at home with the phenomenon of transformation and rebirth not just for those who have passed on but also those who continue to live. But that modernity lacks that vitality. As a result, the modern individual stays stuck in cynicism or delusion. We refuse to pay attention to the fact that many of our cherished ideas come from a movement named enlightenment, let alone understand the gravity of its implications.

Enlightenment never begins as a noble pursuit of higher reality. It always starts as selfishness and self-absorption. It starts as a single-minded pursuit of happiness and a permanent escape from suffering. If the moth could express, it would ask why is the moon unattainable and who lit the flame that burnt it to death? It is this process that carves us into an individual from a member of the unthinking herd. It is this process that makes us conscious of who we are. It gives us awareness, which in turn, invariably desires an expression. The expression, in turn, approaches the realisation that we are not special. That life is the same for us all, whether we are a human being or a moth. This truth burns us but it also transforms us. It is the point where the pursuit of self has the potential to be something noble enough to benefit the collective.

Modern individualism may get to that point eventually but not before it unleashes anarchy. Because when a small community is transformed by its outliers and outcasts, the explosion is easily contained. But when a global collective is meant to be transformed, the repercussions are bound to be that much more serious. To cultivate contentment is vital for our survival as a civilisation, both materially and spiritually. The state of affairs makes it more difficult than ever before but for that reason, it is also more important than ever before.

Contentment is not self-indulgence, aloofness, or childishness. It is a constant practice of acceptance in face of destruction while never forgetting that life can always bring happiness and peace in the very next moment. It is not about demanding that others do our will or achieving it ourselves at the cost of others. Rather it is a constant inner refinement where you allow the soul its moment in the sun, even if no one sees you shine. It is desire not as bondage to the world but our liberation from it. It is the being that does not assert itself no matter how intensely you burned to attain it.
