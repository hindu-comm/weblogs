# In Praise Of Innocence

A few years ago, I read an [essay](https://aeon.co/essays/a-history-of-kidults-from-hello-kitty-to-disney-weddings) about “kidults”, i.e., adults who cosplay as kids, in Japan. It didn’t make much sense to me at the time to be honest and I actually thought it was kind of weird. It made the case that 21st century is defined by what is now known as [polycrisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycrisis) and that’s leading people across generations to regress into their childhood in one way or the other. The author writes that companies like Lego, for instance, are entirely run on the back of adult customers, not children. In Japan, women can make or break industries on the basis of what they find “kawaii”, the Japanese word for cute. In the other part of the world, Harry Potter franchise has been thriving despite the fact that the book series was finished in 2007 and the last movie starring Daniel Radcliffe came out in 2011.

Innocence of childhood is clearly one of the best, perhaps even the best, aspects of life on earth. It is the most joyful, loving, and free-flowing expression of what it means to be human. Kids express their emotions, thoughts, and behaviour without a hint of self-consciousness before the adults around them start teaching them the infinite rules of their world. They retain their exuberance until the inevitable loss of innocence, i.e., when the real world bursts their bubble. They learn that the real world is not only full of strange rules but also human ego and complexes, violence, death, disease, rape, abuse, misfortune, poverty, failure, hurt, and the like.

Innocence of childhood is clearly one of the best, perhaps even the best, aspects of life on earth. It is the most joyful, loving, and free-flowing expression of what it means to be human.

This loss of innocence is pretty much the opposite of innocence. It leads to a consciousness that is highly self-aware, afraid, and calculative. Its most important goal is survival at all costs and it adapts to the reality around it in any way possible just as a plant grows in the direction of sunlight, not straight-up. While conventionally we believe that is the nature of human life, there might be more to it than we realise. If we allow ourselves some more innocence, we might learn that there is more to it than our childhood selves.

Modern psycho-analytic therapy movement makes a lot of noise about healing the inner child but often gets stuck in the trauma discourse (and adult tantrums). I am not sure if most people truly get it but what they are really meant to heal is that loss of innocence because just as faith isn’t real until it has been tested, our innocence could never have been real until it is shattered. The crucial idea here is that human consciousness is meant to go through a fracture before it can transform itself into something useful.

In my initial days at journalism school, I often felt exasperated when my professors asked me “but what’s the story here?” after I’d finished sharing an idea for a topic I could write about for my reporting class. Mostly because I never knew what the story was and it took me a while to understand the difference between a topic and story. India, for instance, is a topic but not a story. A story would be something along the lines of national parks in India and what they can teach us about biodiversity.

Innocence can point the way but it cannot walk the distance and fulfil the journey which is often much, much, much more arduous. Innocence is curiosity unencumbered by the difficulties that arise with seeking and getting the answer. But that does not make it any less relevant. In fact, it is the most reliable star for navigation that we possess as we go through our own lives. It is the form of consciousness that not only helps us enjoy life but actually leads us to our destiny. It is our ability to continually create new life in the best way possible. It is deeply personal in how it tugs at our vulnerabilities and wholly impersonal in how it calls to us to fulfil our role in the collective consciousness.

I recently read a poem by the Irish poet David Whyte that captures this essence really well. It is titled “*The Edge You Carry With You*” and is as follows,

  
*You know so very well the edge of darkness   
You have always carried with you.  
You know so very well, your childhood legacy  
That particular inherited sense of hurt,   
Given to you so freely by the world you entered.  
And you know too well by now  
The body’s hesitation at the invitation to undo   
Everything others seemed to want to make you learn.  
  
But your edge of darkness has always   
Made its own definition secretly as an   
Edge of light and the door you closed might,   
By its very nature be one just waiting to be   
Leant against and opened.   
And happiness might just be a single step away,   
On the other side of that next unhelpful and undeserving thought.*  

Innocence is deeply personal in how it tugs at our vulnerabilities and wholly impersonal in how it calls to us to fulfil our role in the collective consciousness.

Spiritual traditions around the world often emphasise the importance of leaning into the troubles, hurts, and challenges of our lives as the doorway to the answers we seek. Modern consciousness, as fractured as it is, regresses into literal childishness in its desire to experience the joys and usefulness of innocence or becomes enraged and paralysed by the loss of it. What is really needed is the ability to see innocence as anything that feels life-affirming in the most fun way possible— a child’s hearty laugh, a flower gushing with colour under a clear blue sky, a delicious meal thoroughly enjoyed, an adventure sought out with joyous curiosity, a light-hearted moment shared with a stranger, and the like. While it appears like a soap bubble which bursts in seconds, it holds vital clues into who we are and could be at our best in spite of the reality.
