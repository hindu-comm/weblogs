Sieben is not the first name most likely think of when speaking of Neofolk. Largely the work of Matt Howden, a musician from Sheffield, England, Sieben — or The Mighty Sieben as the project is sometimes known — is perhaps among the lesser-known names in the Neofolk scene, but what really sowed the seed for this series of works to begin with was the track “Love’s Promise” from their fourth release, *Sex & Wildflowers* (2003)[1](#footnote-1). While writing on the nature of Primordial Desire (*Kāma*/*Eros*), I was listening, among other songs, to this album, when “Love’s Promise” struck me like an arrow fashioned from a twig of Baldrsbane.

*beith* (birch) - *muin* (vine, neck) - *níon* (ash, fork), the ‘bindrune’ used by Sieben’s Howden in the [Ogham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogham) script

Although I first heard “Love’s Promise” fairly early on in my Neofolk listening journey — over ten years ago now — on the Neofolk Compendium, I cannot say that I truly *listened* to it with the same level of mind that I have paid to it in my latest years. As one might *foredeem*[2](#footnote-2) from the name of the *songbook*[3](#footnote-3) upon which it was first *leesed forth*[4](#footnote-4), it is built upon the *antimbers*[5](#footnote-5) of *wortlore*[6](#footnote-6) and *willing*[7](#footnote-7) are closely tied together by Howden, not only on this track, but on the entire release, which we must first speak of before we can get to the heart of this work. *Sex & Wildflowers* holds thirteen songs, the first nine of which follow the loose framework of the cycle of the *yeartides*[8](#footnote-8). Though most — if not all — of this songbook are worthy of being written about, I swore to first speak on this song.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!SQER!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd25b2acf-9fe2-4b9e-a39e-73f167a2941a_282x200.heic)

[An interview with Matt Howden (Compulsion Online)](https://www.compulsiononline.com/interview_matthowden.htm)

Out of the thirteen tracks found on *Sex & Wildflowers*, I reckon that it is “Love’s Promise” what marks the height of Howden’s craft at that time. Indeed, in this one song, it seems he seems to weave together a truly wonderful and heady bouquet of the sundry greens and blooms he has been singing about thus far.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!i5Eb!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe2745bde-fa48-4e91-b894-722718303800_700x700.heic)

[MATT HOWDEN of SIEBEN talks loop pedals, the power of nature, and the power of the live stream… (Old Man’s Mettle)](https://oldmansmettle.com/2021/10/06/matt-howden-of-sieben-talks-loop-pedals-the-power-of-nature-and-the-power-of-the-live-stream/)

The high, keening sounds of the violin that can be heard throughout many of the other tracks are here thrum in the background like an unending heartbeat before rising in both loudness and pitch towards the end where it tapers off into a tinny wail. Upon this throbbing lifespulse, the outgrowth of Howden’s genius grasp and reach newer heights like creepers on a trellis as he weaves the threads of witchcraft with wortlore and willing with the greatest of ease like a *libwright*[9](#footnote-9) boiling down the sap of the year into the headiest of absinthes. With that said, let us dive into the words of the song.

**Love’s Promise**

The sun has spurge the season,

enchanters night the wormwood sky.

Your musk, your asarina joy,

your radiant woad, our honeysuckle tryst.

Will you come again,

sweet as briar, rose-hipped?

Will you come again,

rough as chervil, lining the lain?

Our fritillary love,

its warp and weft our bindweed trust.

Our wild garlic lust,

our foxglove’s thrust, your butterbur desire.

Will you come again,

perfuse as cicely,

spurge as teasel?

Will you come again,

sharp as shepherd’s needle?

Will you come again,

sorrel and bramble?

Will you come again,

my winter aconite?

Will you come again,

saxifrage dittander?

Will you come again,

my lily of the valley?

Will you come again,

viper’s grass and hogweed?

Will you come again,

my feverfew and tansy?

Will you come again,

love’s promise and betony desire?

Will you come again,

enchanter’s night, our honeysuckle tryst?

The way Howden weaves words is truly one of kind, and it’s unlike anything I’ve seen from any other English wordsmith — at the very least within the Neofolk scene, which has no dearth of good writers. Although other singers have sung about blooms and blossoms — here Douglas P. and Rose McDowall come to mind — it is the way that Howden does so that I think has no peer, which we can see from the first line itself: “*The sun has spurge the season*”, wherein he *notes*[10](#footnote-10) a name as a deed.

Spurge is the name for any *wort*[11](#footnote-11) within the *kindred*[12](#footnote-12) *[Euphorbia](https://euphorbiaceae.org/pages/about_euphorbia.html)*, named for the Greek *leech*[13](#footnote-13) Euphorbos, by his lord, King Gaius Julius Juba II of Numidia and Mauretania. Euphorbos was the brother of Antonius Musa, himself leech to Augustus Caesar. After Augustus had a statue built in Musa’s name, Juba — who in his youth had been brought to Rome as an *isel*[14](#footnote-14) and raised in the Romish ways, first by Augustus’ uncle, Julius Caesar (hence the boy’s name), who had *overwon*[15](#footnote-15) Numidia from the babe’s father, Juba I, and later, by Augustus himself — named the plant after his own leech, Euphorbus.

![Mauretania 200 bc](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CQ4t!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_lossy/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2c2fb9c0-76f1-41f0-ac8f-210a74085059_280x179.gif "Mauretania 200 bc")

[About Euphorbia](https://euphorbiaceae.org/pages/about_euphorbia.html)

Juba, put on the throne of Numidia by Augustus, was ousted by the landsfolk who did not take kindly to his Romishness, and was sent to the western half of Africa’s northern coast along the Middenyardly Sea to Mauretania (western Algeria/northern Morocco). Pliny the Elder, who in his youth was taught by Juba and studied wortlore in Rome, tells that after Juba went westwards to Mauretania, he was *behandled*[16](#footnote-16) by a plant he would come to name after his leech, now thought to be the Resin Spurge of the Atlas Mountains. Euphorbus, meaning ‘well-foddered’ is thought to hearken to both the plant and the healer’s fleshy shape.

This was later taken up by Carl Linnaeus when he gave this line the name of kindred “Euphorbia”. Though the plants of this line may range in shape from cactus-like succulents (such as the type species, *E. antiquorum*, or *E. ampliphylla*, the tallest of this kindred and the only rainforest succulent tree) or shrubs (such as *E. obtusifolia ssp. regis-jubae*), to larger, woody bushes (*E. lamarckii*) and tree-like shapes (such as *E. dendroides*). Even that well-known blossom, so often seen at Christmas time, the poinsettia, is of this line (*E. pulcherrima*)[17](#footnote-17). The Wood Spurge (*E. amygdaloides*) of England is known to take over dry, shady areas under trees through its powerful underground rhizomes.

[Wood Spurge (](https://www.woodlands.co.uk/blog/flora-and-fauna/wood-spurge-and-the-euphorbias/)*[E. amygdaloides](https://www.woodlands.co.uk/blog/flora-and-fauna/wood-spurge-and-the-euphorbias/)*[)](https://www.woodlands.co.uk/blog/flora-and-fauna/wood-spurge-and-the-euphorbias/)

The plants of this line are filled with a thick, rubbery white sap, which drips from even the smallest of cuts given to the plant. This has given rise to the other English name for the wortkindred: *wolfsmilk* (see German *Wulfsmilch*). This ‘milk’ is highly *attery*[18](#footnote-18): should a drop fall on a man’s bare skin, it brings about a burning feeling. This becomes all the worse should it get into his eyes, nose, or mouth — if drunk, it brings about great illness as it runs through the body, upsetting the maw and having a laxative effect, for which *note*[19](#footnote-19) it was put to work by Euphorbos. It is for this **purgative** effect that the wolfsmilkworts got the English name of “spurge”, stemming from the Latin *expūrgō* (‘to purge, clean’), through Old French *espurgier* (the same), and *espurge* (the plant).

[The wolf’s milk atter](https://www.woodlands.co.uk/blog/flora-and-fauna/wood-spurge-and-the-euphorbias/)

In this first line, then, what Howden is telling us that sun has, in *framing*[20](#footnote-20), wiped the yeartide clean and bleached it white, and paints us a stark painting of his *scoply sight*[21](#footnote-21).

In the second line, Howden strings together a delirious vision of bewitchment, death, and birth clad in deepest emerald. The whiteness of the cleansing sun is then painted over by lush, dark green. “Enchanter’s night” likely calls to the wortkindred *Circaea*, also known as enchanter's nightshades (no kinship to the true nightshades) from the belief that it was by means of these plants that Circe bespelled Odysseus’ men.

Wormwood is, of course, the English name of the plant by which is drawn the green spirit absinthe, the common wormwood, *Artemisia absinthium*, as well as the infused wine, vermouth (itself stemming from the elden English name of the wort, *wermod*). Absinthe is, of course, closely linked with hallucinations, madness, and *leedcraft*[22](#footnote-22), even if this is not factually accurate. In *fay*[23](#footnote-23), wormwood is the name for the green oil drawn from the plant *noted*[24](#footnote-24) as a wormbane to slay gutworms. This wortkindred, *Artemisia*, also holds the plant called mugwort (*A. vulgaris*), which stems from the elden English for ‘midge’ and ‘wort’ (*mucgwyrt*), and its kin-name is rooted in the Greek goddess Artemis, for her midwifery as mugwort since it can cause a woman’s womb to pull together, a *frame*[25](#footnote-25) that can also dearly harm an unborn child should a childbearing woman take it too soon because the worts of the kindred *Artemisia* are often full of an *atter*[26](#footnote-26) called thujone, which can bring about cramps.

Some might know of wormwood from the Christian book of *Revelation*, wherein it is the name of a star of whom it is said that it shall fall into a third of the fresh waters at the end of days and make them bitter (likely meaning attery) to drink, thereby bringing about a great many deaths. I do not if Howden also meant to hearken to this, but in the Ukrainian tongue, the name for mugwort is *chornóbyl*, which gave its name to the city that we call Chernobyl. The “wormwood sky”, takes on these associations with the sun and life, but also delirium and death.

As an aside, the poinsettia may well be the capitalist symbol par excellence. Doing a quarter billion dollars’ worth of business each year, with 70 million plans bought in a six-week span, the poinsettias are not only the perfect example of the power of hype, but also of the infectious generative potency of capital: phytoplasmas, a group of bacteria spread by ‘blood’-sucking bugs (just like the Black Plague) and which can lead to many sicknesses in plants, such as blooms turning into leaves (phyllody) and ‘witches’ broom’, are *besunderly* (especially) known for the way in which they stop the middle trunk of a plant from growing above the rest (apical dominance) and instead lead the side shoots to wax past what is *geweanly* (normal), thereby making them bloom more than once per plant and thus more *worthful* (valuable).
