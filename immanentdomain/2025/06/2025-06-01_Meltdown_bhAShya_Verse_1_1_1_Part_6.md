#### Time and God



Land’s (1992b) ‘jealousy’ reaches cosmic heights when he equates it to time:

> “Time is the suicidal jealousy of God, to which each being - even the
> highest - must fall victim. It is thus the ultimate ocean of
> immanence, from which nothing can separate itself, and in which
> everything loses itself irremediably. The black mass of jealous rage
> swells like a cancer at the core of the universe, or like a volcanic
> ulceration in the guts of God, and its catastrophic eruption consumes
> all established things in the acidic lava of impersonality.” (p. 94)

In a certain sense, Land is right. There is a tragic element to the nature of God, particularly whenever He descends to this Earth for the destruction of the wicked and the protection of the devoted. Śrī Rāmacandra was sent into exile for fourteen years and cut off from His kingdom, His mother, brothers, and father thanks to a vow the latter had made. He had to hear the news of His beloved father, Daśaratha’s death from sorrow over their parting. Worst of all, perhaps, He was struck by the fangs of the snake of separation from His Beloved, Śrīmatī Sītā-*rāṇī*, after She was kidnapped by Rāvaṇa. It is only the pasttimes (*līlās*) of Śrī Kṛṣṇa that might show a greater sorrowing. Vasudeva’s Son appeared to His earthly parents in the cells of His maternal uncle, the usurper of the Vṛṣṇi throne, Kaṁsa. There, they were being held for the crime of being prophesied to bear the child that would slay him, and had to witness the murder of all of their children at the hands of their own kinsman before Śrī Viṣṇu showed Himself before them in the dead of night in the form of their newborn son, dark as the monsoon rainclouds. Devakī had to witness the Lord free her husband only for him to take her Highest-Bliss far away, never to see Him again for several years until He would return to Mathurā to overthrow the wicked king and free them. His beloved cowherd maidens of Vraja, who had grown up with Him and partaken in the nectar-like *līlās* of His childhood had to feel the pain of parting from Him as He left for the capital to slay the tyrant. Grimmest of all, perhaps, is the doom that was lain for His kith and kin. After the Great Kuru Civil War of the *Mahābhārata*, the world as our forefathers once knew it began to fall apart under the weight of everything built atop it. Gāndhārī, the mother of the slain Kaurava line lay a doom upon the Lord that He too would witness the same ruin come upon His clan. It so came to pass that Sāmba, another of Śrī Kṛṣṇa’s sons, was dressed as a pregnant woman by several youngs of the Vṛṣṇi line who asked some visiting Ṛṣis what sort of a child ‘she’ would bear. Angered at the disrespect, the sages doomed the ‘woman’ to bear an iron bolt (he was also called Śāmba, possibly deriving from *śambaḥ*, another name for the *vajra* of Indra-deva) or *musala*, hence the name of the sixteenth book of the *Mahābhārata* (Ganguli, 1991), the *Mausala Parva* or ‘Book of the Bolt’, where this tale is found. When the Yadu-*vīra* did indeed bring forth the foretold *mausala*, his grandfather King Ugrasena had the club ground into powder and thrown into the sea. The sage Vaiśampāyana said that in those days:

> “**While the Vrishnis and the Andhakas were thus endeavouring (to
> avoid the impending calamity), the embodied form of Time (death) every
> day wandered about their houses**. He looked like a man of terrible
> and fierce aspect. Of bald head, he was black and of tawny complexion.
> Sometimes he was seen by the Vrishnis as he peered into their houses.
> The mighty bowmen among the Vrishnis shot hundreds and thousands of
> shafts at him, but none of these succeeded in piercing him, for he was
> none else than the Destroyer of all creatures. Day by day strong winds
> blew, and many were the evil omens that arose, awful and foreboding
> the destruction of the Vrishnis and the Andhakas. The streets swarmed
> with rats and mice. Earthen pots showed cracks or broke from no
> apparent cause. At night, the rats and mice ate away the hair and
> nails of slumbering men.…
>
> Beholding these signs that indicated the perverse course of Time, and
> seeing that the day of the new moon coincided with the thirteenth (and
> the fourteenth) lunation, Hrishikesa \[Śrī Kṛṣṇa\], summoning the
> Yadavas, said unto them these words: ‘The fourteenth lunation has been
> made the fifteenth by Rahu once more. Such a day had happened at the
> time of the great battle of the Bharatas \[Kurus\]. It has once more
> appeared, it seems, for our destruction.’ The slayer of Keshi,
> Janardana \[Śrī Kṛṣṇa\], thinking upon the omens that Time showed,
> understood that the thirty-sixth year had come, and that what
> Gandhari, burning with grief on account of the death of her sons, and
> deprived of all her kinsmen, had said was about to transpire. ‘The
> present is exactly similar to that time when Yudhishthira noted at
> such awful omens when the two armies had been arrayed in order of
> battle.’ Vasudeva, having said so, endeavoured to bring about those
> occurrences which would make Gandhari’s words true. That chastiser of
> foes commanded the Vrishnis to make a pilgrimage to some sacred water.
> The messengers forthwith proclaimed at the command of Keshava that the
> Vrishnis should make a journey to the sea-coast for bathing in the
> sacred waters of the ocean.” (emphasis added)

That powdered bolt, however, having been poured into the sea at Dvārakā, had flowed to the sacred waters of Prabhāsa (the site of the Somnath temple) and become the *erakā* grass. It was to this place that the Andhaka-Vṛṣṇis went on pilgrimage. Packing many tasty foods and drinks “The troops of the Vrishnis and the Andhakas, blazing with beauty and endued with fierce energy, then set out from the city on cars and steeds and elephants.” Their pilgrimage quickly became a wild revel, with “The Vrishnis, mixing with wine the food that had been cooked for high-souled Brahmanas, gave it away unto monkeys and apes”. Tongues loosened with drink, the Yadus, who had fought on both sides of The War, began to bring up old grudges from the time of the war, thirty-six years ago. Sātyaki, a kinsman of the Lord and himself born of a part of the Maruts (*sa jajñe marutāṁ devānām*, 01.061.073c, BORI), first insulted Kṛtavarmā. After The War had ended, Kṛtavarmā helped Aśvatthāmā, son of Droṇa, along with the latter’s maternal uncle, Kṛpa, sneak into the Pāṇḍava camp at night while the five brothers and Śrī Kṛṣṇa were not there to seek revenge for his father’s killing. Aśvatthāmā, given strength by the sacrifice of himself to ‘himself’ (as a portion of Hara given mortal shape), began the gory work of slaughtering all the warriors of Pāñcāla and Matsya, the Pāṇḍava’s allies, who quailed in fear at seeing Death himself stalk their camp. Many tried to run away but Kṛtavarmā and Kṛpa, who stood guard at the gates, halted the fleeing warriors and slew them where they stood, unarmed, despite their pleas for surrender, before setting fire to the camp as Aśvatthāmā finished slaying the Pāṇḍava’s children. Kṛtavarmā shot back, calling Sātyaki out for having murdered Bhūriśravas (the killer of his sons) after he had already lost to the latter before his sword-arm was cut off by Arjuna’s arrow and he gave up fighting. Despite Śrī Kṛṣṇa and others’ warnings not to, Sātyaki struck off his foe’s head. Pradyumna lauded Sātyaki for his words. Once again ignoring the Lord’s words to stay his hand, Sātyaki once again took up his blade and slew Kṛtavarmā, cutting off his head. And in that moment, the hour of the doom of the Yadus was nigh, and all were as possessed by blood. At first, the Lord stood by, but after Pradyumna was slain trying to save Sātyaki, who was set upon by the other men, the delighter of the Yadus snatched up a handful of the nearby *erakā* grass, which became as unto Śakra’s *vajra*, and struck all about Him like a vengeful thundercloud. As the sage Vaiśampāyana said:

> “**Then the Andhakas and the Bhojas, the Saineyas and the Vrishnis,
> urged by Time, struck one another in that fearful mêlée**. Indeed, O
> king, whoever amongst them took up in wrath a few blades of the Eraka
> grass, these, in his hands, became soon converted into a thunderbolt,
> O puissant one. Every blade of grass there was seen to be converted
> into a terrible iron bolt. All this, know, O king, was due to the
> curse denounced by Brahmanas. He who hurled a blade of grass saw that
> it pierced through even such things as were utterly impenetrable. In
> fact, every blade was seen to become a terrible bolt having the force
> of thunder. Son killed sire, and sire killed son, O Bharata.
> Inebriated with wine, they rushed and fell upon one another. **The
> Kukuras and the Andhakas met with destruction like insects rushing at
> a blazing fire. As they were thus being slaughtered, no one among them
> thought of escaping by fight**. Knowing that the hour of destruction
> had come, the mighty-armed Keshava stood there, eyeing everything.
> Indeed, the slayer of Madhu stood, raising a bolt of iron formed of a
> blade of grass. Beholding that Samva \[sic: Sāmba\] was slain, as also
> Carudeshna and Pradyumna and Aniruddha, Madhava \[Śrī Kṛṣṇa\] became
> filled with rage. Beholding Gada lying dead on the ground, his wrath
> became enhanced. The wielder of Sarnga \[bow\] and the discus and the
> mace then exterminated the Vrishnis and the Andhakas” (emphasis added)

Even Vabhru the sole survivor of the slaughter upon the sea, sent back to Dvārakā on Hari’s orders, was slain upon stopping to rest when a piece of the *musala* sprang to life on its own and struck him dead as well. Śrī Kṛṣṇa’s brother, Saṁkarṣaṇa Himself, Śrī Balarāma, had already retired to the forest in renunciation, knowing what was to come. After bidding farewell to His father, Janārdana, Śrī Kṛṣṇa came upon Śrī Balarāma sitting alone in the woods. The sage Vaiśampāyana said:

**“**He also saw that Rama had set himself to Yoga and that from out his mouth was issuing a mighty snake. The colour of that snake was white. Leaving the human body (in which he had dwelt so long), that high-souled *naga* of a 1,000 heads and having a form as large as that of a mountain, endued besides with red eyes, proceeded along that way which led to the ocean. Ocean himself, and many celestial snakes, and many sacred Rivers were there, for receiving him with honour.**”**

After the outgoing of His brother, Vāsudeva Śrī Kṛṣṇa too sat down and thought upon the events of His *avatāra* before also entering into Yoga. At that moment, a hunter named Jara (compare Skt. *jaran*, ‘old’, and Grk. *gérōn*, ‘old man’), seeing the Lotus Foot of the Lord, mistook it for a deer and shot an arrow tipped with the single spike that could not be crushed from that ill-begotten club (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa*, Taylor, 2021), hitting the one spot on Keśava’s holy shape that had no guard from harm — His heel. After frovering and forgiving the frightened hunter, the Glorious Lord rose up through the welkin, rising up through the shining heavens of the Devas and took in their prayers before reaching His *paramam padaṁ* (RV 1.22.20).

The concept of time is a significant matter for Land (1992b). ‘God’, he thinks, is reduced to a mere record-keeper or store of human value as a result of both a universal tendency to synchronization and the specific Christian doctrines regarding the nature of the soul. In the first case, synchronization is the result of distillation-out of an “absolute time”, leading to the the creation of ”a transcendent *Aeon* in which to deposit the functional apparatus of *chronos* without fear of decay” (p. 97) like a civilizational egregore. This Aeon can then be used to dam-up the “densely material time of rupture and ruthless re-creation” which allows for humans to measure and manipulate time in a fashion which we can use according to our sense of utility. And to some extent, while this is true, the origins of time (or at least its perception, measurement, and recording) are entirely sublime and transcendental. One need only look at the Nebra sky disk to see this: whether it be the record of a solstice, eclipse, or ‘mere’ map of the heavens, the resources and craftsmanship that went into its making — with metals drawn from the British Isles as well Central Europe and the Carpathian Mountains — show a great concern with the starry welkin. If we accept the solar boat imagery thesis, the sacred nature of the artifact becomes all the more likely. Land’s “book-keeper” god is ultimately a function of Modernity or civilizational senescence in the Spenglerian sense and an aberration from the normal state of affairs wherein Men inhabit a world that is suspended within and suffused by mythic time and not only human historiography.

Time is jealous of being, yet Land (1992b) cautions us not to think of the fact that it erases all things as an acknowledgement of de-materialization “since the only place to escape from matter was God” (p. 95) and Land is nothing if not a pure materialist. Before we can assess the value of Land’s conception of God, we should first understand how he conceives of time, as this will become integral to any further analysis. In any case, between time and being (nature), there is a relationship of mutual infectiousness:

> “As the destroyer the universe is time, and as the destroyed nature,
> but in the destruction nature sloughs-off the crust in which it had
> petrified itself and infests time like rot, regressing to its molten
> core; base-matter, becoming, flow, energy, immanence, continuity,
> flame, desire, death. 'Ecstatic time is not able to find itself except
> in the vision of things that puerile hazard makes brusquely appear:
> cadavers, nudities, explosions, spilt blood, abysses, bursts of sun
> and of thunder' \[I 471\].” (p. 96)

If we compare what Land has said with the eleventh *adhyāya* of the *Bhagavad Gītā*, quoted earlier, does it not seem as though what he is (unconsciously) describing here is the battlefield of Kurukṣetra at the end of the war? This connection becomes all the more intensified as we read on and it has serious consequences for the historiography of humanity. Civilization, he says, is the attempt to resist this ruinous time; there is every reason to resist it and reason is nothing but this attempt. This is what we call the process to “arrest the flow of time” which is a process that works against what he calls the “cosmic sickness” (p. 96), an illness that is nothing but the final *pralaya*[1](#footnote-1) on a localized scale:

> **“**If the deluge, which is danger in itself, is the final motor of
> history, it is the great civilizations which are the engines or
> composite machines, **channeling flows and engendering the mirage of
> function**. As with an ant's nest, what emerges in the aggregate is a
> frenetic immobilism, a literal robotism, converting process into work,
> and work into the further embalming of process. Everything is set
> against 'the explosive immensity oftime' \[I 472\]. **Insofar as a
> civilization functions, therefore, it becomes increasingly sclerotic
> and pyramidalized; rituals, customs, codes, all hardened against the
> release of unendurable forces that would follow from the melt down of
> the energy source (which is pushed further and further upstream,
> purified).**” (p. 96, emphasis added)

First, it is true that many if not most of history’s great civilizations have been engines which channel the literal flows of water of the rivers upon whose banks they were founded. Second, one might consider the division of Mankind into four classes and four stages of life to be something akin to a “mirage of function”; after all, in truth they should form a singular body. Lastly, the end of the aforementioned passage seems almost perfectly to describe to the world of the Kuru dynasty in the waning days of the last *yuga*; indeed, in spite of the best efforts of Gods and Men, it seemed as though the Vedic world was dead-set on tearing itself apart on the basis of feuds and friendships both old and new, collective and personal. The Kingdoms of Men came together upon the field of battle in a clash of horrific splendour unmatched, flowing into the sacrificial pit of the earth (Kurukṣetra) as oblations for Time Himself.

> **“**The Dvaraka river had the Vrishnis and the Andhakas for its
> water, steeds for its fishes, cars for its rafts, the sound of musical
> instruments and the rattle of cars for its waves, houses and mansions
> and public squares for its lakes. Gems and precious stones were its
> abundant moss. The walls of adamant were the garlands of flowers that
> floated on it. The streets and roads were the strong currents running
> in eddies along its surface. The great open squares were the still
> large lakes in its course. Rama and Krishna were its two mighty
> alligators. That agreeable river now seemed to Arjuna to be the fierce
> Vaitarani bound up with Time’s net.”
>
> — *Mahābhārata*, *Mausala Parva*, Section 5 (Ganguli, 1991)

[1](#footnote-anchor-1)

Perhaps of most relevance to the Landian-Deleuzoguattarian tradition is the fact that during this period of destruction, all is wiped in clean in flows of fire or water, not unlike Bataille’s return to the world of Heraclitus’ flows and flames that Land (1992b) references (p. 97).
