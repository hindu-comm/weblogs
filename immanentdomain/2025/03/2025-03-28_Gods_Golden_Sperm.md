Against the PoMo dictum that signs are arbitrary (and therefore effectively interchangable), Y2K promises that global disaster could be precipitated by a specific semiotic trigger. When next some media pundit yawns about the Year 2000 being an arbitrary date, reflect on the fact that Y2K is happening not on a date, but because of a date. More radically put, the event is the date. At no other point in history have semiotic dating practices themselves caused a catastrophe: when people looked to the skies in trepidation in **999**, they didn't imagine that the date itself would bring calamity.

— [A Short Prehistory of Ccru](http://www.ccru.net/id(entity)/ccruhistory.htm)

# [“A twilight gold and grey”](https://youtu.be/bo1d9h7uZQ8?si=tMnxro0PnZIlQ7xp)



A nameless soldier, dressed in a drab gray uniform with a gothic steel breastplate on his torso and a Flecktarn parka tied like a cloak around his shoulders gazes upon ruined castle, now half-rebuilt, rises up on a rocky cliff overlooking a lake in the middle of the Black Forest as a heavy blanket of mist lays atop the remnants of the world he once knew. [The wind that cracks the leaves](https://youtu.be/zpprW613n6s?si=6zDhHfBal-gCParh) carries with it the murmurs the [ghosts of the dead](https://youtu.be/hwYos-GC4Yk?si=WmlYiMEYuEoGk5Lm) which caress the ears of their last living comrades as they gather there for strange rites, bearing daggers, roses, and [crowns of dead meat](https://youtu.be/JrURuVdIauc?si=yoMZzd6qoY1NCkOp). And yet, for all its uncanny feyness, the scent of rich earth, full of rotting leaves and corpses by the thousands clings to everything. A heady [perfume](https://youtu.be/It2CXXKRBgk?si=H9yXQhlSTzzkoidh) of [decay](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2_wXQKdnX1g) impregnates each and every moth-eaten memory not already consigned to the grave. Thick oak roots and thorny briars peek out from clefts in the stone, further prying each brick apart. The soldier’s shoulders slope downwards, burdened by unending weight of an indescribable loss. His days are filled with the [hollows of devotion](https://youtu.be/P0n4SbSCR2A?si=piSmdKOaoCrzw9QK) to a long-dead cause while the sound of marching armies ringing in his ears ensures that his nights are without sleep.

For all its aesthetic self-representation of European antiquity and days long gone, Neofolk is indelibly touched by the kiss of Modernity. After all, it arose from within the punk scene in 1980s England. From this wellspring arose three groups who pioneered Neofolk as we know it today: [Death in June](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_in_June), [Current 93](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_93), and [Sol Invictus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sol_Invictus_(band)). Of the three, Death in June and Sol Invictus share a common lineage in that the former was founded in 1981 by Douglas Pearce (Douglas P.) and Tony Wakeford and Patrick Leagas (Patrick O-Kill). Pearce and Wakeford were members of the avowedly leftist and antifa-aligned punk band Crisis in the late Seventies. Wakeford would eventually be made to leave due to his right-wing political leanings, eventually forming Above the Ruins in 1984, in homage to Julius Evola. But Wakeford would soon come to recant his political allegiances and Above the Ruins would disband only three years later. Finally, he founded Sol Invictus in 1987 of which he continues to be the sole constant member. Douglas P., similarly, has been the only continuous member of Death in June since its founding. Lastly, Current 93 was founded by David Tibet in 1982 and created a sound he called “apocalyptic folk”. Again, like Pearce and Wakeford, he remains the longest continuous member of his outfit.

Alongside the obvious Post-Punk characteristics (more so in the earlier releases by all three of the aforementioned groups), Neofolk’s sound was heavily shaped (as one might expect) by English and European folk music, and was closely connected to Industrial, Dark Ambient, and Noise artists like Boyd Rice (NON) and Throbbing Gristle. In addition to these technical elements, the aesthetics and lyrics of many Neofolk tracks tend to cluster around the themes of militarism, neopaganism, and the occult. Neofolk could well be thought of as the edgier, more martial cousin of Goth. After all, closely related to Neofolk is the musical style known as Martial-Industrial (or Military-Pop), which ratchets these qualities up to another level[1](#footnote-1).

But the clanking and whirring of gears, harsh walls of noise, and the rhythmic stomping of boots are not all that Neofolk has to offer. It has another, more subtle side. One filled with lush, florid landscapes, profuse with the perfume of many-hued blossoms. As the song goes, “On the heath there blooms a little flower”, as the rhythmic thump of jackboots marching by keeps time, “and it's called Erika!”. A maiden reclines on an old wooden cart in the vineyards. A crown of flowers and leaves are woven into her dark hair. She holds a cluster of dark wine grapes in her hands as she bathes in the golden sunlight.

The Ouranic Venus delights in the songs of ROME and Sieben drip like [sticky-dark wine](https://youtu.be/blW1HCQntME?si=5VIIb9k6SQFRk-H4) with the call of the erotic, while the [haunting, angelic voice of Rose McDowall](https://youtu.be/hAawnToLLlo?si=csRzpm4objrrxs9I) [wraps about your heart](https://youtu.be/29bOgY6vDNg?si=Bsd-PHlMkLKXqxZF) like satin ribbons with the kind of lovelorn [sorrow](https://youtu.be/g4HvDHsadSA?si=8X5jjWk9-YIchT09) that only she can evoke. Of course, this wouldn’t be Neofolk if the experience of desire were constrained to soft touches and sprays of flowers in the spring. The Chthonic Venus chuckles as Ordo Rosarius Equilibrio teaches us that just as often, it manifests as an [apocalyptic eternal return of life, death, and demon-worshipping incestuousness wrapped up in a dominatrix’s leather garb, whip and chain in hand](https://youtu.be/URevbmUNAuk?si=XnPAr5v88zpv2jaZ). And of course, how could one forget that iconic logo of Death in June with the gloved whip hand?

# “Who only England know”



Winds of the World, give answer! They are whimpering to and fro—

And what should they know of England who only England know?—

The poor little street-bred people that vapour and fume and brag,

They are lifting their heads in the stillness to yelp at the English Flag!

— [The English Flag](https://www.kiplingsociety.co.uk/poem/poems_englishflag.htm), Rudyard Kipling, 1891

There was a saying, not heard today so often as formerly . .

“What do they know of England who only England know?”

It is a saying which dates. It has a period aroma, like Kipling’s Recessional or the state rooms at Osborne. That phase is ended, so plainly ended, that even the generation born at its zenith, for whom the realisation is the hardest, no longer deceive themselves as to the fact. That power and that glory have vanished, as surely, if not as tracelessly, as the imperial fleet from the waters of Spithead.

And yet England is not as Nineveh and Tyre, nor as Rome, nor as Spain. Herodotus relates how the Athenians, returning to their city after it had been sacked and burnt by Xerxes and the Persian army, were astonished to find, alive and flourishing in the blackened ruins, the sacred olive tree, the native symbol of their country.

So we today, at the heart of a vanished empire, amid the fragments of demolished glory, seem to find, like one of her own oak trees, standing and growing, the sap still rising from her ancient roots to meet the spring, England herself.

Perhaps, after all, we know most of England “who only England know”.

— [Speech to The Royal Society of St George, London](https://whatenglandmeanstome.co.uk/enoch-powell/), Enoch Powell, 1961

To the outside observer, Neofolk and Accelerationism must seem to be completely at odds with one another. The former seems to be “[adrift in dreams of other lives and better times](https://youtu.be/nuuso-kp54I?si=nx7ZDBJPpESbYot6)”. The latter had long ago cracked open the shell of the parochial English cocoon in which it was incubated shred the last morsels of humanity left in favour of cyberian prophecies, arising like the other ‘great beasts’ who made that town their home[2](#footnote-2). What few — if any — seem to understand, however, is just how closely related they are. The sound of Neofolk and the philosophies of Accelerationism both split off from the fountainhead of leftist politics in late-80s Britain: the former from the activist wing and the latter from the academic. Many have heard of the CCRU’s spats with the academic old guard who protested that they were “[still Marxists, you know](https://web.archive.org/web/20250303064116/https://www.theguardian.com/world/2017/may/11/accelerationism-how-a-fringe-philosophy-predicted-the-future-we-live-in)” as Nick Land writhed on the ground and growled into a mic while jungle (a kind of drum & bass EDM heavily influential on the early CCRU crew) played in the background of the Virtual Futures conference. What perhaps few know, however, is that Neofolk was born of a split from traditional leftist politics. However in its case, this departure was less of an overtaking than it was a recoiling away from — a reaction against. I find that (particularly English) Neofolk and (Landian) Acceleration each seem to have found their own aesthetic or intellectual synthesis of Kipling and Powell. In his poem *The English Flag*, Kipling, writing at the height of the Empire, chastises his fellow Englishmen for their ignorant and indolent attitudes towards their dominion and the price that was paid to obtain it[3](#footnote-3). He calls upon the “Winds of the World” to “give answer” to his question of what exactly is the “Flag of England”. For what does it stand? How, he wonders, could the plebeian and ignorant masses standing around watching the flag burn in silence make noise only when it at last fell? Powell, on the other hand, speaking a mere seventy years later clearly speaks of a different world altogether. Having lived to see the decline and end of the Empire, the eventual replacement of the English as world hegemon by the Americans, and the beginnings of mass immigration to the UK, Powell openly speaks of taking another route: that of turning inward. For him, the “the continuity of her existence was unbroken when the looser connections which had linked her with distant continents and strange races fell away.” For Powell, the generation coming back home were finding “affinities with earlier generations of English who felt there was this deep this providential difference between our empire and those others” and that these forerunners would “speak to us in our own English tongue, the tongue made for telling truth in, tuned already to songs that haunt the hearer like the sadness of spring”.

Although Neofolk sprang up “from roots in English earth, the earth of England’s history” (Powell), the England it bloomed in was already irrevocably modern. For Accelerationism, that same biomass was merely a temporary incubator for alien “leeks and thistles grafted upon it here and elsewhere”. Perhaps Land himself best exemplifies Powell’s words, especially in recent years, what with his delving deep into the secrets of the English tongue, discovery of the Beast Pulse, lionization of Milton and Lucifer, and sanctification of the English canon into a new Scripture. Indeed, Powell’s parting words could well be uttered by Land today and elicit little surprise: “The peril can also be indifference and humbug, which might squander the accumulated wealth of tradition and devalue our sacred symbolism to achieve some cheap compromise or some evanescent purpose.” Like the snake eating its own tail, the insectoid serpent of Accelerationism could be said to have closed the spiral and completed the loop — only to have collapsed back into an egg. Having come back home to roost, it hatched, born again and remade in the shape of the Basilisk.

# Afterthoughts



You might be wondering where this essay came from. If you follow me on Twitter, you might have seen me talk about Neofolk. After posting a few oblique tweets and replies, I reckoned it would be good to write about it at some greater length. Although I have made no attempts to hide my fondness for it, it is doubtful that most of my followers and with-speakers truly know of the depths of my love for it, much less of the beauty and worth it holds. I have said that Neofolk and Acceleration are the mirror images of one another (rather than AI safetyism — or ‘Duginist Esoteric Catholic Leftism’ as some have said).

As odd as that may sound, it is my hope that this series of essays, exploring *eros* in Neofolk music, will help to unfold my thoughts both for myself as well as my readers. While I’m not going claim that there is a one-to-one correspondence between Neofolk and Acceleration, they do, nevertheless have similar origins and plunge into similar themes and depths. These explorations are intended to be more of a bridge between the two crowds than anything else — aside from being for my own pleasure.

[2](#footnote-anchor-2)

At some point between 1997 & 1999, the CCRU had to depart from Warwick University as a result of Nick Land’s break with the institution, leading the already embittered philosophy department to utter the now-famous confession of achronicity: “The CCRU does not, has not, and will never exist”. Following their departure from the halls of Warwick’s academy, the collective quickly set up shop in an apartment in the nearby town of Leamington Spa. Leamington Spa was also the birthplace of Aleister Crowley, and, perhaps less commonly-known, the residence of the first president of Republican France: one Louis Napoléon Bonaparte. Following his failed first attempt at a coup, Bonaparte would travel the world before winding up in Leamington in the winter of 1838-1839. After being locked up for another failed after that and later escaping, the once fugitive princeling was elected nearly ten years later as the first ‘Prince-President’ of the French Republic after winning nearly three-quarters of the vote. In 1851, he conducted a coup against his own government and was crowned the second Emperor of France, Napoleon III. This event was named Operation Rubicon, and took place on December 2, the same date as Napoleon I’s coronation (1804) and great victory at Austerlitz leading to the formal dissolution of the HRE (1805). As the nephew of the first emperor, Napoleon III was himself akin to Augustus and his reforms improved France much like Caesar’s successor. In this chain of events, we can see the echoes of the eternal return, with each successive link reinforcing the power of the larger pattern woven in the ‘world-cloak’ of history. Significant also is the fact that the wider county of Warwickshire (in which Leamington is located) is the birthplace of the Bard, whose hometown, Stratford-upon-Avon is located within its borders.

[3](#footnote-anchor-3)

Perhaps for Kipling, who was born in the Colonies, this wider, imperial feeling of Englishness is as inseparable from his sense of self as was the opposite for those he criticizes?
