# A Short Background



Freud is probably not the first person that comes to mind when thinking of psychoanalysts who had connections with India. Usually, it is his former disciple, Jung, whose work and theories seem to find easy parallels with certain currents of thought present in Indian history and Dharmic religions (especially Hinduism and Buddhism). Indeed, with his usage of mandalas, abstract geometric designs or circumscribed symbols with their origins in Dharmic traditions, Jung seemed almost naturally aligned with a view of the world that was in some ways in line with the general Indic conception of the same. As Sharma (2014) says, there have long been many “tensions between religion and psychoanalysis within Indian studies and applied psychoanalysis”. Parsons (1999, p. 73) points out that while key figures in the early history of research into the psychology of religion, including Jung, did not fail to engage with “eastern religions” (namely, Hinduism and Buddhism), Freud’s comparative silence on the matter of the “oriental renaissance” should have been regarded with greater scrutiny in retrospect. It would not, however, be correct to say that Freud had nothing to do with India at all or that psychoanalysis never found any footing there. In fact, psychoanalysis has a long and storied history in India which stretches back to the days when she was still part of the British Empire, according to Hiltebeitel (2018a). Perhaps Parsons (1999) puts it best when he says “Freud’s turn eastwards is more likely to be associated with the establishment of psychoanalysis in India and controversies surrounding the applicability of psychoanalytic theory to other cultures” (p. 41).

Hiltebeitel (2018a) describes the history of the correspondence between Sigmund Freud and Girindrasekhar Bose, one of the earliest adopters of Freud’s creed in India, and more specifically Calcutta, present-day Kolkata, West Bengal. Hiltebeitel (2018a) argues that Freud and Bose’s relationship, which was conducted solely through correspondence, was conflicted and challenging, due both to Bose’s disagreements with Freud on the nature of the pre-Oedipal stage and Freud’s own ambivalence towards Indic views on femininity both divine and mundane. Additionally, he goes into detail describing the various gifts that Bose had sent to Freud and analyzes their significance from a psychoanalytic perspective to give readers a greater insight into how Freud perceived his foreign collaborators as well as how they conceived of him.

Hiltebeitel (2018) was the primary source for this paper, in addition to some other supplementary sources. Jensen’s (2019) *Wie die Couch nach Kalkutta kam* (“How the Couch Came to Calcutta”) would have been another key resource, but as it did not yet have an English translation at the time when I wrote this (and still does not), I relied on an English-language summary (Suter, 2020) to serve in its stead, albeit in a diminished manner.
