# Freud’s Views on India



Beginning largely in the 17th century with the establishment of what would eventually become The Asiatic Society by Sir William Jones, more knowledge began to flow from India to Europe than perhaps ever before. The influence of the arts, myths, and letters of this previously mysterious land can be felt in the life and works of some very important Western and particularly German intellectuals, many of whom were Freud’s forerunners and contemporaries, such as Müller and Schopenhauer, as well as Nietzsche, to a lesser degree. This does not seem to have had a great impact on Freud himself, however, whose writings do not mention much in the way of topics explicitly dealing with Indic or Hindu themes. Indeed, as can be reasonably assumed by his allusion to the myth of Oedipus to describe one of his most fundamental psychoanalytic theories, Freud was far more firmly-rooted in the German and Classical (Greco-Roman) literary tradition, something Hiltebeitel (2018a) reinforces, pointing out Freud’s keeping of a statue of Minerva on his desk as well as the dream he mentioned in a letter to French author Romain Rolland of being at the Acropolis in Athens.

The few times Freud did explicitly mention anything connected with India, Indians, or Hinduism, it tended to be with a cool disdain or a dispassionate acceptance born out of an acknowledgment of their usefulness (Parsons, 1999). For example, after having met Tagore in 1926, he remarked to Ferenczi: “My need of Indians is for the present fully satisfied” (Jones, 1957, p. 128). This attitude of Freud appears to have been a fairly old one, as can be seen in his comments with regards to the *Bhagavad Gītā*, when he supposedly warned a young Swiss poet named Bruno Goetz against studying the text and to be mindful of the monsters that inhabit the depths in 1904 or 1905 (Parsons, 1999). Freud expressed sentiments similar to when he counseled Jung to stay away from the “black tide of mud…of occultism” (Jung, 1989, p. 150). In his relationship with Bose, Freud occasionally comes of as a patronizing colonial master, willing to indulge the members of Bose’s Kolkata circle so long as they flattered and aggrandized his self-image even as he displayed elements of resistance to Bose’s critiques and divergences, minor as they may have been (Hiltebeitel, 2018a).

Another example of Freud’s apprehensions towards all things Hindu and Indian can be seen in a letter he wrote to Rolland on January 19, 1930. In this letter, after a series of letters discussing Rolland’s forays into Hinduism and his planned studies of Swami Vivekananda and his preceptor, Swami Ramakrishna, Freud writes that he will try, with Rolland’s guidance, “to penetrate into the Indian jungle from which until now an uncertain blending of Hellenic love of proportion—*σωφροσύνη*—, Jewish sobriety, and philistine timidity have kept him away”.

# The Influence of Indic Thought on Freud



According to Parsons (1999), although Freud is not associated particularly closely with Hinduism and his works lack any significant references to it save for in scattered footnotes, taken together, these clues can help unearth a greater degree of engagement with aspects of Hindu culture and ways of thinking than was previously known.

One clear hint comes from a footnote in *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* (1920), cited by Parsons (1999), who says that Freud refer’s to Plato’s myth on the origin of sexuality and suggests that it may have been influenced by Upanishadic sources, in contrast to the prevailing opinion of his day, drawing upon Max Müller’s translations as well as his conversations with Heinrich Gomperz, a professor of philosophy at the University of Vienna who wrote about how Indian thought influenced Plato.

# Freud and the Psychoanalysts of India



As mentioned above, Freud was largely interested in India insofar as it could reinforce the prestige of psychoanalysis and prove a fertile ground for its flowering across the world. As he stated in his first letter to Bose from May 29, 1921—what Hiltebeitel (2018a) refers to as “Phase 1” of their correspondence relationship—Freud, replying to the Indian’s outreach remarks with surprise that psychoanalysis was able to arouse interest among people in so distant a country as India.

One of the more interesting episodes in this 12 letter long *Phase 1* is in relation to Bose’s repeated requests in 1921 to Freud that the latter send him a picture of himself—something that he and his fellow travelers in the burgeoning Psychoanalytic Society of Calcutta were apparently highly desirous to see. On January 26, 1922, Bose also sent Freud a pencil sketch of the man drafted by one of the members of the Psychoanalytical Society, one J. K. Sen, despite none of them at the time having any idea of how Freud looked, stating that he wished to see how this would compare with the photograph, and that this sketch was, according to the artist, what he though Freud “ought to look like”. For one as eager to spread the ‘good word’ of psychoanalysis as Freud, his depiction as an Englishman—who in the early 20th century must certainly have been the standard for global power and dominance—would no doubt have contributed to his aspirations, and that too by an Indian, suggesting their unconscious acceptance of his position at the head of this new empire of the mind.

According to Hartnack (1999):

> “Confronted with the challenge to explore the “dark” continents of
> their time—the unconscious, women, and the non-Western world—Freud
> focused on the first, admitted his difficulty in working on women, and
> remained disinterested in an intercultural exchange that went beyond
> confirmation of his own **expansionist strivings**.” (p. 101, emphasis
> added)

This drawing was apparently pleasing to Freud, as Hiltebeitel (2018) points to Hartnack (2001), who says Anna Freud recalled her father saying about the sketch that he was “was glad to look like an Englishman, and not Jewish” (p. 197). Freud would nevertheless let Bose know “that the artist did not take into account certain racial characters”, or rather, characteristics, referring to his Jewish features.

Returning to Freud and Bose’s correspondence, *Phase 1* would come to a frosty end after the latter wrote back to wish Freud a “a merry Christmas and a happy New Year” on November 13, 1922. Freud would answer with a letter saying “A happy New Year to you and as much success in your work as you deserve” on December 28, one full year later, in 1923. As Hiltebeitel (2018) puts it, “Freud’s response, however, reads like a rude joke, one harbored over a year” (p. 19). After this, the two men’s relationship would remain on ice for the next six years.

The records of “Phase 2” (Hiltebeitel, 2018) of the Bose-Freud correspondence begin with a letter from the latter to the former dated to January 2, 1929, answering a now lost cover letter from Bose. Hiltebeitel (2018) compares and contrasts Freud’s attitude in his letters to Bose with those he exchanged with Rolland, whom he said Freud “courted” (p. 23). In Bose’s reply, he sends Freud two sets of several articles, one for the general public and another with technical discussions regarding psychoanalytic mechanism relating to wishes. It is in this letter, dated January 31, 1929, that Bose interestingly chooses to refer to Freud as “the Father of Psycho-analysis” even as he “ventured to disagree with Freud in some respects” regarding the Oedipal wish.

This would open up a serious discussion between the two men. After reading the papers Bose had sent, in a letter dated to March 9, 1929, Freud would answer that he was not fully convinced by the former’s ideas regarding opposite wishes and that he was severely underestimating the power that castration anxiety wielded. He also stated there was, in fact, a connection between the castration wish and a wish to become female while there was, at the same time, a thread linking together castration fear and the fear of becoming female.

Bose would write back to Freud on April 11, 1929 clarifying his argument that the reason why the castration threat held such power was *because* it was connected to the wish to become female, and the tension generated between wanting to be male and wanting to become female gave rise to the fear of being castrated. This, Bose, contended was particularly influential among Europeans. The reason being that while the castration threat was “very common” in Indian society leading to his Indian patients also showing signs of castration anxiety, it was not the same “marked degree” as Freud’s European clientele. According to Bose, this was because the desire to become female was more easily unearthed in the Indian psyche.

If I might digress, I would argue that it is barely hidden if at all. Within the wider river of the Hindu religion, there is a particular stream of great significance. *Bhakti*, or more explicitly, *Bhakti-yoga* as it is called in the *Bhagavad Gītā*, is the path of “Devotional-union”, wherein an aspirant seeks to attain the vision (*darśaṇa*) of the Divine by means of loving devotional service to a personal God who becomes the all-encompassing focus for that devotee’s life—physically, emotionally, and mentally. There has been a long tradition of male Bhakti poet-saints writing, composing, and singing devotional literature from the perspective of a female. This is especially the case in Vaiṣṇava and Śaiva traditions, which are dedicated to Śrī Viṣṇu and Śrī Śiva respectively, two male deities who are commonly considered the Supreme Being by their respective worship traditions. Among the various relationships that a devotee can have with his or her God, one possibility is relating to God as one’s lover. This is referred to as mādhurya, and in orthodox Bhakti traditions, such as most mainstream forms of Vaiṣṇavism, is only considered appropriate when one considers oneself as female in relation to Śrī Viṣṇu or Śrī Kṛṣṇa. Within the metaphysics of Vaiṣṇava traditions which share the concept of Puruṣa, the Primordial Male, that category applies only to the central figure of that sect’s worship. Furthermore, as the *Śaktimān* (“the Energetic”), in relation to the Puruṣa, all other substances are considered as His *Śaktī* (“Energy”), which is by definition feminine. This also includes the *Ātman* (“Self”). As such, those who want to relate to Him as a lover must necessarily take on the role of a woman. This is also why the works of female Bhakti poetess-saints such as Mīrābai and Andal, which speak about relating to Śrī Kṛṣṇa as a lover from the perspective of a woman, are treasured and performed to this day as shining examples of the heights of Devotion.

There are also many stories of men becoming women within Hindu texts, even discounting Hindu doctrines of reincarnation and transmigration: Ila (male)/Ilā (female), founder of the Lunar Dynasty of Hindu kings; Śrī Viṣṇu’s Mohinī *avatāra* to deny the Asuras, enemies of the Devas, the nectar of immortality; Śrī Kṛṣṇa’s disguising himself as a woman to reconcile with Śrīmatī Rādhārānī; Śrī Śiva’s Gopīśvara *avatāra* to participate in Śrī Kṛṣṇa’s secret *Rāsalīlā*. But perhaps most best-known of all is Śikhandi, whose story is part of one of the two great Hindu epics, the *Mahābhārata*.

Returning now to Bose and Freud’s correspondence, in the previously mentioned letter, Bose would also say that he believed the Oedipal mother to be a composite parental image and related to the concept of a mother goddess, which, as Hiltebeitel (2018) also mentions, Bose, as a Bengali Hindu, would have been intimately familiar with. He also explained the mechanics and metapsychology of his opposite wish theory and how it functioned thanks to the Freudian concept of the pleasure principle. Interestingly, it does not seem that Freud pushed him on this matter, and in fact accepted Bose’s claims of a difference in character between Indian and European patients. This is, in fact, quite curious, as one would expect Freud, who had concocted a highly androcentric human history in *Totem and Taboo* (Freud, 1913/1990), to have fundamental theoretical problems with the concept of a mother goddess, which, additionally, would have had no place in either his own Jewish culture nor that of the Christian Germans among whom he lived. Hiltebeitel (2018) argues that Bose’s Oedipus mother actually have a significant pre-Oedipal characteristic, since she is an composite parental image and the relation that Bose posits she has with the child has different characteristics to the Oedipal relationship. Citing Vermorel and Vermorel (XXXX), Hiltebeitel (2018) refers to this pre-Oedipal mother figure as “maternel singulier” (p. 35), or the “originary mother” who represents some of the earliest stages of human development, an “all-powerful” and “bisexual imago” to which a human baby has a purely binary and dependent relationship until, as the child grows older, this relationship “*cedes place—without disappearing—to the oedipal triangulation*” due to the influence of the father who allows the child to leave the state of binary dependence (p. 36).

As mentioned earlier, Freud is also something of an imperialist, with colonial ambitions—likely not an uncommon tendency for a man in his historical context. Signs of this can be seen in the letter he wrote back to Bose on December 13, 1931, as thanks for a gift. At some point between 1929 and 1931, Bose sent Freud a package containing a carved ivory statuette of Śrī Viṣṇu sitting atop His serpentine servant, Ananta Śeṣa. Freud found this gift “charming” and gave it the “place of honour on his desk” as a means of bringing to mind “the progress of psychoanalysis” and “the proud conquest it has made in foreign countries”. Hiltebeitel (2018) draws upon “Period 2” of Freud’s correspondence with Roland who wrote about the “oceanic” feeling in true religiosity to compare and explain the unconscious aspects of Bose’s gift to Freud, since Śrī Viṣṇu is often depicted reclining on the coils of Ananta Śeṣa atop the Ocean of Milk (*Kṣīrasāgara*).
