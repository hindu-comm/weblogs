#### Becoming woman: the gendering of philosophy & the motor of the cosmic economy



In his discourse on Nietzsche in the first chapter of *TFA* Land (1992b) makes much use of what we may term ‘gendered and gendering language’ to characterize the various philosophical doctrines he discusses. Specifically, he disparages the concept of ‘masculinity’ while elevating ‘becoming woman’, likely as a result of his reading of D&G and their emphasis on flows. Masculinity is seen as accumulating, solidifying, and repressing — perhaps not unlike Nietzsche’s idea of the ‘Apollonian’ as described in his work *The Birth of Tragedy*. Femininity is less a coherent or concrete object in Land’s reading as it seems to be more a process of ‘becoming’. Understanding Land’s use of gendered language is integral to even begin to slide into its shadowy depths. Let us first mark each time he uses this language before we analyze and critique it.

1\. The first usage of ‘gendered and gendering language’ by Land (1995b) is in his explanation of how Derrida’s reading of Nietzsche ‘feminizes’ and ‘lesbianizes’ the latter’s work, something which stands in stark contrast to Heidegger’s masculinist reading of the same. No doubt it must sound odd that there could be a ‘feminine’ and ‘lesbian’ reading of such a stereotypically masculine vitalist thinker, but as Land explains:

> **“**An important instance of pseudo-contact[1](#footnote-1) is found
> in Derrida's discussion of Nietzsche and femininity in Spurs - a text
> that serves as a supplement to Heidegger's interpretation of *The Will
> to Power as Art*. This reading marks out some traits of Nietzsche's
> sexuality, a sexuality of and as writing, indicating a web of
> relations to the history and structure of logocentric metaphysics. The
> condensation of these remarks into a pointed proposition, a stylate
> form that is more frequent in Derrida's texts than many of his
> commentators imply, might generate something like this:
>
> Text within this block will maintain its original spacing when
> published
>
> ``` text
> Nietzsche's textuality is worked by a repressed lesbian stratum that subverts the traditional logic of truth and appearance.
> ```
>
> According to Derrida the system of repression that partially dominates
> Nietzsche's writing is orchestrated by a principle of castration,
> having two moments, articulated as follows:
>
> 1 He was, he dreaded this castrated woman.
>
> 2 He was, he dreaded this castrating woman (Spu 101).” (p. 21)

‘Castration’ has traditionally been understood as a “distribution of a moment of pure and ultimate lack” (Land, 1992b, p. 21). For Derrida, however, the difference between having and not-having cannot be condensed down to plenitude or lack because it is itself regulated by something far older: a “primordial propriative movement” (p. 21). According to Land, Derrida “takes this propriative difference to be a moment of deconstructive lesbian excess” (p. 21); this is read by Derrida into Nietzsche as the figure of a woman in love with herself who serves as the “unstable principle of its unfurling” (p. 21). This “figure of lesbian desire” is then inserted by Derrida (against Heidegger’s “ponderous masculinity”) into the problematic of the Nietzschean line of flight from traditional metaphysics. This, he says, is done “in order to mark the auto-affection of non identity, or the asymmetric other of the Phallus in touch with her (non)self.” (p. 22). In any case, Land (1992b) sees these attempts as a multitude of compromises, as a result of which “Zero or the *sacred* is retained within the constriction of profane negativity, and religious fate is interpreted through the technical prowess of philosophy.” (p. 22). To Land, dualities are not acceptable when speaking of the zero, which is characterized by “unilateral propulsion” (p. 22)

2\. Second, Land (1992b) explains that Nietzsche made a clear distinction (albeit an “unstable” one) between creative production (art) and the production of critique (including philosophy) as being respectively manly and womanly:

> “Nietzsche argues that a woman's aesthetics, biased towards the
> question of receptivity, have dominated our understanding of art. He
> suggests that one should not demand of the artist, who gives, that he
> *becomes a woman*, and receives. The production of art is
> characterized as masculine, whilst the reception of art, including the
> entire history of aesthetics, and even philosophy as a whole, is
> allotted to the feminine.” (p. 22)

Importantly for Land’s philosophy, Nietzsche conceived of creative production as something like a contagious nervous illness, throwing Derrida’s aforementioned “pseudo-contact” with his work into a new light. Nietzschean artistic production is characterized as a state in which a complete cut-off of incoming information occurs even as all the gates to output are removed in an excrescent flood. Land, ever the economist, refers to this as a “peculiar economic model… in which a disequilibrium between expenditure and income is pushed towards its extreme”, which from a “bourgeois perspective” is “the ultimate form of dangerous madness; a process of anti accumulation that is totally out of control”, an economy of “Chronic squandering” (p. 23) incompatible with principles of reciprocity of loss and gain. This economy departs from the logical “intelligible human economy” (p. 24) of double entry book-keeping. The Nietzschean connection and the propulsive nature of zero are fundamental to understanding Land because:

> “Reality returns in appearance like the ripple of a shock-wave;
> opening wider and wider domains for migration. Since reality is itself
> the stimulus for such migrations they will become progressively more
> devastating, as this stimulus becomes progressively ‘selected,
> strengthened, corrected’ or, to abbreviate, ‘intensified’. Here at
> last—where nothing is last—is the convulsion of zero, eternal
> recurrence, the libidinal motor of Nietzsche’s economics.” (p. 25)

Let us briefly make note of the imagery of increasingly intensifying (or accelerating) ripples and shockwaves mentioned here. This connects to the significant influence that cybernetic theory had on the work of Land, something which will be discussed in the exegesis of the rest of verse below (\[\[1\]\].\[\[2\]\]). The weightiness of economics to Land’s philosophy is again strengthened here. Think back what has been said earlier about the Bataillean concept of the solar-economic. This solar-economic, also called ‘general-economic’ or ‘Dionysian economy’. Whatever the word used, it shows libidinal materialism’s concern with a cosmic economics. As Land (1992b) explains:

> “Nietzsche’s economy of the artistic process, or Dionysian economy, is
> built beneath the Vesuvian antilogic of eternal recurrence. Such an
> economy is a perpetual re- emergence of inhuman squandering; an
> inappropriable excess messily exhibited in the transfiguration of
> negation into profligate zero. It is intrinsic to desire that it
> always has fresh and—when unmutilated by repression—increasingly
> sophisticated constructions to waste. A Dionysian economy is, indeed,
> a slash and burn agriculture of solar stock, in which the negative
> limit of each conceptual dyad is reconstituted as an intensification
> of the positive; as an increasing virulence of difference. The
> delirium of squandering flows from this inevitability that logical
> negation never arrives, even though zero impacts. In other words, the
> thought of eternal recurrence is this: that the abolition of
> integrated being in the process of desire, or unconstrained wastage,
> corresponds to an intensification of plague and not a (logically
> intelligible) negation of assets. Epidemic difference is only enhanced
> by the spasmodic aberration from itself.” (p. 25-26)

In contrast to how we commonly conceive of the field of economics, with our humanist concerns of profit and wealth, Land, in no uncertain terms, states that the economics of the universe is fundamentally about wastage (which is to say, sacrifice). Keep in mind the use of volcanic metaphor (“Vesuvian”) in describing this process.

3\. Third, Land (1992b) states that Nietzsche can be read as stating that becoming a woman is tantamount to becoming a Christian, and especially, a pious woman, which Derrida suggests is Nietzsche’s version of the castrated man. Land takes issue with Nietzsche’s comparatively shallow critique of Christianity. In his words, it is described as “an identitarian monotheism, insulated against zero, and a privileged graveyard of the sacred; burying the vortex of vulvocosmic dissolution beneath the monument of eternal being.” (p. 24). Land’s gynolatry can thus be seen as a reaction against the theological foundation of the Christian West, in both its sacred and secular variants.

4. Fourth, when Land (1992b) discusses the ‘Dionysian economy’, he explains that this economy is characterized by a “flux of impersonal desire” forever re-energized by eternal recurrence, which occurs at “the 'point' which patriarchal productivism had reserved for its limit[2](#footnote-2); at the end of each becoming a woman” (p. 26). Desire can therefore be defined as differing levels of becoming woman, and as Land admits, “it is always possible to become a pious woman, to begin a history, love masculinity, and accumulate, because to become a woman is to depart from reality” (p. 26), though, following in Nietzsche’s tracks, he thinks that this kind of womanly becoming is the manner of the church.

5\. Fifth, Land (1992b) explains the nature of reality and its relation to ‘zero’ as a becoming of a more primeval or basic kind of woman:

> **“**But reality drifts upon zero, and can be abandoned over and over
> again. In the lesbian depths of the unconscious, desires for/as
> feminizing spasms of remigration are without limit.
>
> Everything populating the desolate wastes of the unconscious is
> lesbian; difference sprawled upon zero, multiplicity strewn across
> positive vulvic space. Masculinity is nothing but a shoddy bunk hole
> from death. Socio-historically phallus and castration might be serious
> enough, but cosmologically they merely distract from zero; staking out
> a meticulously constructed poverty and organizing its logical
> displacement. If deconstruction spent less time playing with its
> willy[3](#footnote-3) maybe it could cross the line . . .” (p. 26)

Having enumerated Land’s primary uses of ‘gendered and gendering language’ in characterizing philosophical concepts let us now analyze his thoughts and see how it can be understood from a viewpoint rooted in our Dharma, thereby attempting to enter it from its outside.

[1](#footnote-anchor-1)

As per page xii of *TFA*, ‘contact’ for Land (1992b) is synonymous with “infection, and - at the limit - the anegoic intimacy that he \[Bataille\] calls 'communication'".

[2](#footnote-anchor-2)

The use of the word ‘limit’ here is weighty as it is intimately connected with the critical philosophy of Kant, whose centrality to Land’s (1992b) work can be seen by the fact that his name itself is the very first word of the first chapter of *TFA*, as well his very explicit statement that “Philosophy (comprehending all 'theory') has no socio-historical pertinence for us other than its relation to Kant.” (p. 3). There, he defines critique as “an instrument of dissolution; a regression to conditions - to the magmic\* power of presupposition - upon which all order floats.” (p. 2; note the use of volcanic language once again), which causes reality to become fluid or malleable, as though one were reshaping the world as though in a dream. Weightily, he also says that capital and critique are both names for “metamorphosis as such” (p. 2), thereby identifying the latter with the former and indicating their nature as that which endlessly brings about the new (a throwback to Deleuze’s ‘difference’ and ‘repetition’). Most crucially for us is that Land defines critique as having to do with boundaries or the “*delimitation of domains of application for concepts.*” (p. 6). Recall that the ‘noumenon’ is called the “terrain of unthinkability” (p. 6). The outside nature of the noumenon was perhaps Land’s fundamental concern. After all, his previous Twitter handle was ‘@Outsideness’. To Land, that which is on the outside is fundamentally unable to be known through human reason, however advanced and purified (or transcendental).

[3](#footnote-anchor-3)

Probably a reference to Jaques Derrida’s ‘phallogocentrism’.
