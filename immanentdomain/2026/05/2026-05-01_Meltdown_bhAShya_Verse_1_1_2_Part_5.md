### “*Like a cloud blown in from the desert…*”



------------------------------------------------------------------------

*The arrival of Islam in India: Buddhist fundamentalism & complicity with the Arabs in the case of Sindh*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The echoes of these [truths](https://immanentdomain.substack.com/p/meltdown-bhasya-verse-112-part-4)[1](#footnote-1) can be heard in the very history of our people. Perhaps the only reason that *Sindhu-deśa* ever fell to ibn Qāsim was that the *[Sthaviravādis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada)* of the *Saṁmitīya*-*nikāya* (*Bauddha* equivalent of *sampradāya*) grew increasingly intolerant, both of Hindus and especially the Hinduizing *Bauddha*-*nikāyas* of the *tāntrika* variety. Their co-*adharmīs* in Bihār even went so far as to engage in a bout of proto-butshikanery against an image of Hevajra and burned *tāntrika* texts.

In his thesis *Religion And Society In Arab Sind*, Derryl N. Maclean (1984) describes the Saṁmitīyas and suggests certain factors that were inherent to their *nikāya* which may have made them more amenable to becoming fanatical *marU*rianists following the Arab conquest. He says that unlike the “highly intellectualized systems” (p. 254) of other *Bauddha*-*nikāyas*, the Saṁmitīyas stood out for their peculiar doctrine of *[Puggalavāda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pudgalavada#Sa%E1%B9%83mit%C4%ABya_and_other_sub-schools)*[2](#footnote-2) (Skt. *Pudgalavāda*, ‘Personalism’: the belief in a sort of transmigrating *puggala* (‘person’) as the subject of karmic fetters, yet one somehow still distinct from the *Āstika* concept of the Ātman.

Maclean suggests that the basis of this doctrine might lie in the highly textualist and literalist tendencies of this *nikāya*, which existed prior to contact with Islam. As he reports it, one defining characteristic of Sthaviravādi-*sampradāyas* is and was the great deal of weight given to the *Vinaya Piṭaka* (‘Basket of Discipline’) — a series of texts which forms one-third of the *Pāli Tipiṭaka* (Pāli Canon) and specifically deals with the monastic code of conduct for Buddhist monks. This part of the Canon, Maclean says, “in many ways, was more important than doctrine to early Theravâda Buddhism” (p. 254). Although the Canon (and the *Vinaya* specifically) is of importance to all *Bauddha*-*nikāyas*, Maclean notes a difference in the attitudes towards it of the Sthaviravādis and the Mahāyānists: while the latter rationalized the teachings in the *Vinaya Piṭaka* as a set of attitudes to be cultivated internally, the former held the text up as the very definition of what it meant to be a Buddhist, used it as the supreme authority in resolving any doubts, and “maintained the literal importance of the sayings of the Buddha, all of which were deemed obligatory and sacrosanct.” (p. 256). It was on the basis of this exegetical conservatism that the Saṁmitīyas, who Maclean says were even more dedicated to radical textualism and literalism than other Sthaviravādis, justified their theory of Personalism, arguing that if the Śākyamuni had used the term then it must have had a real existence which was not merely allegorical.

How all of this might have tied into the marUrianization of Sindh is explained by Maclean’s (1984) theory which stresses the weight of taking into account “preexisting cognitive styles” in order to understand the form of the religion that the natives were converting into. And what was this form of the Third Urianism that many Sindhis converted to? The highly traditionalist *aṣḥāb al-ḥadīth* tendency, which, like the Saṁmitīya-*nikāya*, was particularly noted for being populist, literalist, and textualist that most Sindhi Muslims adhered to — even more than followed the more well-known mystical varieties of the religion. This was not the only similarity between the former and latter inhabitants of Sindh, though. As Maclean explains, the *Ḥadīths*, which reported the marUrianist prophet’s words and deeds, had some similarities in form, content, and process of collection to the Bauddha *Vinaya Piṭaka* and were used by followers of the *aṣḥāb al-ḥadīth* in “maintaining the superiority in governing Muslim behaviour” (p. 254) based on the words of their prophet, “many on relatively minor matters sueh as dress or cuisine—which assume the force of law from the Prophet’s praxis (*sunnah*)” (p. 255). Furthermore, like the Saṁmitīyas, the *aṣḥāb al-ḥadīth* held the view that all doubts must be solved by recourse to these textual pronouncements and strict adherence to their mandates, without any rationalization on part of the member of the community. As Maclean concludes in this section, while “the argument here is not that conversion occurred due to congenial ideological similarities…it is surely reasonable to expect that the converts would opt for a type of Islam intelligible within their previous religious perspective” (p. 257).

While the Saṁmitīyas were consorting with the Thaqafite invaders in *Sindhu-deśa* and smashing Vajrayānist *mūrtīs* in Bodh Gayā, their Sthaviravādi cousins were attacking Hindu social norms, deities, temples, state funding, and rituals, serving pick apart and unravel the threads that had once been woven together to form a pan-Indian (and indeed, pan-Asian) cultural and spiritual memeplex. Like moths, they kept eating away at the fabric of the ‘texture’ of Hindu civilization, leaving numerous holes that compromised its stability and ability to endure in the face of invasions — a rather disappointing end for an age in which great Indian kings from the Guptas to their successors had fought off multiple Hūṇa (eastern ‘Huns’) incursions for nearly three centuries, the likes of which had flattened Central Asia, Xinjiang, and even the Sassanian Empire (‘[Geopolitics of the tantra age: an attempt at a blunt assessment](https://manasataramgini.wordpress.com/2009/04/02/geopolitics-of-the-tantra-age-an-attempt-at-a-blunt-assessment/)’, mAnasa-taraMgiNI, April 2, 2009).

*“The death of the primitive system always comes from without; history is the history of contingencies and encounters. Like a cloud blown in from the desert, the conquerors are there: ‘In some way that is incomprehensible to me they have pushed right into the capital, although it is a long way from the frontier. At any rate, here they are; it seems that every morning there are more of them. . . . Speech with the nomads is impossible. They do not know our own language.’ But this death that comes from without is also that which was rising from within: the general irreducibility of alliance to filiation, the independence of the alliance groups, the way in which they serve as a conducting element for the political and economic relations, the system of primitive rankings, the mechanism of surplus value—all this already prefigured despotic formations and caste hierarchies.”*

*—* Deleuze and Guattari (*Anti-Oedipus*, p. 195)
