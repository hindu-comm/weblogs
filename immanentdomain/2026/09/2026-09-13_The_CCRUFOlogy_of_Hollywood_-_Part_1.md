Nick Land seems to have become something of an *éminence grise* (to Yarvin’s Cardinal Richelieu), in the tech circles of Silicon Valley, but I think his influence is being felt more widely in California these days, including in the cultural-production industry. To be more accurate, however, it is not simply Land who seems to be infecting Hollywood’s hivemind (consciously or not, *willingly* or not), but the collective he was once a part of, the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (Ccru). Neither the Ccru itself nor its work seems to get as much attention as Land himself (aside from perhaps Tucker Carlson’s episode on the Numogram), and yet, it seems one cannot help but find their fingerprints on the edge of popular culture to this day, even after disbanding decades ago [“in Chronos” or “conventional linear time”](http://www.ccru.net/id(entity)/ccruhistory.htm).

One obvious exemplar of this (at least to me) was Steven Spielberg’s latest film, *[Disclosure Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disclosure_Day)*, which seems to deeply echo key themes, motifs, and plot points from some of the Ccru’s greatest works of theory fiction, hyperstition, and occulture. Indeed, the one thought which kept running through my head while watching this movie was that it felt like the kind of picture the Ccru themselves might have made back in the ‘90s if they had the resources — or, the kind of fever dream that an AI might produce if it were trained on Mark Fisher’s 2002 work of hyperstitional theory fiction, *Who’s Pulling Your Strings?* and *I was a Ccru Meat Puppet*, the found text-style testimonial nested within.

Indeed, *Disclosure Day* — like the Silicon Valley moguls cashing in on Nick Land’s mystique to launder whatever they are scheming— largely treats the themes it shares with the Ccru as cheap thrills to titillate and terrorize while serving as a thin veneer for humanist interests instead of engaging with the utter outsideness which any exacting treatment with their work and thought asks. And yet, perhaps it is precisely in this misuse that they have sown the seeds for their own suborning. The “Old Ones” don’t exactly ask for permission, just a call — and this movie was like yelling at the top of one’s lungs into a megaphone in the middle of an open field. It’s just asking to be invaded from the Outside. *Disclosure Day* is not a great film, or even a good one, what with its wooden dialogues, clunky CGI, and the frequent abrupt and often illogical cuts between scenes, but I think that if one comes to watch it in the way I did, as something out of a dream or fragments of an ancient myth found in a crumbling scroll, he may well find something entertaining in it. More importantly, however, the discerning viewer might also note these same parallels I make note of here.

Several of these layers of meaning immediately stand out as comparable (even if somewhat muted echoes) to the themes in *Disclosure Day* — as well as to certain metatextual elements. Of relevance to us are 1-2 and 5-9. The film’s opening shot is perhaps the least subtle political allegory ever. Dr. Daniel Kellner, played by Josh O’Connor, is watching a wrestling match between one wrestler clad in red and his foe in blue while the split crowd cheer for the champion of their colour.

## 1.”\[Astronomical\]. Conjunction (including opposition).”



The first of these twins is one the film does not acknowledge formally — Casper Boyd (*Henry Lloyd-Hughes*), an agent of the primary antagonist Noah Scanlon (*Colin Firth*) and his company WARDEX, a security company with ties to the US armed forces and DoD. Casper Boyd is Daniel Kellner’s ‘evil twin’, so to speak. Both of them begin as employees of the same organization, but while Boyd’s backstory is never revealed, Kellner’s is — he was sent to federal prison for “cybercrimes” (yes, the actual film script is exactly that vague) and headhunted by Scanlon as soon as he had served his sentence (“WARDEX recruited me straight out of the parking lot the day I was released.”).

In deleuzoguattarian terms, we could perhaps loosely see this as the capture of a member of the nomad war machine, as spoken of in *A Thousand Plateaus*, by an organ of State sovereignty. Both Boyd and Kellner serve as the primary agents of their respective commanders’ wills; while Boyd obviously acts as an enforcer for Scanlon and WARDEX, the data stolen by Kellner that sets off the events of the movie was on behalf of Hugo Wakefield (*Colman Domingo*), the leader of a splinter cell of former WARDEX workers and the ‘heresiarch’ who first unveiled the maddening knowledge of what that organization was truly up to.

Boyd and Kellner’s relationships to their bosses also track an intriguing parallelism; while both Scanlon and Wakefield maintain a tight grip on the flow of necessary information that their subordinates are allowed to access, Boyd tends to be kept in dark until the very end while Daniel’s misadventures begin with an unasked for and unwanted revelation or unveiling of horrifying knowledge — an apocalyptic gnosis engineered by a nonhuman intelligence. Boyd and Kellner’s paths both spatially and metaphysically continue to diverge as the character of Margaret Fairchild (*Emily Blunt*) intersects with their trajectories, which we will come back to in Part 2 of this series.
