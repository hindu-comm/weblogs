I have a long post in the drafts about whether the unflattering depictions of non-western peoples by early orientalists (in the original sense of that term) are all just orientalist (in the modern sense of that term) fantasies or even depictions that seem to just call out for anti- and de- colonial ‘*deconstructions*’ do have some truth to them. I’m lazy and write slowly. So it will probably remain in the drafts for a long time to come. One of the topics I will discuss in that post is that most nineteenth and twentieth century accounts of Nepal by English authors seem to emphasize the vice of gambling that was supposedly deep set. This passage from William Brook Northey’s *Land of the Gurkhas* (1937) is perhaps the most descriptive of them all:

> If, however, the Nepalese can be acquitted of any undue tendency to
> drink, the same can hardly be said of their passion for gambling, to
> which they are certainly inordinately addicted. In general, gambling
> in public is strictly prohibited in Nepal, except on certain very
> special occasions such as that of the Diwali festival. But as we all
> know, it is one thing to make laws of this kind and quite another to
> secure their enforcement, and the Nepalese in point of fact gamble at
> all times and in all places whenever they can possibly manage to do
> so.
>
> Here, as in the case of drink, Sir Chandra Shumshere[1](#footnote-1)
> was fully aware to the evil, and, as one means of checking it,
> introduced legislation prohibiting the giving of credit to the loser
> and compelling the payment of all debts of this kind in cash on the
> spot, whereby a certain amount of good was certainly affected, though
> naturally hardly making much difference in the case of such orgies as
> those of the Diwali festival when all restraint is thrown to the
> winds.
>
> The street scenes in Kathmandu and the other towns of the valley on
> the occasion of this particular festival must be experienced indeed to
> be believed. Upon the beat of a drum every other occupation is
> immediately abandoned by one and all and the whole population gives
> itself up forthwith to its one all-absorbing passion, for which, as it
> is hardly necessary to add, opportunities in plenty are provided. For
> not only are there innumerable booths in every street with their
> enticing “tables”, but practically every house and shop in addition
> has its own gambling party all feverishly engaged in some form of
> play.
>
> …
>
> Many and extraordinary too, not to say incredible, are the stakes for
> which they play, as authentic records attest. Thus men have been known
> to stake their wives and children on a throw of the dice, while one
> man is even said to have cut off his left hand and put it down under a
> cloth as his stake. It is recorded further that on winning the game,
> he insisted on his opponent cutting off his hand, or else restoring
> all the money which he had previously won.

The last anecdote about the hand cutting man is a particular favourite among these authors although contrary to what Northey says it is just a popular rumour for which no ‘authentic record’ exist. The conclusion I draw in the piece is that both the legal and documentary sources do show that gambling was indeed widespread at the time and that the government was at times compelled to ban it completely, at others to merely restrict it.

What to do with gambling is a question relevant not only to the individual but to society at large as the losses of the gambler often leads not only him but his family to ruin as well. As in modern times, there were various ways that the state and various thinkers responded to gambling in ancient times. For the specific case of South Asia, the two diametrical attitudes towards gambling may be exemplified by the texts of *Kauṭalya’s* *Arthaśāstra* and the *Mānavadharmaśāstra* respectively.

While *Kauṭalya* has state-run gambling dens which pay taxes to the state and has well defined laws for dealing with any disputes that arise, *Mānavadharmaśāstra* summarily condemns gambling and has the king punish the gamblers with, believe it or not, *execution*.

> The king shall suppress gambling and betting within his realm; they
> are the two vices of rulers that devastate a kingdom. Gambling and
> betting amount to open theft; the king should make constant effort at
> eradicating them both.
>
> When it is done with inanimate things, people call it "gambling"; when
> it is done with living beings, on the other hand, it is known as
> "betting." The king should have anyone who engages in or facilitates
> gambling or betting executed…
>
> *Mānavadharmaśāstra IX.221-224* (From Patrick Olivelle’s translation)

Modern readers, for understandable reasons, tend to appreciate *Kauṭalya*’s method. But the more I have suffered from the ills of gambling personally and the more I hear or read of other such horror stories, I do wonder whether some sort draconian laws against institutional gambling, not execution of course, may not be as bad as people think. Modern world, however, seems to be moving in the opposite direction. In particular online betting (or ‘Prediction Markets’) that have risen over the last decades seem to me to be absolutely despicable development. Even Substack has now integrated a prediction platform. Oh well …

In any case, what I think or don’t changes nothing at all in the world. This post isn’t about the state of modern betting either. I just have a nice poem to translate on the evils of gambling, not from modern times but from the late bronze age.

Though the vast majority of the 1018 hymns included in the *Ṛgveda* are, expectedly, religious and ritual in nature, there are a few that are not. Some scholars have used the word ‘secular’ in reference to them. I do not think ‘secular’ is a good descriptor for this. I would characterize them as ‘not explicitly religious’ rather than ‘secular’. In any case, there are a dozen or so of these poems.

In one of these, the 34th hymn of the tenth book, a gambler laments about his woes. The tenth book is usually considered to be the latest book of the *Ṛgveda* and includes more popular material which are largely absent from the other books which are concerned with the orthodox and hieratic Soma-cult.

This little hymn is, as far as I know, the oldest one of its type. There may be some older things in Mesopotamia but I know of none which reach the same level of literariness. As for the date, it must have been somewhat older than ~1000BC but by how much I cannot say.

The language itself is more modern than the archaic hymns - almost comprehensible to those who understand classical Sanskrit. It is composed, in my view, in a simple but emotionally evocative style which is only rarely the case with its contemporaries. It is not an accident that this piece has been repeatedly translated, anthologized, quoted and so on.

Macdonnell calls it a ‘*striking poem*’.

Brereton and Jamison introduce their translation with, ‘ *The monologue form found here is almost unprecedented in the R̥gveda, and the shifting play of emotions—guilty excitement, tender remembrance of his past life and family, scorn for his own weakness and the disgust he arouses in others, bitter anger at the inanimate dice that brought him low, to which he ascribes agency and malign intention and for which he still longs—is psychologically convincing and very moving. Showing our own biases, we might proudly deem it quite “modern.”* '

Moriz Winternitz says of it, ‘*But the most beautiful among the non-religious poems of the R̥gveda-Samhita is the song of the gambler, RV X. 34. It is the monologue of a repentant sinner who has destroyed his happy life by his irresistible love for gambling. In verses composed in a language that moves the listeners the gambler describes how the dice have robbed him of his domestic happiness:*’

In addition to the the hymn itself, the identity of its author is interesting as well. The verses reference the dice games used for gambling as well. These are quite different from the games played nowadays. I have discussed them below the translation. *Vibhīdaka* is a brown nut used as dice. This is the fruit of a tree called *Terminalia bellirica.*

The original and my translation follow now. My translation, though divided into lines, is to be considered formatted prose and not poetry (of which I am mostly incapable of composing anything even remotely bearable in English). The text bounces between the third and the first persons. I have kept this as is.

### On The Author



The identity of the author is also interesting. The ancient indexes ascribe this poem to either a person named *Kavaṣa Ailuṣa* or to *Akṣa Maujavant* (The Dice from Mount *Mūjavat*). After long and difficult deliberations, I have decided to rule out the second option as the author.

This *Kavaṣa Ailuṣa* is interesting because unlike the vast majority of *Ṛgvedic* seers, he seems to have a patently un-Aryan name. No other person of this name is known except for a certain ‘Old *Kavaṣa*’ who appears in the Battle of the Ten Kings. Whether this is the same as our author is unknown. Probably he is not.

> Then famous old Kavaṣa he wrenched down into the waters, and Anu and
> Druhyu—he with the mace in his arms. The ones devoted to you, who
> cheered you on, (were) choosing there your partnership for their
> partnership.
>
> In an instant Indra split open all their fortified places, their seven
> strongholds, with his might. He shared out the patrimony of the
> descendant of Anu to Tr̥tsu. We defeated the Pūru of scornful speech at
> the rite of distribution.
>
> Ṛgveda VII.18 (From Jamison and Brereton’s translation)

But an interesting story is told in later times about this *Kavaṣa Ailuṣa* which explains his name. His mother, as it turns out, was an un-Aryan Dāsa woman.

> ṛṣayo vai sarasvatyāṃ satram āsata te kavaṣam ailūṣaṃ somād anayan
> dāsyāḥ putraḥ kitavo’brāhmaṇaḥ kathaṃ no madhye’dīkṣiṣṭeti tam bahir
> dhanvodavahann

> The seers performed a sacrificial season on the Sarasvati; they drove
> away Kavaṣa Ailuṣa from the Soma, ‘The child of a slave woman, a
> cheat, no Brahman; how has he been consecrated in out midst?’ They
> sent him out to the desert, …
>
> *Aitareyabrāhmaṇa* II.19 (From A.B Keith’s translation)

*Kavaṣa Ailuṣa* does manage, by supernatural means, to get back and comes out victorious in this case but the insults labelled at him are interesting nonetheless. The word translated as “cheat” by Keith is ‘*kitava*’ which normally means ‘gambler’ and is infact the very word that occurs again and again in the verses ascribed to him that we translated above. Maybe he was actually a gambler in real life after all. In any case, gamblers, especially those who lose (which is practically most of them), don’t seem to have a very high social status, neither then nor now.

I would not personally characterize the supposed author as the ‘downtrodden’ or ‘subaltern’ and make a big deal about supposedly ancient piece of ‘subaltern’ literature but for people who are into that sort of thing, there is enough material here to work with. It is also interesting that it is from an author so characterized that we get one of the simplest (in grammatical terms) and charming composition as compared to the arcane and bewildering style of the more elevated seers. Some of this maybe due to the lapse of time as very archaic language tends to be the most difficult but surely it is not the whole reason. The compositions attributed to *Vasukra Aindra* (X.27-29) that are placed just before those attributed to *Kavaṣa Ailuṣa* (X.30-34) are similarly late in date but are some of the hardest to understand.

### How to gamble



As I said earlier, the dice are repeatedly referred to as ‘brown’ in the text. These were the *vibhīdaka* nuts. The exact method of playing the game is not entirely clear but it must have been something like this.

There were a large number of nuts used as dice (cf. fifty-three in verse 8) which were thrown into a set place in the ground. The nuts were then thrown and somehow separated (maybe by a depression in the ground or something like that) so that what was left wad divided by five. If the remaining number of nuts after the division is four, that is the best score called *kṛta*. This is the best result. If the results are three, two and one respectively, they are called *tretā*, *dvāpara* and *kali* respectively with each being worse than the preceding score. So when our gambler says that “*But for a dice that scored just one, I have estranged my faithful wife.*”, what is meant is that he got the lowest possible score in the game after turning away his wife to play it.

These names for scores in a dice game would hardly be anything more were they not ultimately responsible for giving their names to the four world-ages: *kali-yuga* and so on.

### Some Notes on the Translation



##### Verse 1

- *Mūjavat* is the name of (probably legendary) mountain where Soma was
  brought down from heaven for the use of mortals. If there was a
  corresponding real mountain, it must have been somewhere in the Hindu
  Kush.

- The *iriṇa* is more correctly not a ‘board’ but an area of ground with
  a little hollow made for the game.

##### Verse 3

- Some have interpreted the ‘mother-in-law’ as not the gambler’s mother
  in law but the mother in law of his wife, i.e. a circumlocution for
  his his mother who has disowned him. This is accepted by many scholars
  but I really don’t get it.

- The last two lines are the direct speech that is said about the
  distressed gambler.

##### Verse 5

- ‘*like a woman in love to her spot*’ or more correctly the gambler
  goes to his den like a woman in love goes to her secret designated
  location to meet her lover.

##### Verse 6

- The original *jeṣyā́mī́ti* of the second line can be interpreted either
  as a statement ‘I will win.’ or ‘Will I win?’.

##### Verse 8

- The group of nuts is repeatedly likened to a war-band also in verses
  12 and 14.

##### Verse 10

- ‘*he goes to others’ houses at the cover of night’*. Probably to steal
  or to ask for loans. It is unclear what the reason is.

##### Verse 11

- It pains gambler to see other women or to see their lives because his
  own is ruined.

- The brown horses maybe referring obliquely to the brown nuts of dice.

- *vṛṣala* is a diminutive form of the word for ‘bull’. In later times
  it is an insulting word for low caste people. This sense had probably
  not developed by the time considered here but considering the identity
  of the author, it is not impossible.

- The phrase ‘*when the fires end*’ refers to late night.

##### Verse 12

- *vrāta* is the Vedic equivalent of Indo-European *kóryos* or
  *männerbund*.

- *‘I’m not hiding any wealth*’. Many translations have ‘money’ instead
  of ‘wealth’. I’ve not done that because ‘money’ implies physical
  currency like coins which would not come into use until centuries
  later. As for what he is supposed to be not hiding, maybe gold or
  something, idk.

##### Verse 13

- This verse is quoted almost 3000 years later in an early modern
  satirical poems about praising ugly water buffaloes of all thing. That
  is an interesting poem too. I’ll write about it someday if I feel like
  it.

- ‘noble’ or more literally ‘Aryan’. Interesting the word here with
  short a initial vowel instead of the more common long a.

##### Verse 14

- *manyu* which is translated as ‘battle-fury’ here is probably cognate
  to Greek μῆνῐς (mênĭs), the first word of the Iliad.

- I cannot remember the exact video now but in some interview, [Jackson
  Crawford](https://www.youtube.com/@JacksonCrawford) and his guest once
  mention that a crucial difference between the worldview of modern
  people as opposed to their pre-modern counterparts is that for
  pre-modern bad things *will* happen. They just hope that those things
  will happen to *someone else*. Where we might say, “Let this gambling
  habit not ruin me”, they would say “Let this gambling habit ruin
  *someone else*, not me.” This is of course not a hard and fast rule
  but it is an interesting observation and one which I agree with.

- This last verse makes is similar in content to some later prayers in
  which a gambler prays for victory.

### A last reminder



Though anyone who is already used to gambling is not going to take my unsolicited advice and those who don’t gamble have no need for, I offer it anyway. Don’t gamble guys. It’s not worth it. There are no diamonds at the end of the tunnel. Unless you are literally an *Übermensch* beyond the grasp of us mere mortals or are using insider info in online betting or something, it’s not a good thing. You *will* lose. I’ve experienced this personally. There’s no good ending to it.
