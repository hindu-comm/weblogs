### Why “Sanskrit” Canon?



In the very beginning one might wonder why I would call it ‘Sanskrit Canon’ rather than something like ‘Indian Canon’ or ‘Hindu Canon’ or ‘South Asian Canon’ or ‘Indic Canon’ or something else like that. While ‘Sanskrit Canon’ isn’t an entirely accurate term either, these alternatives are even more misleading.

1.  “Indian Canon” is misleading for two reasons. The **first** reason
    is that the authors of the works were certainly not the citizens of
    the modern country called India and though the probability that the
    citizens of East and West Pakistan would make any significant
    attempt at studying and furthering their pre-Islamic heritage that
    is, at least in some sense, their inheritance too as long as we
    infidels are still around is pretty slim, there are still countries
    with non-muslim majorities (Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka) for whom this
    ‘canon’ is equally our inheritance too. The **second**, and the more
    important, is that there are literary traditions in other languages
    (Tamil, Persian, etc) which would be excluded in such a naming.

2.  ‘Hindu Canon’ would obviously be misleading as Buddhists, Jains and
    others participated in significant numbers. After some hesitation in
    the beginning, by the start of the Christian Era, (northern)
    Buddhists and Jains started writing in Sanskrit in large volumes.

3.  “South Asia” is a US foreign-policy term that people in Pakistan
    seem to find useful as an alternative to “Indian Subcontinent”. Its
    use as anything other than a geographic descriptor is not really
    helpful.

4.  “Indic Canon" is a better term but suffers from the same problem as
    said in the second reason number one. Even if we were to narrow the
    term to mean only ‘pre-modern work in an Old Indic or Middle Indic
    language’, it would leave us with a strange place for Pali works of
    the southern Buddhists. Theravadins in Sri Lanka both preserved a
    large canon of Buddhist sacred writings in Pali and continued
    producing new works in that language continuously.

So, “Sanskrit Canon” is the best term I can find for the works from what Sheldon Pollock calls the ‘Sanskrit Cosmopolis’. His arguments may be read fully fleshed out in his 2006 book “The Language of the Gods in the World of Men”[1](#footnote-1) but the gist is this: Between around 300 and 1300 large parts of Asia, from Afghanistan in the west to Indonesia in the east, started issuing their public documents in Sanskrit and their literature and political culture, though not unified by any single empire or religion or script and obviously interacting with local condition, are a part of this cosmopolitan world with great similarities, at least on the elite level. There is no evidence, contrary to what some people think, that Sanskrit was ever a lingua franca in this region. Unlike the spread of Latin, it was not spread, even in the beginning, by the might of a single empire. Unlike the Catholic Church, there wasn’t a single religious organization spreading its roots either. There is no reason to assume a large scale migration. Why then and how did this world, which Pollock calls ‘Sanskrit Cosmopolis’, come to be?

I don’t really buy into Pollock’s explanation of either how this cosmopolis first came to be or how it ended or even how it functioned but the name itself is a pretty useful one and his book is a very informative read. In my own canon, I would obviously include works much earlier as well as much later than the time period considered by Pollock but it is the same literary continuity which blossoms in this cosmopolis before, during and after that period.

### Whose Canon and For Whom?



We live in a time period when the ideals of democracy are strong, even if they be strong only in ideals. Barring a few Middle Eastern theocracies, even the most crackpot dictatorship, and usually only them, has something like “Democratic” or “People’s” in their name. The idea that the political legitimacy comes from the will of the people as a collective is accepted by most people.

This was obviously not the case in pre-modern times. Even in polities which were nominally republican or democratic, the franchise was so limited that they would certainly not be considered democratic nowadays. Much of what would constitute the Sanskrit Canon was written by Brahmins and usually for Brahmins. More often than not there was not even the vaguest pretence at ideals of equality. In fact more or less the one thing that united all the vast land from Afghanistan to Indonesia, with all their astonishing diversity, was the presence of Brahmins. Sanskrit works were produced wherever there was any significant population of Brahmins and where there was not such population, such works were never produced in any significant quantity. Tibetans and Chinese translated tens of thousands of pages of Sanskrit works into their respective languages mostly during the first millennium AD but there is no evidence that real production of new Sanskrit works was ever there, barring a few personal projects of the great Lama-s. In contrast, there are thousands of Sanskrit inscriptions from South East Asia and indications that there was a flourishing manuscript culture as well because a native Brahmin class ultimately did arise there. Most of these are now long gone due to the rise of Theravada Buddhists and Islam but a small population of Brahmins still survives in Bali in Indonesia, in Thailand and in Vietnam. The only ‘ethnic group’ - and Brahmins more or less are that - you could encounter everywhere between Indus and Indonesia was the Brahmins.

I should clarify that when I mean by ‘Brahmin’, I mean anyone who was by birth a brahmin. Brahmins were, in this sense, massively overrepresented even in Buddhist and Jain circles. Even in Greer’s list quoted above, of the three named Buddhist authors two are Brahmin and one was apparently a prince.

In his model of the Sanskrit Cosmopolis, Pollock naturally tries to deny any link at all between Brahmins and Sanskrit. [2](#footnote-2)

> Second, the conditions of possibility for Sanskrit's diffusion as a
> language of politics: No organized political power such as the Roman
> imperium, or coherent, scripture-based religious idea-system such as
> Islam, was at work here. Sanskrit's spread was effected by traditional
> intellectuals and religious professionals, often following in the
> train of scattered groups of traders and adventurers, and carrying
> with them disparate and decidedly uncanonized texts of a wide variety
> of competing religious orders, Śaiva, Buddhist, Vaiṣṇava, and others.
> Third, its social domain: There is little to suggest—the very fact
> that we have to ask the question is counterevidence—that Sanskrit was
> an everyday medium of communication in South let alone Southeast Asia,
> or that ever functioned as a language-of-trade, a bridge-, link-, or
> koine language or lingua franca (except among those traditional
> intellectuals) like other imperial or cosmopolitan languages such as
> Greek, Latin, Arabic, Persian, Chinese.

Johannes “Brahmins are like Nazis” Bronkhorst has a great rejoinder to Pollock’s arguments on this point. The whole paper is quotable but I’ll only extract the necessary here:[3](#footnote-3)

> Elsewhere in his article Pollock suggests that there was no specific
> link between Sanskrit and Brahmanism during the period he considers.
> He does so while discussing the first appearances of Sanskrit in
> inscriptions in South Asia. In short, his argument is that ruling
> dynasties with a clear penchant for Brahmanical religion did *not*
> necessarily use Sanskrit in their inscriptions, and that the first
> Sanskrit inscriptions we have were commissioned by rulers who had *no
> special links* with Brahmanism. The Sātavāhanas – whose rule lasted
> from the last quarter of the third century BCE to about the middle of
> the third century CE – constitute the most important example of the
> former. As Pollock puts it (p. 202): “From the multitude of
> inscriptional and numismatic evidence available to us now \[...\],
> something very striking emerges: Although this was a decidedly vaidika
> dynasty, as evidenced both by their continual performance of śrauta
> rites and by explicit self-identification (e.g., ekabamhaṇa \[...\]),
> there is no evidence for their use of Sanskrit in any non-liturgical
> context \[...\]”.
>
> …
>
> By disconnecting Sanskrit from Brahmanism and from Brahmins, Pollock
> can then formulate questions relating to the spread of Sanskrit in
> terms of the language itself rather than in terms of its users. This
> allows him to propose his hypothesis of “politics as aesthetic power”.
> A consequence of this disconnection is that “we cannot simply read off
> automatically from the choice to express political will in Sanskrit
> any particular social consequences (e.g., hierarchization, hegemony;
> the production of false belief)” (p. 245). No, the qualities of the
> language itself have to account – if not fully, then at least to a
> large extent – for its extraordinary expansion: “This had to be a
> language of transethnic attraction; a language capable of making
> *translocal* claims \[...\]; one powerful not so much because of its
> numinous qualities \[...\], but because of its aesthetic qualities,
> its ability somehow to make reality more real. \[...\] These aesthetic
> qualities, moreover, are authenticated by the language’s possessing a
> tradition of literary texts that embody and realize them.” (p.
> 239-40). Indeed, “the unique expressive capabilities of Sanskrit
> poetry allow the poet to make statements about political power that
> could be made in no other way” (Pollock 2006: 139).
>
> …
>
> In order to answer these questions we must be clear what we are
> talking about. Pollock’s observations about the spread of Sanskrit are
> enlightening and, by and large, sufficient for our present purpose.
> But what is meant by “spread of Brahmanism”? The expression
> *Brahmanism* can be used to designate the religion and culture of the
> Veda, but it is only in a very limited sense that these can be said to
> have spread during the period following the Mauryas. No, the spread of
> Brahmanism was primarily the spread of Brahmins as *Brahmins*. That is
> to say, a region is brahmanized when its population, or its rulers,
> accept Brahmins as the by right most eminent members of society. This
> population, or these rulers, are not so much converted to a different
> religion: no converts are made to Vedic religion, or to any other
> specific religion promulgated by the Brahmins. No, these populations
> or rulers are made to accept a different vision of society, in which
> Brahmins are highest because they have access to the supernatural. An
> important instrument in the hands of the Brahmins is their knowledge
> of the Veda, a collection of texts which the vast majority of the
> population is not even allowed to hear recited, much less study. It is
> their often secret knowledge that gives them the power to work for the
> good of a kingdom, its ruler and its population. It also allows them
> to do the contrary, and this is an important reason to humor them.
>
> For reasons that are not at all clear at present, Brahmins succeeded
> in the course of time to convince many rulers that it was a good thing
> to provide them with what they needed to carry out their rites and do
> whatever else would benefit the kingdom. The growing presence of
> Brahmins all over South Asia is well documented, but they also came to
> be present in Southeast Asia, even in countries that became Buddhist:
> “even in states where Hinayana Buddhism prevailed, Brahmans played an
> important ceremonial part, especially at Court, and still do so in
> Burma, Siam and Cambodia, though themselves strikingly different from
> their counterparts in India.”

And most importantly:

> The part of this conclusion which must be emphasized is that
> Brahmanism is distinct from the specific cults of Śiva or Viṣṇu, or
> any of their innumerable kin, and that the Brahmins stood for a social
> order. This seems obvious and undeniable, and yet it is often
> overlooked by scholars who wish to assign Brahmanism to the category
> ‘religion’. In reality, Brahmanism is primarily a social order. Only
> this way can we make sense of the evidence from Southeast Asia, as
> well as of the evidence from South Asia.

And

*The Catholic Brahmin thing is actually true. There’s a whole class of them.*

I could provide quotes from many primary sources but Bronkhorst makes the point I want to make pretty clearly in a single place.

Many, many famous Jain and Buddhist authors of the first millennium were Brahmins. In Nepal a class of non-celibate Buddhist priesthood exactly parallel to Brahmins, and occupying analogous place in the caste hierarchy there, has existed for a better part of a millennium now. Cārvāka materialists are in modern times characterized as scientific materialists and atheist upstarts at the fringes of society pitifully marginalized by superstitious Brahmins. In actual sources however, every account we have of the Cārvākas suggests they were largely Brahmins themselves. The author of the one surviving work that we can potentially ascribe to an actual Cārvāka, *Tattvopaplavasiṃha*, is Jayarāśibhaṭṭa who bears a patently Brahmin name. Whenever we encounter Cārvāka-s in literature, they are usually Brahmin counselors of the King. The one historical Cārvaka we can identify, Udbhaṭa, was, far from being some intellectual rebel, the head of a literary assembly in Kashmir with a very high salary paid by the state. Unfortunately his Cārvāka works do not survive (though we know that they did exist due to quotations and references) but his work on poetics does.

This obviously leads to interesting political considerations. I don’t like to talk politics and what little I have to say related to the politics of this matter will be said in a later section but I’ll just add the main point here. Academics are overwhelmingly left-wing politically and despite their own remonstrations to the contrary, they do privately hold on to a teleological vision where they are certain what counts as ‘the right side of history’. In our context, it surfaces as a deep dissatisfaction at the idea that Buddhism (which we are told was a rational, anti-caste, egalitarian, scientific philosophy) ultimately ‘lost’ in the end. Bronkhorst himself wrote extensively on this topic. His three books on ‘Greater Magadha’ are mostly around this question. Of those “How Brahmins Won? From Alexander to the Guptas” is basically a book length ‘copium’, as the youngsters say, filled with all sorts of hilarious mental gymnastics, shoddy conclusions based on well massaged out and thin evidence. Anyway, this either leads to, on the one hand, a wholesale condemnation of the Brahmin tradition along with a teasing out of works with little to no impact on the subsequent course of history as the real ‘authentic voices’. On the other hand, it leads to strange interpretation of literary works where writings by some of the most elite people in the kingdom heaping hilariously extravagant praises on the literal royals are interpreted as somehow subversive and subtle critique of monarchy and the social system, so subtle in fact that no pre-modern readers ever found out and it took modern academics to eke that meaning out.[4](#footnote-4)

Obviously it is very understandable why people would care about the voices of the ‘subalterns’ (can they even speak?). I am, to a large degree though not entirely, sympathetic to their concerns. Still, it is obvious why the people who are firm in that sort of political persuasion are unlikely to even approach the ‘Sanskrit Canon’ in its own terms, much less identify with it or celebrate it except, perhaps, by ‘reclaiming’ it so that the original is all but unrecognizable.

### The Question of Aesthetics



The Sanskrit Grammar of William Dwight Whitney, first published in 1879, is perhaps the most comprehensive one of its type in English aimed at general students. There are obviously other works that delve into certain aspects in much more detail or are aimed mainly at scholars and so on but Whitney’s work is useful even now. It has, for example, much more information on pitch-accent than most Sanskrit grammars and I’ve been using it for that purpose myself. In its introduction, he says, among other things, this:[5](#footnote-5)

> By the term “classical” or “later” language, then, as constantly used
> below in the grammar, is meant the language of those literary
> monuments which are written in conformity with the rules of the native
> grammar: virtually, the whole proper Sanskrit literature. For although
> parts of this are doubtless earlier than Pāṇini, it is impossible to
> tell just what parts, or how far they have escaped in their style the
> leveling influence of the grammar. The whole, too, may be called so
> far an artificial literature as it is written in a phonetic form (see
> grammar, **101 a**) which never can have been a truly vernacular and
> living one. Nearly all of it is metrical: not poetic works only, but
> narratives, histories (so far as anything deserving that name can be
> said to exist), and scientific treatises of every variety, are done
> into verse; a prose and a prose literature hardly has an existence
> (the principal exceptions, aside from the voluminous commentaries, are
> a few stories, as the **Daçakumāracarita** and the **Vāsavadattā**).
> *Of linguistic history there is next to nothing in it all; but only a
> history of style, and this for the most part showing a gradual
> depravation, and increase of artificiality and an intensification of
> certain more undesirable features of the language — such as the use of
> passive constructions and of participles instead of verbs, and the
> substitution of compounds for sentences.*
>
> *This being the condition of the later literature, it is of so much
> the higher consequence that there is an earlier literature, to which
> the suspicion of artificiality does not attach, or attaches at least
> only in a minimal degree, which has a truly vernacular character, and
> abounds in prose as well as verse.*
>
> (The italics are mine, the bold faces are original.)

Even when actually writing a grammar of the language, Whitney cannot help but express his disgust at how that language is actually used in actual literature. He uses ‘depravation’ here but such adjectives as ‘degenerate’ are not hard to find in similar contexts in the work of his peers. The earlier literature, which is to say Vedic stuff and especially the Ṛgveda, appeals far more to Euro-American scholars than much of Classical Sanskrit literature. In the nineteenth and early twentieth century, when racialist views were far more common before Hitler and co made them taboo, they might say, and actually did many times, that it was because the early Vedic people were actually their fellow Aryans with sensibilities understandable to them while by the time of Classical Sanskrit race-mixing had had its consequences.

Modern scholars will obviously not use such words but the same feeling is not hard to sense even now. If you were to read books on comparative Indo-European philology, myth and literature, you might legitimately come out with an impression that the only thing that exists in the Indic branch is the Ṛgveda. Seriously, 99% of the Indic evidence is from the Ṛgveda. I get it κλέος ἄφθιτον = śravas akṣitam or something. Get some new material.

It is obviously easy to accuse those colonial-era scholars of racism or orientalism or what not but even in purely literary terms, their reaction is, to a degree, understandable. Early Sanskritists were generally either classicists by training or at least had a thoroughly classical education. And if you have come out of reading Homer and Pindar, the Ṛgveda does genuinely feel like familiar territory and you may recognize not only common Indo-European cognates but even poetic techniques and turns of phrase. But the long and interminable compounds of classical Sanskrit, which feels more like something from an agglutinative language than an inflected one, the passive constructions, the poetic motifs and so on, genuinely do feel far more foreign so that a classic of Sanskrit prose might be described as[6](#footnote-6):

> compares most unfavourably … by a subtlety and tautology which are
> almost repugnant, by an outrageous overloading of single words with
> epithets: the narrative proceeds in a strain of bombastic nonsense,
> amidst which it—and if not it, then the patience of the
> reader—threatens to perish altogether: . . Bāṇa's prose is an Indian
> wood, where all progress is rendered impossible by the undergrowth
> until the traveller cuts out a path for himself, and where, even then,
> he has to reckon with malicious wild beasts in the shape of unknown
> words that affright him.

A contemporary view of this phenomenon by a Sanskritist is by Whitney Cox who says[7](#footnote-7):

> It is here that the slippery slope between objective and subjective
> criteria really becomes apparent. Briefly, there is a compression and
> intensity that marks out these authors’ work. This can be seen in
> their reliance on the figures of grammar and construction—figures like
> chiasmus, anacoluthon and asyndeton—that are staples of classical
> Greek and Latin rhetoric, but which are almost entirely untheorised in
> *alaṃkāraśāstra*. At times, Kalhaṇa writes as much like Ovid as he
> does like Kālidāsa.

which is footnoted by:

> I am no Latinist. All the same, this idea had occurred to me some time
> ago and so it was with a sense of surprised delight that I learned
> from Gary Tubb (in conversation) that this was also the opinion of
> Daniel Ingalls, as well-read a classicist as he was a scholar of
> Sanskrit. I can only attribute my happening onto it to some
> unexplained instance of postmortem *saṃkrāntiyoga* on my fellow
> Virginian’s part. This perhaps goes some way towards explaining some
> of these authors’ favourable reception in modern Indology: I suspect
> that the high regard that European Indologists have had for Kalhaṇa
> and Somadeva rests as much in their undoubted poetic skills as it does
> in the Europeans’ unconscious recognition of and attraction to this
> feature.

Cox’s point is about Kalhaṇa, who wrote around 1148-49, and other contemporary or slightly earlier Kashmiri authors. So, it is definitely not about ‘earlier’ literature but the general point stands, I think.

The point of quoting this is not to criticize the mentioned scholars but to note that the sort of aesthetics that classical Sanskrit authors cultivated deliberately is, by and large, foreign even to us ‘natives’. I think, though I cannot prove it, that it is due to two reasons: first that this sort of aesthetics is largely an acquired taste and for those who haven’t been brought up in that tradition, it is strange and largely was strange even in its heydays. The second is that European influence has spread so much that anyone who doesn’t live in the Amazon or the Andamans has internalized them to some degree.

Obviously, the aesthetic of classical Sanskrit authors isn’t a single thing and its opposite, whether European or otherwise, isn’t a single thing either but speaking in generalities, I have found it to be true. I will explain what I mean by the ‘aesthetic’ of classical Sanskrit authors by some examples.

### On Translations and “Translations”



Paul J. Griffiths once lamented that the translations of Buddhist texts into English produced strange and, in his words, barbarous results. Said he[10](#footnote-10):

> Buddhist thought has a strange, and in many respects deplorable,
> effect upon language; in India it produced that barbaric language we
> usually call by the equally barbaric name of Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit,
> a language in which large numbers of long, repetitive, obscure, and
> subtle works were composed over a period of more than a thousand
> years. It forced the Tibetans to invent not only an alphabet but also
> what was in effect a new language, the most mechanical form of
> translationese which the world has yet seen. It managed to disturb
> even the severe balance and precise rhythms of classical Chinese. And
> it is now in process of wreaking its havoc upon the English language,
> creating a dialect comprehensible only to the initiate, written by and
> for Buddhologists, a dialect which has provided the title for this
> paper: Buddhist Hybrid English.

and

> Before we close this paper we should look at an example of Buddhist
> Hybrid English. It is not a phenomenon confined to graduate students
> or recent Ph.D. candidates, but something which afflicts the most
> mature scholars. Take this for instance:
>
> *.. . all dharmas are situated in permanence, ease, the self, the
> lovely; and likewise in impermanence, ill, not-self and the unlovely;
> in greed, hate, delusion, wrong views; for an entity made by false
> views does not exist, how can the false views themselves take place?
> For situated in Suchness are all dharmas, and from that situation they
> do not depart. And why? Because the coming and going of Suchness
> cannot be apprehended. And so for the Dharma-element, the
> Reality-limit, Sameness, the unthinkable element, and immobility.*
>
> This example of Buddhist Hybrid English was chosen pretty much at
> random from the late Edward Conze's translation of the
> *Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikāprajñāpāramitāsūtra*. This translation was
> originally published without notes or explanatory apparatus of any
> kind, and one cannot help but wonder if Dr. Conze ever thought about
> his audience. Non-Buddhologists, those who. have no Sanskrit and no
> training in the intricacies of the *prajñāpāramitā*, cannot possibly
> make any sense of it whatever. Dr. Conze's translation bears only the
> most tenuous relationship to the English language in terms of syntax,
> and is full of unexplained technical terminology; this much should be
> obvious even from the short extract quoted here. Its only advantage is
> that the Sanskrit original shines through with a fair amount of
> clarity; it isn't difficult for the Sanskritist to reconstruct the
> original. But it is precisely the expert who doesn't need a
> translation. He can read the original, and should prefer to do so.
>
> …
>
> I chose this example not because Dr. Conze's translations are worse
> than anyone else's; in fact they are better than most. Rather, it
> illustrates with a concrete example the kind of gibberish that is all
> too often produced by the Buddhological community in the sacred name
> of translation. I might add that still more striking examples of
> Buddhist Hybrid English could be adduced if we were to look at the
> results obtained by those who translate Tibetan texts.

Griffiths’ point is only in the field of the translations from religio-philosophical Buddhist works but this seems applicable to a large number of translations from Sanskrit and not only to English. There are generally three types of translations from Sanskrit which are, to some extent, separated by time. In the first place we have those archaizing Victorian translations with all the -eths and -ests and thou-s and thee-s which would have been quaint even to their contemporaries but are far more so for us now, but without the charm. I mostly like these but they are not to everyone’s taste.

### Playing with Language



A.B. Keith (1879 – 1944) is sometimes presented as a caricature of the type of colonial scholar who has nothing good to say about the objects of his study. Such a portrayal of Keith is indeed just caricature but it does have some basis. In his “A History of Sanskrit Literature”, it feels like he had nothing but hatred for all the poets after Kālidāsa as if they had personally wronged him. Still, his words in the preface to his work ring particularly true:[14](#footnote-14)

> The neglect of Sanskrit Kāvya is doubtless natural. The great poets of
> India wrote for audiences of experts; they were masters of the
> learning of their day, long trained in the use of language, and they
> aim to please by subtlety, not simplicity of effect. They had at their
> disposal a singularly beautiful speech, and they commanded elaborate
> and most effective metres. Under these circumstances it was inevitable
> that their works should be difficult, but of those who on that score
> pass them by it may fairly be said *ardua dum metuunt amittunt vera
> viai*. It is in the great writers of Kāvya alone, headed by Kālidāsa,
> that we find depth of feeling for life and nature matched with
> perfection of expression and rhythm. The Kāvya literature includes
> some of the great poetry of the world, but it can never expect to
> attain wide popularity in the West, for it is essentially
> untranslatable; German poets like Rückert can, indeed, base excellent
> work on Sanskrit originals, but the effects produced are achieved by
> wholly different means, while English efforts at verse translations
> fall invariably below a tolerable mediocrity, their diffuse tepidity
> contrasting painfully with the brilliant condensation of style, the
> elegance of metre, and the close adaptation of sound to sense of the
> originals. I have, therefore, as in my Sanskrit Drama, illustrated the
> merits of the poets by Sanskrit extracts, adding merely a literal
> English version, in which no note is taken of variations of text or
> renderings,.

This is true, at least in some measure, for all poetry that it cannot be translated but it is especially true for translation between languages of very different sort like Sanskrit and English. It would perhaps not be amiss to say that the only cases where translations of pre-modern poetry capture the modern reader’s attention is either if they are narrative poems or if the identity of the poet is itself something in vogue. If the story itself is something that the readers are interested in, the poems (usually epics) would not suffer too much in translation. If the author is from a marginalized section of the population, say women or sexual minorities, there would be interest in them. But for authors who are just elite men and whose poetry is notable only for the beauty of the language necessarily would suffer from being translated where the beauty of the language can hardly be preserved. I’m not saying that no translation is ever possible in such cases. That’s obviously not the case. But it is nonetheless true that the more the original is interested in sound effects or other poetic pyrotechnics, so to speak, the more trite translations tend to sound.

This is obviously the case with kāvya. Epigrams can be usually, though not always, translated well enough. But in the Sanskrit epic, the plot thread is, by modern standards, pitifully thin. They are above all characterized by beautiful language which, more or less, doesn’t preserve well in translation. It is only in a few cases that I have read a translation of Sanskrit kāvya and not thought to myself, “Wow! this sounds really stupid.”

I could give many examples about many sorts of tricks in the Sanskrit poet’s toolkit that are difficult to translate but I’ll just focus on one of those. There are many Sanskrit verses where the earlier lines lead one to believe one thing is being described while the last lines dramatically reverse it. An example, though not a very good one, is from the drama of Vatsarāja we had quoted above:

> Text within this block will maintain its original spacing when
> published
>
> ``` text
> śyāmā sadasṅgasubhagā karapaṅkajāgra-
> durlālanā viharamāṇamonojñakāmyā ।
> trailokyāvarmanṛpater manasi pramodam 
> āviṣkaroti karavālalatā na kāntā ॥
>
> Dark in colour, with all parts beautiful, to be caressed by lotus like hands and delightful as she moves around – The blade of a sword brings joy to the mind of Trailokyavarman, not beautiful women.
> ```

All the adjectives in the first two lines of the original (and before the ‘ – ’ in translation) are grammatically feminine and such as would be expected to be applied to beautiful women. But the last two lines undo all of that and make the reader realize suddenly that the adjectives do not describe a beautiful woman (kāntā) but the sword-blade (karavālalatā) which is grammatically feminine too.

And this is a fairly simple effect that may, by some skillful poet in English, be rendered to some degree in translation. Others, like those of long compounds or of śleṣa where a single verse refers to two or more meanings at the same time not metaphorically but literally, can hardly be translated without liberal use of parentheses or of footnotes which, however, tend to take the focus away from the beauty in language and make a puzzle out of a poem so that the effect achieved by the translation is quite different than that achieved by the original.

The Wikipedia [page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitextual_work) on śleṣa has a pretty good example:

>  Daṇḍin (fl. 7th–8th century) in his *Kāvyādarśa*, which is the
> earliest surviving systematic treatment of poetics in Sanskrit,
> illustrates the concept of *sleṣa* with the following verse:
>
> > असावुदयमारूढः कान्तिमान् रक्तमण्डलः ।
> >
> > राजा हरति लोकस्य हृदयं मृदुभिः करैः ॥
> >
> > asāv udayam ārūḍhah kāntimān raktamaṇḍalaḥ ।
> >
> > rājā harati lokasya hṛdayam mṛdubhiḥ karaiḥ ॥
>
> The meaning of this verse can be understood in two different ways as
> follows (translated):
>
> > - “This person has ascended the region of prosperity and is full of
> >   splendour; and the mandalas of districts are attached to him; and
> >   he, the king, captivates the hearts of the people by light taxes.”
> >
> > &nbsp;
> >
> > - “This resplendent moon has ascended the heavens and is full of
> >   splendour and is reddish (as he newly rises), and he captivates
> >   the people by soft rays.”
>
> These two interpretations arise due to the multiple meanings of words
> that appear in the verse. The word “*udaya*“ has several meanings. In
> the first interpretation, it refers to a king’s rise to power; in the
> second, it refers to the eastern mountain over which the moon ascends.
> Likewise, the word “*maṇḍala*“ translates to “circle”, like the moon’s
> disc, which is the meaning used in the second translation of the
> verse. However, it can also refer to a king’s circle of allies, which
> is the meaning chosen in the first translation. The word “*kara*“
> refers to taxes levied by a king, but it can also be translated as
> “\[the moon’s\] rays”. In a more general setting, such as in Sanskrit
> traditions, the moon itself is imagined as the king of the stars.

Yigal Bronner’s “Extreme Poetry: The South Asian Movement of Simultaneous Narration”[15](#footnote-15) is, as far as I know, the definitive treatment of śleṣa that exists in English. In the very first pages, he gives a pretty interesting comparison to show how this śleṣa or bitextuality would work:

> Imagine a poem of large or even epic proportions, say, the Iliad. Now
> try to imagine that the language of this poem is constructed in such a
> way that it simultaneously tells an entire additional story. Suppose,
> in other words, that each verse of the Iliad could simultaneously be
> read as narrating the Odyssey as well. It is hard to imagine that
> language could sustain such an effort and still be intelligible, let
> alone beautiful. We can conceive of punned words or even proverbial
> utterances that are doubly readable, such as “Gladly the cross- eyed
> bear” for “gladly the cross I’d bear,” but a large- scale poem that is
> consistently “bitextual” seems inconceivable.
>
> Now try to imagine the effort required to put together such a work. As
> a preliminary step, the poet would probably need to go through a whole
> set of dictionaries and systematically record all homonyms (e.g.,
> cross, bear). Our poet would also do well to list as many homophones
> as possible (eyed/I’d; night/knight), which an ordinary dictionary
> would not indicate. In addition, the poet might need to study special
> lexicons of scientific or other jargon, because the daunting task of
> making every line in a text convey two different meanings may force
> him or her to draw on less- than- common linguistic registers. The
> author would also have to gain a perfect knowledge of syntax and its
> possible ambiguities (e.g., “visiting relatives can be tedious,” where
> “visiting” can be either a verbal noun with “relatives” as its object
> or an adjective modifying “relatives”), as well as the intricacies of
> grammar. And, of course, he or she would have to be very familiar with
> phonetics, because it is useful in the creation of homophonous
> utterances (e.g., “the stuff y nose can lead to problems” for “the
> stuff he knows . . .”). Only then could the poet attempt a merging of
> the two epics— word by word, scene by scene.
>
> Even if there were a person qualified to compose such a bitextual
> poem— a master linguist, philologist, literature specialist, and
> gifted poet in one— it would be far from easy to establish a
> readership for it. The decoding of such poetry would require a reader
> just as knowledgeable as and no less capable than the poet. The reader
> would have to master the same dictionaries and lexicons as the poet
> and go through the same linguistic and literary training. He or she
> would have to be an equal partner in the act of making double sense of
> a single text.
>
> However, it is not just the immense difficulty of composing and
> reading such poetry that makes it so hard to imagine. The very idea
> seems alien to modern aesthetic values and to our notions of how
> literature should be enjoyed and how language works. Why, one might
> ask, would poets invest such effort in composing a bitextual poem? Why
> would readers take the trouble to read it? What possible enjoyment
> could one find in the conarration of the Iliad with the Odyssey
> besides marveling at the actual feat of combining them?
>
> At the very least, it is difficult to imagine that such poetry would
> be the result of a sudden, inexplicable burst of creative energy. Had
> we been asked to believe that a few dozen Iliad-Odyssey works actually
> existed, we could only assume that they were the product of prolonged
> cultivation by a large group of authors, readers, language
> specialists, and critics. Only then could we envision a variety of
> bitextual works, including not just double- epic poems but also, say,
> “an Iliad where every line and every word should bear a secondary
> reference to Napoleon’s campaign in Upper Italy.”
>
> In South Asia the phenomenon I have described here does, in fact,
> exist. The creation, consumption, and study of doubled texts using the
> literary device called ślesa was a robust literary movement that
> lasted over 1,000 years throughout the Indian subcontinent. It is
> primarily associated with Sanskrit, but it existed in several other
> languages as well. *Śleṣa* was used for many purposes, but most
> productively to conarrate the two great South Asian epics, the
> *Rāmāyaṇa* and the *Mahābhārata*. But despite the central place this
> phenomenon occupied within the Sanskrit tradition, the existence of
> *ślesa* is wholly uncharted in modern scholarship. It is often ignored
> or deplored and at times even denied by researchers. Some of its
> manifestations are treated as if they never existed, while others are
> presented as the result of a sudden outburst of individual creativity
> that requires no explanation. On the whole, it is a phenomenon viewed
> today as too peculiar to be taken seriously and, at the same time,
> something natural to India. It is an aberration, but it is also
> normal.

### Language and Jargon



Even a very surface level walk through the ‘Sanskrit Canon’ must have some notice of both technical subjects such as Grammar, Metrics, Maths, Music, Philosophy and so on which form a major part of the surviving literature. But the problem is that these subjects, especially philosophy, are filled with field-specific terminologies and more or less assume that the reader is already knowledgeable about the subject, only that he subscribes to one view or the author that our author at hand wants to argue for or against.

Let’s take grammar as an example. Bloomfield, for example, says this of Pāṇini[16](#footnote-16):

> We find the Hindu grammarians extending their interest from the
> Scriptures to the upper-caste language, and making rules and lists of
> forms descriptive of the correct t3rpe of speech, which they called
> *Sanskrit*. In time they worked out a systematic arrangement of
> grammar and lexicon. Generations of such labor must have preceded the
> writing of the oldest treatise that has come down to us, the grammar
> of Panini. This grammar, which dates from somewhere round 350 to 250
> b.c., is one of the greatest monuments of human intelligence. It
> describes, with the minutest detail, every inflection, derivation, and
> composition, and every syntactic usage of its author’s speech. No
> other language, to this day, has been so perfectly described. It may
> have been due, in part, to this excellent codification that Sanskrit
> became, in time, the official and literary language of all of Brahmin
> India. Long after it had ceased to be spoken as anyone’s native
> language, it remained (as classical Latin remained in Europe) the
> artificial medium for all writing on learned or religious topics.

Pāṇini’s grammar really is, as Bloomfield says, one of the greatest monuments of human intelligence. Every major author after Pāṇini’s time up to the modern period knew him more or less by heart. In the thousands upon thousands of commentaries upon a all sort of topics written in the medieval period, commentators quote him more or less every other line. But if a reader today wants to just get a sense of what it is about, he cannot just open the Āṣṭādhyāyī and read it like he would read a modern grammar. Even if our hypothetical reader has a perfect command of Sanskrit, he would still not understand a single thing. It is an extreme example of the sūtra (lit. thread) genre where everything is condensed to the shortest text possible so that the result is more like equations than sentences. There is even an ancient joke to the effect that a sūtra-writer is as glad after compressing his sūtra down to one syllable less as he is by the news of the birth of his son. The end effect is that the expert will say something to the effect of “Trust me bro, Pāṇini is some big-brain stuff” and you have just shrug and say, “I guess so” unless you want to spend years learning just Pāṇini’s grammar.

The same thing applies to philosophy. Unlike say, Plato’s dialogues which are a joy to read even for a beginner, most of Sanskrit philosophy is written in a more, let’s say, scholastic style which is aimed at an already proficient audience. The intended audience for most of them was the equivalent to professors today and thus are definitely technical. The following is, for example, an extract from a popular introduction to all major philosophies written in the1350s. The extract is from early in the first chapter about the Cārvāka materialists who do not accept inference as an acceptable means of proof (pramāṇa).[17](#footnote-17)

> “Be it so,” says the opponent; “your wish would be gained if
> inference, &c., had no force of proof; but then they have this force;
> else, if they had not, then how, on perceiving smoke, should the
> thoughts of the intelligent immediately proceed to fire; or why, on
> hearing another say, ‘There are fruits on the bank of the river,’ do
> those who desire fruit proceed at once to the shore?”
>
> All this, however, is only the inflation of the world of fancy.
>
> Those who maintain the authority of inference accept the *sign* or
> middle term as the causer of knowledge, which middle term must be
> found in the minor and be itself invariably connected with the major.
> Now this invariable connection must be a relation destitute of any
> condition accepted or disputed; and this connection does not possess
> its power of causing inference by virtue of its *existence*, as the
> eye, &c., are the cause of perception, but by virtue of its being
> *known*. What then is the means of this connection’s being known?
>
> We will first show that it is not *perception*. Now perception is held
> to be of two kinds, external and internal \[*i.e.*, as produced by the
> external senses, or by the inner sense, mind\]. The former is not the
> required means; for although it is possible that the actual contact of
> the senses and the object will produce the knowledge of the particular
> object thus brought in contact, yet as there can never be such contact
> in the case of the past or the future, the universal proposition which
> was to embrace the invariable connection of the middle and major terms
> in every case becomes impossible to be known. Nor may you maintain
> that this knowledge of the universal proposition has the general class
> as its object, because if so, there might arise a doubt as to the
> existence of the invariable connection in this particular case \[as,
> for instance, in this particular smoke as implying fire\].

For philosophers already in the know, this may not be difficult but for us laymen, it quickly becomes confusing unless learning with a teacher or with a copious amount of annotation. Dialogues are much easier to comprehend, especially when there is a dramatic situation built-in. I think this does, to a certain extent at least, explain the relative popularity of the Bhāgavadgīta or the early Buddhist sutta-s in Pāḷi.

### Some Political Musings



The people who will have any significant degree of interest in the ‘Sanskrit Canon’, as I have called it, will inevitably be Hindus and, to a lesser extent, Jains. The likelihood that these works will ever be studied and cherished in Pakistan or Bangladesh as long as the idolaters have any significant demographic presence or political power is effectively zero. The probability any of these would breach containment from university walls in the West to reach significant readership is, though perhaps higher than in case of Pakistan or Bangladesh, very low too. Maybe the East Asians would be interested in the more Buddhist stuff but hardly much else.

The long and short of it is that the only people who care will ultimately be Hindus. There are only two Hindu majority countries with a substantial population - Nepal and India. Nepal is a poor country by even poor country standards with no sign that it’s going to change anytime soon and, what’s more, we Nepalese aren’t, to put it mildly, much given to intellectual pursuits (Lynn says the IQ is 42, so maybe that’s why ,lol).

I would assume the rank and file are mostly reasonable people but sky will probably fall before the elite of this category, more like brown Westerners than South Asians, stop slavishly repeating second-hand from some White scholar, either current or colonial, managing to mangle even them in the process. I really don’t know what leads them to act like this. One might be tempted to say leftist ideology but there are a ton of those in Nepal as well (and far, far more doctrinal communists, leninists, stalinists and maoists in actual ruling parties) who are certainly not as anti-Hindu as those in India. Even strident leftist scholars in Europe or USA at least produce decent to great scholarship but the scholarship from the Indian Academia in humanities is consistently bad.

The diaspora leftists aren’t much better. The little time they have from marching in solidarity with Palestine or lecturing about the intrinsic evil of whiteness, they will spend explaining how the racist insults against Indians are actually okay because something something Modi, something Fascism, caste and so on. As the politics in much of the West in the coming times is going to be dominated either by Leftists toying with Islamism or by Ethno-nationalists of one variety or the other, I don’t know how they will fare. For the Islamist any idol-worshipping kafir will remain so no matter how much they praise Islam or produce atrocity porn against ~~Hindus~~, sorry, Caste system and Hindutva fascists. Leftists will accuse them for supporting fascism the moment they deviate from their script[18](#footnote-18). As for the white ethno-nationalists, one can just scroll Twitter, or even parts of Substack for that matter, for less than five minutes to see what love they have for Indians.

The right, either institutionally or individually, is who you would expect put some effort into promoting the “Sanskrit Canon”. They do, to a certain extent, but they have an outstanding number of people with, to put it mildly, pseudo-scientific views among them. And what is more, they are extremely loud mouthed on the internet, often combining retarded pseudoscience and pseudohistory with a rather poor grasp of English. People who have not an ounce of knowledge of Sanskrit screeching in comments to people who obviously do. And don’t even get me started on the ‘Aryan Invasion Theory’ stupidity. In any case, I don’t think anything good is going to happen anytime soon on any side.

### For the Next Time



At some twelve thousand words, this post has already gotten far longer than I had intended and I have still not said all that I wanted to. I haven’t made any list myself. I haven’t tackled the question of whether even reading in translation is advisable at all or whether people should be encouraged to learn the language themselves. There are a lot of other paths one could take. A particularly interesting one is what ideas of ‘canon’ or some equivalent thereof were floating in actual ancient and medieval times. I think the question is very interesting but it would take quite a lot of space. Maybe I will tackle them someday. Maybe not. I’m lazy and not particularly intelligent. So, we’ll see, I guess.
