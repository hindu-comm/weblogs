In a previous post, I had said:

> There are other matters that contribute to the same effect of
> foreignizing the taste, like the use of very long compounds or the use
> of ridiculous beauty standards, that I will not discuss in detail
> here, except just a single example. The heroine in many works is so
> described that she has breasts so large and a waist so thin that she
> cannot physically walk with a straight frame.

Now, I’m not going to explain myself in detail here but it is true, generally speaking, that the world of Kāvya, even in subjects where love isn’t the main topic, is filled with a sort of eroticism that is hard to put into words. Sometimes it has been taken to imply that people in ancient times really were sexually liberated. The sort of people who say this often also have let’s say interesting ideas about how the supposed liberation came to change. In any case, I don’t think that is true. The poems, or even the sculptures, are the works of a narrow elite and definitely not representative of actual society which was, and remained, conservative. As for the erotic atmosphere of the poetry, I would say that the poets just don’t consider the love or relation between a man and a woman[1](#footnote-1) to be an unnatural thing or anything to be ashamed of.

In a previous instance, I had said the following in introduction which, more or less, is accurate for the present instance too:

> Erotic Poetry (śṛṅgāra) might be the only genre of Sanskrit poetry
> that is well represented in English translations. Andrew Schelling’s
> translations include mostly erotic poems, Parthaswamy’s *Erotic Poems
> from the Sanskrit* and Anusha Rao’s *How to Love in Sanskrit* tread
> the same ground as well. Dāmodaragupta’s Kuṭṭanīmata has got a new
> English translation. Major collections like Amarukaśataka,
> Caurapañcāśikā, Śṛṅgāraśataka, etc. have been translated multiple
> times into English.
>
> Humorous and obscene verses seem to be much less covered. Of course,
> one might say that the erotic verses are often very explicit and might
> be considered obscene. The erotic verses are, however, highly stylized
> and are the works of urban and upper class poets. Although they often
> depict amorous villagers, the poets are anything but that. Indeed,
> among the critics *grāmya* (village-like) is often used to mean
> ‘vulgar’ or ‘obscene’.
>
> Well, what counts as ‘vulgar’ or ‘obscene’? After all what sounds
> vulgar to us today might not be the same things that were so to the
> authors themselves. This is not a good place to go into depth on such
> issues and I’ll just state the obvious. I have no specific criteria
> for what is ‘vulgar’ and what is not but as U.S. Justice Stewart
> remarked about pornographic materials: *I know it when I see it*.
>
> The verses translated here are mainly collect from the well-known
> medieval anthologies *Śārṅgadharapaddhati* (1363 CE), *Sūktimuktāvalī*
> (1257 CE), *Subhāṣitāvalī* (15th Century CE) and *Subhāṣitaratnakośa*
> (12th Century CE). When the verses are attributed to individual poets,
> I have indicated their names and the rest may be considered anonymous.
> As I’m not a poet ( not in English anyway) and my versification skills
> are as wonderful as that of Cicero, no effort has been taken in
> translating into verse. I’ll translate each stanza separately and note
> anything that sticks out. Many of the verses are pretty
> straightforward but many contain puns or double entendres that are
> common in Sanskrit tradition.

## 10

``` text kākāl laulyaṃ yamāt krauryaṃ sthapater nityaghātitām । ādyākṣarāṇi saṃgṛhya kāyasthaḥ kena nirmitaḥ ॥

Subhāṣitāvalī 2324. Kṣemendra.

Taking fickleness from crows, cruelty from death and regular beatings from a construction-worker, and taking the first letter from their names as well, who even first created the bureaucrat at all? ```

This one is by Kṣemendra. The first chapter of his Narmamālā is basically a long rant against the kāyastha-s. Kāyastha literally means scribe but it is clear from the context that his ire is against the state officials and I have used the word ‘bureaucrat’ as the modern equivalent. Here the word kā-ya-tha is derived from the first letters of **kā**ka (crow), **ya**ma (death) and **stha**pati (craftsman). The beatings of the craftsman is the one he does during work not that he is beaten himself.

## 23



Text within this block will maintain its original spacing when published

``` text keśolluñchanasāmye’pi hanta paśyai’tad antaram । upasthāḥ kīṭam aśnanti ghṛtabhaktaṃ digambarāḥ ॥

Sūktimuktāvalī 89.18. Anonymous.

Though both are shaved clean, see the difference between the two. Pussies feed on cum while the monks eat rice mixed with butter. ```

I’m not sure of the meaning of this verse and haven’t seen a translation anywhere. The first line is clear but the part with ‘upasthāḥ kīṭam aśnanti” is unclear to me. It would literally mean something like “The upastha-s eat insects”. Now, “upstha” would etymologically mean “that which lies near” and generally means “loins”. Maybe one could take it literally as meaning “laymen” as opposed to the “monks” but why would the laymen eat insects at all? I think the textual transmission is corrupted here. In fact there are other readings in the second line and I’ve chosen the one that made sense the most. I think the “insect” is an euphemism for the translation I’ve given but it is possible that my understanding of the verse is wrong. As I said, I’ve not seen a translation of this verse and if any reader has a better interpretation of his own or has seen a translation elsewhere, I would encourage them to comment.

The monk is “digambara” or “sky-clothed”. This is used for a sect of Jain monks who don’t wear clothes to not harm even small insects and such things.

My hair has become quite white. My face has three wrinkle-lines. My teeth have all but fallen away but I don’t care about that at all. But when gazelle-eyed young women see me in the street and say “Father”, it’s like I’ve been hit by a thunderbolt. ```

One foot has already crossed the doors of death and this is what you’re concerned about?

``` text kāka āhvayate kākān yācako na tu yācakān । kākayācakayormadhye varaṃ kāko na yācakaḥ ॥

Mahāsubhāṣitasṅgraha 9290.

A crow calls out other crows to him but a beggar doesn’t call out other beggars. Between a beggar and a crow, the crow is better, certainly not the beggar. ```

True? Maybe.

## 32

``` text kāminyāḥ kucadurgaparvatabhuvi tvaṃ mā manaḥpānthaka saṃcāraṃ kuru romarājigahane tatrā’sti nābhyāṃ guhā । tallīno madhusūdanasya tanayas tenā’tra caureṇa bho nirvastrīkriyate divā’pi hi naro rātrau tu kiṃ kathyate ॥

Mahāsubhāṣitasaṅgraha 9654

O mind-traveller, don’t go wandering in a young woman’s breast-mountain-forts, a deep navel-cave is located there just above the pubic-hair-forests. The son of Madhu’s killer lurks there. And he strips a man naked even during the day; do I even need to mention the night? ```

A longer version of the same trope as in No.25 above.

“son of Madhu’s killer” is the god of love. “romarāji” more literally means “mons veneris”.

## 33



Text within this block will maintain its original spacing when published

``` text ṛjunā nidhehi caraṇau parihara sakhi nikhilanāgarācāram । iha ḍākinīti pallīpatiḥ kaṭākṣe’pi daṇḍayati ॥

Mahāsubhāṣitasaṅgraha 7339.

Place your feet straight, my friend, and abandon your urban ways here. The village chief here punishes as a witch even for a sidelong glance. ```

“Leave your degeneracy in the cities, we burn witches here” - the village chief, probably.

## 34



Text within this block will maintain its original spacing when published

``` text kaviḥ karoti kāvyāni paṇḍito vetti tadrasam । kāminīkucakāṭhinyaṃ patirjānāti no pitā ॥

Mahāsubhāṣitasaṅgraha 9097.

A poet composes poems but connoisseur enjoys the poems’ flavours. Only the husband knows how firm a woman’s tits are, not her father. ```

Not false but did you really need to put it this way? As a matter of fact, there are a lot of variations for the second line of the original with things like “hand picks up the food but the tongue knows the flavour” and so on.

``` text kiṃ tayā kriyate lakṣmyā yā vadhūr iva kevalā । yā na veśyeva sāmānyā pathikair upabhujyate ॥

What use is the wealth that is only like a good wife and not like a whore who is enjoyed in common? ```

How do such comparisons even enter a person’s mind?

``` text akṣarāṇi samānāni vartulāni ghanāni ca । parasparavilagnāni taruṇīkucakumbhavat ॥

Mahāsubhāṣitasaṅgraha 143

The letters should be of the same size, rounded, dense and joined to one another: like the jar-breasts of young women. ```

Again, the same thing. Maybe it’s just me but I’ve never looked at my handwriting and thought “You know what it looks like? Boobs!”

## 37



Text within this block will maintain its original spacing when published

``` text akṛśaṃ nitambabhāge kṣāmaṃ madhye samunnataṃ kucayoḥ । atyāyataṃ nayanayor mama jīvitam etad āyāti ॥

Mahāsubhāṣitasaṅgraha 114

My life is coming towards me - unslim of ass, thin of waist, large of breasts and long of eyes. ```

Objectification, quite literally. Also, I’m quite intrigued that the length of eyes appear again and again as a mark of beauty. Unlike say the Himalayan areas, there probably weren’t many people with East Asian eyes, so why the idea of long eyes so prominent, I don’t know.

## 38

``` text aṃśukena jaghanaṃ tirodadhe kañcukena ca kucau mṛgīdṛśām । pīyamānam aniśaṃ priyekṣaṇaiḥ kṣāmatām iva jagāma madhyamam ॥

Mahāsubhāṣitasaṅgraha 004

The deer-eyed beauties cover their tits with kañcuka and their loins with aṃśuka. So their waists, drunk daily by the glances of men, seem to grow thinner and thinner. ```

A “kañcuka” is a cloth worn in the upper body and an “aṃśuka” a silken-cloth worn in the lower body. Maybe those, like say a sarī, didn’t cover the waist. And as I have said earlier, the beauty standards favoured the waist to be as slim as possible. Some sculptures are so over the top they look like those poorly edited Instagram models.

``` text ślāghyaṃ nīrasakāṣṭhatāḍanaśataṃ ślāghyaḥ pracaṇdātapaḥ kleśaḥ ślāghyataraḥ supaṅkanicayaiḥ ślāghyo’tidāho’nilaiḥ । yatkāntākucapārśvabāhulatikāhindolalīlāsukhaṃ labdhaṃ kumbhavara tvayā nahi sukhaṃ duḥkhair vinā labhyate ॥

Śṛṅgāratilaka. pseudo-Kālidāsa.

It was good being hit a hundred times by dry wood; the extreme heat applied was good; it was good being covered by layers of thick mud; the intense wind-blasts were good. All of it was good, dear jar, since it means that now you enjoy swinging with creeper-arms and pressing against the tits of the beautiful woman. There’s no comfort without suffering. ```

If the pot could read, he’d be happy, I guess.

pseudo-Kālidāsa. Śṛṅgāratilaka.

A bizarre sort of fire can be seen in the breasts of beautiful women. They burn your limbs from afar but, when pressed, make them cool. ```
