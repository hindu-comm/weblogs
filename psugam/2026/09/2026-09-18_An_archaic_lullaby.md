Though the vast majority of the 1018 hymns included in the *Ṛgveda* are, expectedly, religious and ritual in nature, there are a few that are not. Some scholars have used the word ‘secular’ in reference to them. I do not think ‘secular’ is a good descriptor for this. I would characterize them as ‘not explicitly religious’ rather than ‘secular’. In any case, there are a few of these poems.

The one translated here is *Ṛgveda* VII.55 attributed to Vasiṣṭha Maitrāvaruṇi. The quarrels of Vasiṣṭha with Viśvāmitra is already present in this early time which give rise to a whole series of mythical cycle in later times. Even in the Ṛgveda itself there are grave accusations between them (including that of being sorcerer). Durga, who wrote a commentary on Yāska’s great work on etymology *Nirukta* sometime before ~500 AD, still refuses to comment on one of the words there because, in his words, “The context in which this word occurs is an anti-Vasiṣṭha hymn and I am from Vasiṣṭha’s line in the Kapiṣṭhala branch. So, I will not provide its etymology.” Well, I’m not from the family of Vasiṣṭha (or of Viśvāmitra for that matter) but I too will pass over that in silence here.

Though there have been other interpretations over the years, the most natural seems to take it at face value as a sleep charm. Some verses included here also occur in the *Atharvaveda* in IV.5 (*Śaunaka* Branch) or IV.6 (*Paippalāda* Branch) but in a quite different context there.

I’ll provide some notes after the text itself but more or less the only ones needed for the understanding of the translation is that “Saramā’s son” means “dog” and the “thousand-horned bull” refers to the “moon”. You can read, and hear etc., the original [here](https://vedaweb.uni-koeln.de/texts/rv/browse/66695ac77dbde8fe2678a119).

### TRANSLATION



Text within this block will maintain its original spacing when published

``` text Dwelling-lord, illness-remover, 
you who have entered all forms be as a propitious friend to us.  1

When you bare your teeth, 
white-brown son of Saramā, 
they glitter like spearpoints in jaws of the one who snaps.
        Go to sweet sleep.  2

Bark at the thief, Saramā’s son, or at the robber, run back at him. You’re barking at Indra’s singers. Why are you bothering us?
        Go to sweet sleep.  3

You should frighten the porcupine and let the porcupine frighten you. You’re barking at Indra’s singers. Why are you bothering us?
        Go to sweet sleep.   4

Sleep mother, sleep father. Sleep dog and sleep clanlord. Sleep all of you kinsmen sleep everyone who is around.  5

Of him who sits and who walks and of him who looks at us, we close the eyes of them all as we close this house of ours.  6

The thousand-horned bull who rose up from the sea, with that mighty one’s help we lull them down to sleep.    7

Bench-sleepers, couch-sleepers, the ladies who sleep in beds too, the women with sweet scents, we lull them all down to sleep.  8 ```

### SOME NOTES



#### Verse 1



- Dwelling-lord’ translates vāstoṣ pate “lord of the inhabited
  building/dwelling”. Name of a god.

#### **Verse 2**



- ‘son of Saramā’ means ‘dog’ as I said before. Saramā is the mythical
  mother of the dogs.

- Two colour words ‘white’ and ‘brown’ are used in vocatives for the dog
  in different lines that I have joined together.

- There is a very interesting dialogue in X.108 between Saramā and a
  group of demonic robbers called Paṇis. These Paṇis have stolen the
  cattle of the gods and have hidden them in a cave beyond the probably
  mythical river Rasā. The gods, with Indra at their head, send Saramā
  to find the cattle. The Paṇis first threaten and then try to bribe
  Saramā to not report them to Indra but Saramā rejects their words and
  reports back to Indra. In a later version of this story (in
  *Jaiminīyabrāhmaṇa* 440) Indra says to her, “I make your descendants
  grain-eaters, Saramā, because you found our cattle.” meaning that
  humans will care for them. The author then adds the remark “Even today
  the Mācāla-dogs among the Vidarbha-s even kill tigers.”

- The river Rasā seems to a legendary river at the end of the world. A
  cognate word is used for a river in Avestan too and it is possible
  that it was once the name of the river Volga among the Indo-Iranians.

- The ‘one who snaps’ is the dog being addressed.

#### Verse 4



- “You should frighten the porcupine”. The word that I have translated
  as ‘frighten’ literally means ‘to tear apart’ and has been translated
  by many as ‘bite’ or ‘bark’ and so on.

- The word translated as ‘porcupine’ is ‘sūkara’. It is either analyzed
  as descending from the Indo-European word for ‘pig’ + the suffix
  ‘kara’ or alternatively as ‘sū’ sound + kara ‘maker’ as ‘something
  that makes the sound sū’. In either case, it usually meant ‘pig’ or
  ‘boar’ from the first millennium BC onwards. But in some early texts
  ‘varāha’ and ‘sūkara’ are deliberately contrasted. Alexander Lubotsky,
  in an article published just this year, derives, I think convincingly,
  the word derives from an Indo-European word for ‘porcupine’ and the
  merger with ‘boar’ or ‘pig’ happened later. Read the paper
  [here](https://www.academia.edu/175651644/The_meaning_and_etymology_of_Skt_s%C5%ABkar%C3%A1_).

#### Verse 6



- “we close the eyes” in the original means something like “we strike
  the eyes” but the example of the house shows what is meant.

#### Verse 7



- “the thousand-horned bull” means the ‘moon’ as I said above.
  Interestingly, in Vedic the normal word for the moon is still ‘mās’
  which is inherited from the Indo-European word for the moon (and
  cognate with the English ‘moon’ too). But it also shows up as a
  compound ‘candramas’ or ‘glittering moon’ and ultimately just ‘candra’
  or ‘glittering’. The word for the moon in many New Indo-Aryan language
  comes from the ‘glitter’ part and not from the ‘moon’ part.

- ‘lull them down to sleep’. The original ones use the causative form of
  the verb for sleeping to mean something like ‘we make them sleep’.

#### Verse 8



- Two words for women are used in this verse, one has been translated as
  ‘ladies’ and one as ‘women’.

- ‘the women with sweet scents’. I don’t know who they are or whether
  they are different from the ladies already mentioned as sleeping in
  various places.
