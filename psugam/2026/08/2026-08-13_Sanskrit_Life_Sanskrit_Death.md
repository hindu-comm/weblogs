# Sanskrit Life, Sanskrit Death

### A Rant.

If you search on Google when Nepal was founded, you’ll probably see the date of 1768. Is this, you might wonder, because a new dynasty took over then? No, the same dynasty ruled then which was founded in 1559 and would rule afterwards until 2008. Is this because the system of governance changed? No, the same king who had started his reign in 1742 was ruling then and would do so until 1775.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!uHka!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcd00a723-6396-4663-a3fb-af97ff3be263_398x217.png)

The reason, if reason it may be called, is that between 1768 and 1769, Gorkha, which had been trying to do this from 1744, finally conquered the three city-kingdoms in what then was called the valley of Nepal. The date of 25 September 1768 that the Wikipedia list shows in the image above is of the final battle of Kathmandu which has since the early 19th century been the capital. I think 1768 is not a very good date for the ‘formation’ of Nepal and that better dates could be suggested. That, however, is not my intention here.

What I found more interesting is that there are only two contemporary literary treatments of these victories written by a person who had actually witnessed the events firsthand. Both are, not coincidentally, in Sanskrit. There is, of course, quite a volume of contemporary correspondence, official documents, inscriptions, coins and so on from which these events could be reconstructed with a reasonable degree of accuracy even in the absence of narrative sources. This is the 1760s after all not the 760s[1](#footnote-1). The Chronicles, in Nepali, do cover these events in detail but most of these were written in the early to mid 1800s when even the youngest eyewitness must have been at death’s door, if not far past it.

The two Sanskrit poems are both by the same author, Lalitāvallabha Aryāla, and were composed soon after the events themselves in early 1770. One is called *Bhaktavijayakāvya* (The Victory over Bhaktapura) and the second *Pṛthvīndravarṇodaya* (The description of the rise of Pṛthvīnārāyaṇa). I am actually quite fond of the first one. It is in rhymed *upajāti* verses that I quite like. It is, of course, propagandistic but the portrayal of the most determined antagonist, King Jayaprakāśa of Kathmandu, is quite positive and not without a certain pathos.

Considering that these poems are the contemporary depictions of such a crucial point of history, it would be expected that there would be great interest in them. But, alas, this is not so. There are a couple editions with Nepali translation but these were published in the 1950s to 70s and are very difficult to get a hand on nowadays. They are sometimes referenced in contemporary historical works but I get the impression that unlike the twentieth-century historians, contemporary ones are working either through the translations or at second hand. I could, of course, be wrong but that is my impression. There are neither editions nor translations in any other language, as far as I know, and even simple mentions are rare, not to speak of any sort of criticism.

These works are not some one-off aberrations. Śaktivallabha Aryāla, from the same family as Lalitāvallabha and his contemporary, wrote more than half a dozen Sanskrit works beside those in Nepali. Another of their contemporaries, Lakṣmaṇa, wrote a very peculiar book which is basically a transcription of a poetry competition held sometimes in 1769. There are many dozens of poets who participate and for each the judges either praise or criticize their verses. One of the three judges, coincidentally, is Śaktivallabha himself.

From only those Sanskrit works which were composed in Nepal between 1700 to 1900, I counted around a hundred works that have been published. And this is only the tip of the iceberg of all that survive.

Now, even if we assume that there only some 10% of what survives has been published (a very conservative estimate), then it would imply some 1000 works in the time period and boundaries under consideration. This is, of course, not a very substantial corpus when we judge by modern standards. But I think it is pretty substantial when we keep in mind that it is in Sanskrit, supposedly long dead, in a part of the world where we are, to put it mildly, are not particularly known for our literary leanings. If the places under consideration were those which were centers of traditional learning, the corpus would undoubtedly be much, much larger.

I post translations of Sanskrit verses quite often here on Substack. The verses are sometimes ancient, sometimes medieval and even modern - even some of my own, rather bad, attempts at versification. But are these all in more or less the same language or are there significant differences between them, either due to natural evolution of languages over time or due to dialectal differences or both?

People who are familiar with the history of Sanskrit literature would probably answer by saying that after ~400 BCE (i.e. after the work of the great grammarian Pāṇini) or so all the Sanskrit texts with any sort of literary pretension are in what they call “Classical Sanskrit”. The same people would also tell you that before this regularization of language, the language is usually referred to as Vedic whose earliest specimens, from mid to late second millennium BCE, are recognizably an earlier form of the same language but is quite different in many aspects nonetheless.

Things are, of course, more complicated once you take a deeper look at it but all of this is more or less true. The division into “Classical” and “Vedic” is also more or less the same, though not exactly, as the traditional classification into *laukika* (i.e. Worldly or Quotidian) and *chāndasa* (i.e. metrical) that has existed in some form since Pāṇini himself. The Quotidian, we would today say, is the Classical language and the Metrical is the Vedic.

Calling the language standardized by Pāṇini and the subsequent grammarians as Classical Sanskrit is not exactly wrong but I have always disliked this naming. The languages generally deemed classical have a certain time and place associated with them. The Latin that is called classical was the language of the Roman elite during the late republic and immediately after. The literature of that time and place set the standard for ‘correct’ Latin for millennia afterward. The greatest names associated with Latin literature lived and wrote then: Caesar, Cicero, Vergil and others. Similar things could be said of Attic Greek of the age of Athenian democracy.

But the same is not true for Classical Sanskrit. We hardly even know who spoke the language described by Pāṇini[2](#footnote-2). We hardly have any literature from the people who supposedly spoke this language. Maybe a few late *brāhmaṇa*-s and *upaniṣad*-s are close. Well, people still read the *upaniṣad*-s nowadays but I would be surprised if there were even a handful of people learn Sanskrit to read *Gopathabrāhmaṇa* or something. Maybe calling it something like “Literary Sanskrit”, like how Classical Chinese is often called Literary Chinese, would be better. Unfortunately, I don’t have any concrete suggestion for any alternative name otherwise.

The great age of Sanskrit literature, the *classical* age if you will, is often held to be 300-1200CE for reasons that can only be called ridiculous. The earlier date is chosen due to the rise of the Gupta Empire, when the epigraphy starts becoming overwhelmingly in Sanskrit. The problem is that Gupta epigraphic corpus isn’t particularly large or impressive:

> The primary reference point for any discussion of empire in
> first-millennium India is the Gupta formation (founded c. 320). But
> what data from the imperial Guptas themselves are actually available
> to us? The records issued in the name of the kings and queens of this
> dynasty, including seals, consist of a grand total of twenty-odd
> fragmentary documents and hardly more than 250 lines of printed text;
> from the founder of the lineage himself, Candragupta, we have nothing;
> from Samudragupta, only four records. Adding to these all the
> documents produced by those who directly or indirectly declared their
> subordination to the Guptas, we can double the number, and if we
> include two dozen recently published copperplate texts, another three
> hundred lines. All told perhaps a thousand lines of text (some of
> which are only partial or simply repeat genealogical information)
> constitute the entire direct textual basis of our knowledge of the
> nearly three centuries of Gupta imperial rule—thin gruel indeed, even
> if these calculations are off by a factor of two or three or more.
>
> -Sheldon Pollock’s *The Language of the Gods in the World of Men.* pg.
> 250.

To put this into perspective, individual rulers in later times, like say *Vikramāditya*-*VI* or *Kṛṣṇadevarāya*, have an inscriptional corpus larger than centuries of the Gupta empire combined. I am pretty sure the volume of Sanskrit inscription in Nepal, as late as 1700s and 1800s, is larger than that of the Gupta empire. The same could be said of the literary corpus. Combine all the works of literature supposedly patronized by the Gupta emperors, even the edge cases, and you will get a corpus smaller than even of individual authors from later times.

The case for the end date of 1200CE is even stupider. As northern India came into the control of Muslim rulers of the Delhi Sultanate starting in 1191CE, this would supposedly end the court patronage for Sanskrit authors. This is true enough but new texts were still produced continuously. And that is without counting South India which didn’t come into any sort of Islamic authority until the 1600s which even then was fleeting. Nepal, and much of the Himalayan belt, never came into Islamic subjugation and the patronage of Sanskrit authors was continuous until nearly our own times.

The texts after the supposed classical age aren’t lacking is grammatical complexity (the complaint is usually the opposite) like people often characterize medieval Latin; they aren’t lacking in number (even Pollock, arguing for the ‘death’ of Sanskrit after our time period, nevertheless had to concede that Sanskrit literature was produced in a substantial quantity in Vijayanagara); whether or not they are of high quality or are *good* is a question of personal taste that I, for one, think is better to answer on a case by case basis rather than condemning entire eras wholesale.

I think it is rather sad that a confluence of factors has so led that much interesting literature lies not only unstudied but often unpublished. It is hard not to let a sigh when you see the gazillionth translation of the *Bhagavadgītā* or a billionth article on ecology of *Meghadūta* or something. Of course you cannot blame people for not doing things. I just wish more people would publish on and about the treasure trove of medieval and modern works that survive so that amateurs like me would have the opportunity of enjoying them.

On a more positive note, things may be getting better. Initiatives like *egangotri* have in recent times uploaded literally millions of manuscripts as well as works published in small runs over at Internet Archive. These are useful even to amateurs like me but more professional attention may not be lacking as well. Recently, I found out about [this](https://kasaharaksa.de/storage/pdf/KSH-Summary-en-long.pdf) project on cataloguing and editing Kashmiri texts from the manuscripts newly made, or to be made, available. I have been pessimistic about the conditions of academia but the situation is not all dark and a small stream of attention does indeed fall on supposedly ‘late’ works. One can only hope that more would fall in the future.

I personally had wanted for a long time to get my hands on some Nepalese Sanskrit works of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries but hadn’t been able to as they were published in small-run journals back in the day but much of these have been now made available on the internet. Even if to do nothing more than to read them for personal satisfaction. And there are many interesting things there too. One of my favourites is a short, pamphlet-like poem written in 1820 in the wake of Nepal’s defeat by the East India Company calling on all the petty princes of India to join together in a single anti-British league. Another, written later that century, is an epic on the life of Prime Minister Junga Bahadur Rana in which, when describing his journey to England, the author, who seems to be working on information obtained through a game of telephone, consistently takes ‘Queen’ to be the personal name of the English King.

I would perhaps do a piece on a couple of these soon. Well, maybe. I constantly procrastinate and hardly get anything at all. So, we’ll see.
