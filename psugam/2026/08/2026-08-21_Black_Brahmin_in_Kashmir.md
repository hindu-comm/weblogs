One of the things that really grinds my gears, so to speak, is colorism. Colorism seems to be rife all over the brown parts of the world. Such prejudice is at least understandable if it comes from actually white people. It doesn’t, after all, take much brainpower to figure out why one might harbour prejudices that are favourable to themselves. But to harbour prejudices that might as easily be wielded against you sounds like some other level of retardation to me. Well. It is how it is, I guess.

What may be the reason for this? There may be some deep-seated bias against darker people due to the bias against men generally. Men are usually much more physically violent than women and men are usually darker than women of their own ethnicities. That bias against darker complexion originates from this fact of sexual dimorphism in humans is the argument of Peter Frost’s *Fair Women, Dark Men (2005)*. I’m dumbing down his much more complex thesis, but that’s his reasoning in a nutshell.

Another reason more commonly found is that colorism is rooted in class dynamics. Elites in the pre-modern world could afford to be in the shade much of their lives but commoner, which in a world after the introduction of agriculture meant peasant farmers, could not. Elites would then necessarily be less tanned than the commoners. So, colorism, in this view, is associated with class dynamics.

But perhaps the most popular explanation seems to be, at least to me, to blame it all on European colonialism. This is, of course, the magic pill of *anti*- and *de*-colonial folks pontificating over the evils of Whiteness: anything wrong in the third world is actually due to European colonialism three generations ago. How could brown folk ever be at fault: the reason a brown guy hurls slurs against a slightly browner guy is due to what the Europeans did in the 19th century. The brown guy may bear some fault but the root is clearly White Supremacy or something.

I don’t find such reasoning convincing. For one, there is enough evidence , at least in some places, for bias against darker complexions long before the European colonization of the wider world gained momentum in the last ~500 years. East Asians, even today, often take offence if you suggest that their obsession with pale complexion or with double eyelid surgeries, might have anything to do with emulating European features and will remind you that their own beauty standards, mostly based on class lines as explained before, prioritized paler complexions long before the arrival of Europeans to their lands.

Colorism is, of course, rife in South Asia too. I wanted to learn, preferably from primary sources, what the reason might be because I have heard two competing theories, neither of which seems very satisfactory. The first relates to caste and the second to British colonialism. So I started going through any and all references to skin complexion in the primary sources from 1500 BC to 1500 AD. Of course any such compilation must necessarily be selective, especially for the more recent times when the sources are much more voluminous. I had pretty comprehensive extracts, translations and comments thereon up to around 1AD but the whole thing had already bulked out to around 20,000 words and I lost any motivation to continue. Perhaps I will continue. Perhaps not. If not, I will post the part up to around 1AD at least some day. Resuming from the two reasons thing, I don’t think either reason accounts for much. The longer post, if I end up finishing it, should clear that up.

Here I would just include a couple examples that I found when researching that I think are interesting.

### Looks like a Monkey



From around the 750s to the 1250s, Kashmir was perhaps *the* intellectual center of the Sanskrit world. Particularly in the field poetry, dramaturgy and literary criticism, even those authors elsewhere who didn’t support or oppose their theories directly composed in terms set by the Kashmiris. Kṣemendra (~11th century) is one of these Kashmiri authors. He, like most of his countrymen, wrote much on literary topics but also on topics ranging from a history of Kashmir to a commentary on the *Kāmasūtra*. He even has a primer on how to become a Sanskrit poet that people even today might find useful. He also wrote quite a few satirical works. Among these, one is called *Deśopadeśa* (Advice from the Countryside, as one translation has it). In seven chapters it makes fun of seven types of people (Villain, Miser, Prostitute, Bawd, Bon-vivant, Student and Old Man married to a young woman). Judging by the pointed insults hurled at them, it seems to me that they are based on people he actually knew though he names them not.

The Student might be properly called the *Foreign-Student*. He is from what Gauḍa (modern Bengal) come to study, for free of course, at the institutions that the kings of Kashmir used to endow in those days. I can’t obviously go over the whole thing verse by verse but this summary from a study of Kṣemendra’s works should do the job:[1](#footnote-1)

> This Upadeśa deals with the description of the student or the chātra.
> In this Upadeśa, the poet ridicules the student who comes from Gauḍa
> to Kashmir for study. Before arriving Kashmir the student looked like
> a skeleton having no flesh and blood in the body. Everyone is scared
> seeing him from a distance and stay away thinking him an alive
> skeleton. But the nutritious food and healthy atmosphere of Kashmir
> soon change his life.
>
> The Gauḍa student strictly maintains his purity. But soon his reality
> comes out. He begins to eat meat in his meal.[2](#footnote-2)Gambling
> becomes his favorite game. He pays a huge amount of money to have a
> prostitute, but alas! the prostitute also rejects him. The lewd
> student is not only interested in harlots and gambling, he is also a
> thief. If he goes to a shop, gives less money and seeks much. The
> Gauḍa student also tortures other students. Nor love nor any
> incentives can ordain the Gauḍa student.

The Bengali student is, according to Kṣemendra, a first class hypocrite. He acts all uppity to touch, presumably against supposedly lower castes, but is ever visiting prostitutes. Even those prostitutes don’t want him unless he pays three or four times as usual. He is cruel to his slaves and dependents but tolerates those who have beautiful wives. He hardly knows how to read but makes a fuss about reading well known philosophers.

1.  This description, which is hard to describe this as anything but
    blatant colorism with even the monkey image so commonly encountered
    in racist caricatures, comes from the mid 1000s centuries not only
    before any European presence but before even Islamic (i.e. West

2.  Caste and colour are not the same thing. People seem to think caste
    is based on colour. It isn’t. Presumably to a Kashmiri like
    Kṣemendra even Brahmins from Bengal would look black.

3.  A dark-skinned Brahmin and a fair-skinned dalit wouldn’t have their
    caste changed or something. The ‘student’ in the satire would
    probably be darker than *all* Kashmiris, even dalits. That doesn’t
    change the fact that he will act so uppity about his purity and
    anyone touching him that even sages would be embarrassed at second
    hand. (So says verse 3). That also doesn’t change the fact that he
    is being supported in his education.

I will elaborate a bit on point 2 above.

When I say that caste is not colour, I don’t mean that there is no relation between the two. There is. For any given region (except in North-West India/Pakistan and any area with Sino-Tibetan minorities), Brahmins are, on average, a bit fairer than the other people of the same ethnic group in those regions. With modern DNA studies, we pretty much know that this is because their proportion of ancestry from AASI (Ancient Ancestral South India, which is the indigenous population of South Asia and is distantly related to the Andaman Islanders and had very dark skin). But this is a matter of averages and there will be naturally many, many exceptions. The difference, even when they exist, is of a few shades in any case.

May be it is due to American media being so dominant and the consequent influence of American style social-justice activism that people seem to think caste is a black and white thing quite literally. The story is that foreign Aryan invaders invaded India, invented caste to enslave the natives and put themselves on top as Brahmins to prevent mixing. This is of course not true. You don’t need any phrenology to tell that Brahmins don’t look like Norwegians or something; two eyes (actually even one for that matter) are enough. The consolidation of caste happened mostly after people had been mixing for generations. More or less everyone in South Asia (except for some Sino-Tibetan tribes and a few tribal peoples) derive their ancestry from the same three or four ancestral groups. The difference is of ratios rather than of kind.

None of this should be taken as me defending the caste system or something. That is obviously not my intention. I’m just saying that no one was saying “you’re rather fair, so you’ll be Brahmin” or “you’re black, so you’ll be untouchable” or something.

You might think I’m fighting a strawman here. Maybe I am. But I’ve encountered a depressing amount of such views from both European and East Asian people which seem to combine a condescension about *o how the inhuman the caste system is* *unlike our noble egalitarian views* and whatnot with a surprising smug satisfaction that if they themselves were dropped in medieval India, they would obviously be hailed as über-Brahmin or something because one’s caste is obviously based on the colour of their skin or what percent of their ancestry is derived from the Proto-Indo-Europeans or Steppe or something.

### Whiter than Snow and Ghastly



So if pale skin would not be any indication of caste, obviously more so for a foreigner, what would be the probable attitude toward it? Do we have any clue? Yes.

Unfortunately, he was defeated and killed in another battle in 1192 and the Ghurids and their successors would establish the Delhi Sultanate. ### White Kashmiris, White Mughals



While researching for the original post, I got to know all sorts of amusing things. One is a little essay by seventeenth-century French author François Bernier. Bernier lived around a decade in the Mughal Empire and his classification of the people of the world into separate races is commonly pointed out as one of the first of its kind in that it departed from older religious biblical models and paved the way for the race science of colonial era. Some have even called him the father of scientific racism. What was surprising was that while he classes even dark brown people into the same race ( or species as he calls it) as the Europeans and is, though obviously not PC, not especially hateful towards even Africans or Native Americans, he goes all batshit insane on the so-called Lapps (Sami people of Scandinavia) who apparently are a race unto themselves:[4](#footnote-4)

> The Lapps make the fourth species. They are little stunted creatures
> with thick legs, large shoulders, short neck, and a face elongated
> immensely; very ugly and partaking much of the bear. I have only seen
> two of them at Dantzic; but, judging from the pictures I have seen,
> and the account which I have received of them from many persons who
> have been in the country, they are wretched animals.

Also Kashmiris were apparently white.

> The women who live in the Ganges at Benares, and downwards towards
> Bengal, are generally esteemed. Those of the kingdom of Cashmere are
> still more so; for besides being as white as those of Europe, they
> have a soft face, and are a beautiful height; so it is from there that
> all those come who are to be found at the Ottoman
> Court[5](#footnote-5), and that all the Grand Seigniors keep by them.
> I recollect that as we were coming back from that country, we saw
> nothing else but little girls in the sort of cabins which the men
> carried on their shoulders over the mountains. But although the women
> of Lahore are brown like the rest of the Indian women, still they
> seemed to me more charming than all the others; their beautiful
> figure, small and easy, with the softness of their faces, quite
> surpassed by a great deal that of the Cashmerians.

It should be clear that Bernier uses white more loosely than people today would, to include lighter brown people.

This professed superiority of the Mughals and their predecessors owing to their lighter complexion is, in my view, much more responsible for contemporary colorist notions than any supposed British influence. For nearly 600 years, the elites of the subcontinent professed themselves to be superior not only on the basis of religion but of complexion as well. Though they would over time be assimilated to the general complexion of their subjects, their attitudes were strong in the days of their power. Contemporary literature is full of such colorist rhetoric.

### Dark like black beans



This colorist attitude of the Islamic conquerors in the second millennium is genuinely new as it is in marked contrast to the prevailing mood in the previous millennium. There genuinely seems to have been an embrace of darker complexion in that time period and colorist attitudes like those in the succeeding era is hard to find.

This embrace itself, however, was something of a new development of the first millennium. Before this there are just constant tirades against black out-groups - usually called *dasyu* or *dāsa*. By the time we get the Buddhist canon, there are multiple instances of Brahmins claiming to be superior due to, among other things, lighter complexion. The interesting thing is that, unlike in the Buddhist sources, it is more or less never explicitly encountered in writings by actual Brahmins that they are light complexioned. There are many, many places where the ‘blacks’ are just assumed to be bad and enemies while no contrasting self designation is found.

I found this kind of interesting. By the rise of caste between ~1000 to ~500 BC, the genetics of Brahmins should have mostly what they are now for present-day Brahmins in the north-west of the subcontinent, or at any rate not very far from it. I would expect them to look more or less how they look now, which is certainly not white.

There is, fortunately, one description from about 150 BC in the most unexpected of places: in a discussion of privative compounds in the great grammatical commentary *Mahābhāṣya* of Patañjali. Privative compounds are compounds in which a particle negates the meaning of the word and makes the second word into its antonym. English examples would include a-theist from theist, im-mortal from mortal, un-interesting from interesting and so on. In Sanskrit, this is done in a way similar to English with the particle a- or an-. As the whole argument is technical and uses specialized vocabulary difficult for us non-grammarians to comprehend completely, I would urge readers to just focus more on the example that is illustrative of our enquiry and less on the argumentation. > the qualities, may also mean someone who is missing some of them.
>
> For someone who is missing the quality - ‘he is a non-brahmin who
> pisses standing’ or ‘he is a non-brahmin who eats standing’.
>
> For someone who is missing the birth/caste, the word brahmin may be
> used due to doubt and misdirection.
>
> The doubt may be like this: When one sees a person with fair
> complexion, pure habits, chestnut (eyes?) and brown hair, then one
> believes that he is a brahmin. Then one finds out that he was not a
> brahmin but a non-brahmin. In this case, the word brahmin was used due
> to doubt and after finding out his birth/caste, the usage is excluded.
>
> The misdirection may be like this: One is misdirected, “There’s a
> brahmin in such and such place. Fetch him.” Then one goes to that
> place and believes that whoever is present in that place must be the
> brahmin. Here the word brahmin was used due to misdirection and after
> finding his birth/caste, this is excluded.
>
> How do we know this (misuse of the word brahmin) happens only due to
> doubt or misdirection?
>
> **Because no one would see a man with skin as dark as a heap of black
> beans (kālaṃ māṣarāśivarṇam) sitting in a shop and assume that this
> man is a brahmin. He knows well (that the person is a non-brahmin).**

The colour terms are very difficult into render to modern terms as the ancients tended to use them very liberally for all sorts of things but for the hair colour, *kapila*, should generally be the yellowish brown colour of a monkey. The *piṅgala* is also a brownish colour. Here it may mean the colour like a bay horse and I have translated as a chestnut. I have assumed that this refers to the colour of the eye, as have some others, because I cannot see what else it could refer to as the complexion is already specified and the hair colour comes next.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!jllh!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6664b28e-44fa-4f02-aff5-f7ee8fc8e5ce_330x239.jpeg)

The complexion is said to be *gaura*, fair. I think it refers to the comparatively light brown of say Kashmiri Pandits today. In any case, this is obviously a relative term. The descendant words in modern languages are of no help to us as they mean different things in different languages. In my mother tongue Nepali, the descendant term is colloquially used to mean the light brown complexion found among ourselves, while in Hindi and other languages, it means a white person.

Again, what is fair and what is black are relative. My parents often call me something like blackie, using the same word as used for black beans above but fortunately affectionately in my case, as I’m quite darker than my brother and the same fair word is often used for him though it is obvious that he is not, literally speaking, white, nor I black.
