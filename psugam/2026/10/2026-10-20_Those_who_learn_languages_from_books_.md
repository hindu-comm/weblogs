# Those who learn languages from books...

[](https://substack.com/@psugam)

Readers of this Substack will likely know that I have some slight interest in, and even slighter knowledge of, ancient languages like Sanskrit and Latin. I have no academic background in any of these languages, or even any humanities or liberal arts in general. Consequently, I don’t really know other people who take an interest in these languages in real life. So, I read but mostly never speak them. The only output, so to speak, remains occasional, and pretty lousy, attempts at versification.

Sometimes I do try to listen to poetry recitations on YouTube or something but more often than not I simply can’t bring myself to enjoy them. The Latin recitations often seem too expressive for my taste. My native tongue uses more or less the same quantitative metres as Classical Sanskrit and my ears expect quantitative verse to be recited, even in Latin, with a steady rhythm with pauses only at line breaks or caesuras and without too much emotion ‘according to sense’. But this seems, as far as I can understand from the internet, too ‘monotonous’ or ‘bland’ or something like that.

Similar problems exist there for Sanskrit recitations as well. The pronunciation of visarga (ḥ) as *hi, hu, hai* and whatnot or of the vowel ṛ as *ri* or *ru* usually breaks the metre completely. And to add to that, they are too much singsongy for my liking usually.

Most of these are, obviously, due to my personal prejudices and not some ‘historically accurate’ preference. I have hardly any knowledge of what historically accurate recitations would be like. If, to take an example, Quintilian’s remarks on poetic recitations are any guide (and if I remember them correctly that is) to how Romans recited Latin poetry, I don’t think I’d be very fond of them either.

Well, I’ve strayed far from the topic at hand. But anyway, I was wondering if people in the past who learnt ancient languages had problems with pronunciations. For long-standing classical languages, there must have been people even then who either learnt entirely through textbooks or lived in areas where good speakers (whatever that might mean in a given context) might be rare. Surely, there must be some out-of-touch Roman aristocrats going, “How do you do, fellow Hellenes?”[1](#footnote-1) in the Hellenistic East with atrocious and heavily accented Greek, right?[2](#footnote-2) In later times, there is obviously the famous scene when Catherine of Aragon and Arthur of England can’t understand each other when they first meet even though both are speaking Latin because of their different accents and pronunciations.

In Sanskrit, there are some interesting anecdotes here and there[3](#footnote-3) but due to the lack of a robust historical tradition, we are usually in the dark about how pronunciations or accents worked in real time. So, I was really intrigued by this passage in a Tibetan text.

This is from the diary of the Tibetan monk Situ Panchen (1700-1774 A.D). He was a polymath and a pivotal figure in the history of Tibet in multiple areas. Among others, he also translated a lot of Sanskrit works in Tibetan. He journeyed southwards to Nepal twice in his life: in 1723-24 and 1748. This interesting excerpt is from his diary entries that he kept during the first visit:[4](#footnote-4)

> At this time, the well-known Brahman *paṇḍita* from Tirahut named
> Bachurao Jha Viṣṇupati came to meet me and we had a long conversation
> in Sanskrit. I was thinking that I really knew some Sanskrit, but when
> I was actually speaking with him I realized that my pronunciation and
> accents were not correct. The Brahman immediately found fault with my
> speech. Afterwards, we wrote on the floor in order to communicate.
> After familiarizing myself in this manner, I felt more comfortable
> with the words and my reading improved. We talked about Sanskrit
> grammar and logic and I learned a great deal about Indian knowledge,
> especially grammar \[Skt. *Vyākaraṇa*\]. \[These were: Pāṇini’s
> Vyākaraṇa commentary made by Nāga, also the Amarakośa commentary by
> Bhaṭṭojī Dīkṣita, both of which are about 12 thousand verses; also the
> Amarakośa commentary by Subhūti; also another lexicon made by
> Medinīkarana. I made some studies of all these texts.\]

The tone of the text is, though not apologetic, a bit hushed but his pronunciation must have been atrocious if they couldn’t understand each other at all and had to write ‘on the floor in order to communicate’. I mean I can only imagine how a Tibetan native speaker who had never actually heard any speaker of an Indo-Aryan language in his life would have pronounced Sanskrit. Anyway, he, as would be expected for someone of his calibre, quickly improved his pronunciation. Though the extract doesn’t mention it, Situ Panchen and Paṇḍit Viṣṇumati went on to collaborate to produce Tibetan translations of at least two Sanskrit grammatical texts.[5](#footnote-5)

I thought the anecdote interesting because accounts of Tibetan monks in Nepal usually go like: The monk got there and instantly explained the meaning of the texts nobody had been able to understand. So, the Buddhists all became his disciples and the *tīrthika*-s said “Wow! You are the wisest person ever. Śiva’s blessing must be upon you, otherwise how would it be even possible?” and everybody clapped or something like that. [6](#footnote-6)

In the unlikely case that I time travel to 4th Century B.C. Gandhāra or Augustan-age Rome or something, I don’t think I’d fare much better than the learned Tibetan polymath.

**namo vaḥ**
