In a previous [post](https://psugam.substack.com/p/sanskrit-life-sanskrit-death), I wrote that I really don’t like the appellation ‘Classical Sanskrit’ at all. Among other things, I said that neither the number of literary works nor the epigraphy that we can associate with the Guptas with certainty is very large:

> The great age of Sanskrit literature, the *classical* age if you will,
> is often held to be 300-1200 CE for reasons that can only be called
> ridiculous. The earlier date is chosen due to the rise of the Gupta
> Empire, when the epigraphy starts becoming overwhelmingly in Sanskrit.
> The problem is that Gupta epigraphic corpus isn’t particularly large
> or impressive.

This is indeed so. But it is nonetheless true that it is from the time of the Guptas that we get a consistent stream of Sanskrit inscriptions, not only in stone as had been the case for most epigraphy in earlier times, but also in copper plates. Their rule probably wasn’t the cause because the same phenomenon can be observed not only in South India, where Gupta power didn’t reach but even in South-East Asia. A large proportion of them are either completely in verse or at least contain some verse.

Most of these verses are what we call *praśasti*-s ‘eulogies’. As most of these were commissioned by some sort of kings or lords who have the poets (though calling them poets might be a bit controversial) praise not only them but their ancestors too. Since the deeds of kings, like wars or monumental projects, affect not only themselves but the state as a whole, one might assume that if were a substantial corpus of such inscriptions, one might get a pretty decent idea of the history.

This is, in fact, not entirely false and were it not for such *praśasti*-s and other inscriptions our knowledge of more or less all of South and South-east Asia before the late medieval period would be, with some important exceptions, pretty dark. The problem, at least for historians trying to reconstruct a narrative, is that praśasti-s are not, and did not even attempt to, record anything even approaching a neutral historical record. They are bombastic in language and more often than not far more care is given to sound cool.

D.C Sircar says in his book on epigraphy:[1](#footnote-1)

> It has to be admitted that only some of the inscriptions can be
> regarded as truly historical documents. No doubt many of them deal
> with historical events and personages; but history is often shadowed
> in them by poetical, eulogistic and conventional elements. Most
> records therefore give the impression that references to historical
> events in them are incidental…
>
> In some cases, the poets are found more eager to display their skill
> in rhetoric rather than in the accuracy of their statements. The
> Candella king Yaśovarman (middle of the 10th century A.D.), who was a
> feudatory of the Gurjara Pratīhāra emperor of Kannauj, is described in
> a Khajuraho inscription as a subduer of the Gauḍas, Khaśas, Kośalas,
> Kaśmīras, Mithilas, Mālavas, Cedis, Kurus and Gurjaras, though during
> the period in question neither were Kośala, Mithilā and Kuru separate
> states nor was the Candella king likely to have had anything to do
> with them. It seems that the lure of alliteration (cf. *kośalaḥ
> Kośalānām, śithilita-Mithilaḥ, Kuru-taruṣu marut*, etc.) carried the
> poet far away from historical accuracy.

Seeing, then, that the poets who wrote the *praśasti*-s composed them as poetry and not history, it is to be expected that they would be appreciated as poetry. This, sadly, usually doesn’t happen. [2](#footnote-2)

> As mentioned before (7.2.2.1), the position of epigraphic compositions
> within the Sanskritic literary tradition is more or less marginal.
> Much of the *praśasti* literature, it must be conceded, is not much
> better than hackwork. Nevertheless, there are not a few cases where
> inscriptional *kāvya* rises above the ordinary level…
>
> Altogether, the inscriptional records embody both the best and worst
> of Sanskrit poetry: its richness and variety of style and imagination,
> and its stiffness and pedantry.

I think the last point is particularly true. Epigraphic poetry is overwhelmingly mediocre, but then so is what we have from manuscript traditions. And among tens of thousands of inscriptions, even if only a small percentage consists of decent samples, the result would still be quite a large corpus.

Though modern scholars tend to emphasize the contrast between real poets whose works survive, or are known, through manuscript tradition and men who were essentially bureaucratic versifiers, not above heaping hilariously extravagant praise on dark age despots for a quick buck, the line between them is not as clear as one would like to imagine. Quite a few poets whose works survive also wrote praśasti-s for inscriptions[3](#footnote-3) and it does not strain one’s imagination to believe that many whose verses have survived only in epigraphic form wrote normal works too, which have not survived to the present day. Umāpatidhara, for example, was a poet who was praised by his contemporaries and the stories circulating about him thousands of kilometres from his home shows that he was quite popular in his time. But except some verses quoted in anthologies, the only substantial work that survives is the 35-verse Deopara Inscription from the time of Vijayasena[4](#footnote-4).

After the Gupta-s such *praśasti*-s are found all over the Sanskrit-speaking world and can get quite long. Praśasti-s with 30-40 verses in long metres (about 120-160 lines in usual prints) are by no means uncommon and many are far longer. The absolute masters in writing humongous, nearly book-length *praśasti*-s were the Kings of Khmer Empire in present-day Cambodia and the surrounding regions. The longest such inscription is 298 verses long and several others which are longer than 200 verses. By my rough estimate, it should take about 2.5 to 3 hours if one were to recite. I have read more than one expert on Khmer epigraphy remark that the literary quality of inscriptions there is much higher compared to India. Whether this is really so or due to their own personal tastes, I don’t know. The few I’ve read were pretty good, so maybe it’s true. In any case, Dominic Goodall has published the second-longest Cambodian Sanskrit Inscription (218 verses) with translation and ample commentary especially aimed at *kāvya*-readers rather than historians. It’s available as [pdf](https://hal.science/hal-03938904) here.

Those are not actually the longest ever. One [madlad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raj_Prashasti#) from the 1600s wrote a whole epic poem and had them inscribed on 25 stones. It consists of 1,106 verses (must be more than 3000 lines at least) and divided into 24 cantos.

Personally, I’m partial to early Sanskrit inscriptions (upto c.800 A.D.) from Nepal for reasons you can probably guess. The corpus is fairly small, but there are a number of inscriptions there that are, to put it anachronistically, straight-up bangers. I have written a bit about the earliest substantial one here. There’s one[5](#footnote-5) from the early 500s which is in the form of a prayer addressed to *Dvaipāyana* and half of it is like “Wow, it’s great you wrote the Mahābhārata, otherwise those evil, evil Buddhists would’ve won.”

It will be too much to discuss, or even list, all the *praśasti*-s I like, but I’ll point out a few. These are, perhaps, not the very best, but I’ve chosen examples that would connect them with the wider literary traditions.

### Fame of the Moon King



There is an ancient pillar in Delhi famous for two things. It is, for one, covered with inscriptions from various ages among which the oldest, and the one which we’re interested in, is quite beautiful in language. For another, though made of iron and subject to the whim of open weather for millennia, it has shown minimal effects of rusting. We’ll leave the rust and follow the inscription. In the script of fourth/fifth century, we have three verses in polished Sanskrit[6](#footnote-6):

> published
>
> ``` text
> yasyo’dvarttayataḥ pratīpam urasā śattrun sametyā’gatan 
> vaṅgeṣv āhavavarttino’bhilikhitā khaḍgena kīrttir bbhuje ।
> tirttvā sapta mukhāni yena samare sindhor jjitā vāhlikā
> yasyā’dyā’py adhivāsyate jalanidhir vviryyānilair ddakṣinaḥ ॥
>
>
> khinnasy'eva visṛjya gām narapater ggām āśritasye’tarāṃ 
> mūrttyā karmmajitāvaniṃ gatavataḥ kīrttyā sthitasya kṣitau ।
> śāntasye’va mahāvane hutabhujo yasya pratāpo mahān
> nā’dyā’py utsṛjati praṇāśistaripor yyatnasya śeṣaḥ kṣitim ॥
>
>
> prāptena svabhujārjitañ ca suchirañ cai’kādhirājayaṃ kṣitau
> candrāhvena samagracandrasadriśīṃ vaktraśriyaṃ bibhratā ।
> tenā’yam praṇidhāya bhūmipatinā bhāvena viṣṇau matiṃ 
> prāṃśur vviṣṇupade girau bhagavato viṣṇnor ddhvajaḥ sthāpitaḥ ॥
> ```

which reads as follows in Fleet’s translation:

> (Verse 1) He, on whose arm fame was inscribed by the sword, when, in
> battle in the Vanga countries (Bengal), he kneaded (and turned) back
> with (his) breast the enemies who, uniting together, came against
> (him); – he, by whom, having crossed in warfare the seven mouths of
> the (river) Sindhu, the Vahlikas were conquered; – he, by the breezes
> of whose prowess the southern ocean is even still perfumed; –
>
>   
> (Verse 2) He, the remnant of the great zeal of whose energy, which
> utterly destroyed (his) enemies, like (the remnant of the great
> glowing heat) of a burned-out fire in a great forest, even now leaves
> not the earth; though he, the king, as if wearied, has quit this
> earth, and has gone to the other world, moving in (bodily) form to the
> land (of paradise) won by (the merit of his) actions, (but) remaining
> on (this) earth by (the memory of his) fame; –
>
>   
> (Verse 3) By him, the king, attained sole supreme sovereignty in the
> world, acquired by his own arm and (enjoyed) for a very long time;
> (and) who, having the name of Chandra, carried a beauty of countenance
> like (the beauty of) the full-moon,-having in faith fixed his mind
> upon (the god) Vishnu, this lofty standard of the divine Vishnu was
> set up on the hill (called) Vishnupada.

As with much of kāvya, the translation hardly does justice to the original. For a reader who can’t read Sanskrit, it would be hard to believe that the original is actually quite poetic. The whole inscription is basically one long sentence with many relative clauses, and we find the name of the king only in the last verse where it says “who, having the name of Chandra, carried a beauty of countenance like (the beauty of) the full-moon” punning the name of the king, Candra, which means moon. All the verses are in 19-syllabled *Śārdūlavikrīḍita*.

Notice also that the lands which the Moon king supposedly conquered lie at the extreme ends of the subcontinent: *Vaṅga* (Bengal), *Vāhlika* (Bactria) and the *Dakṣiṇa Jalanidhi* (Southern Ocean). Though the sequence is not exactly the same, the area is the same as that covered the mythical *digvijaya*-s of, say, Raghu in Kālidāsa’s *Raghuvaṃśa*.

Who could this Moon king be? From palaeographic evidence, the inscription should be from the fourth or fifth century, and considering the deeds ascribed to him, the only ruler named Candra who could be a possible candidate is Candragupta II (r. 375-415) of the Gupta empire. From what we can infer from epigraphic evidence, he defeated the Western Satraps, a dynasty of Scythian Kings who had ruled in western India for some four centuries. Most of north India, from the Indus to *Vaṅga* as the inscription says, was under his rule and for much of his reign the great power of Southern India was under the rule of his daughter as the queen regnant.

But for all these deeds, he was more or less forgotten by the modern period. Only after the epigraphic research proceeded under British rule in the nineteenth century was it revealed that the legendary King Vikramāditya “Sun of Valour”, popular from a range of medieval stories including the famous *Vetālapañcaviṃśati* “[Twenty-Five Stories of the Vampire](https://psugam.substack.com/p/alcuin-of-york-a-medieval-indian)”, was likely based upon the historical Candragupta II who bore the epithet Vikramāditya too. And like Candragupta II, the legendary Vikramāditya was credited with expelling Scythians too.

But the legendary Vikramāditya also came in contact with a tantric necromancer who tries to gain magic power by sacrificing Vikramāditya himself. Guided by the vampire, Vikramāditya in turn kills the tantric and gains magic power himself. Surely this is just later growth of the legend right. Surely the great Gupta emperor wouldn’t stoop to performing black magic?

### King Moon Defamed?



The dynasty of the Rāṣṭrakūṭa-s ruled much of South India from c. 753 to c. 973 A.D. Among their corpus of inscriptions are many copper plates recording detail grants of lands to Brahmins, temples or generals and others. These are usually prefaced by long *praśasti* poems praising both the ruling king and his ancestors. We are here concerned with incidental mentions in two such copper-plate inscriptions from the reign of Amoghavarṣa I (r. 814-878 A.D.) and Govinda IV (r. 930-936 A.D.) respectively.

The first one is:[7](#footnote-7)

> Text within this block will maintain its original spacing when
> published
>
> ``` text
> hatvā bhrātaram eva rājyam aharat devīṃ ca dīnas tathā
> lakṣaṃ koṭim alekhayat kila kalau dātā sa guptānvayaḥ ।
> yenā’tyāji tanuḥ svarājyam asakṛd bāhyārthakaiḥ kā kathā
> hrīs tasyo’nnatirāṣṭrakūṭatilako dāte'ti kīrtyām api ॥
> ```

Or in English:

> The wretch killed his brother, snatched his kingdom and his wife. Then
> he ordered it to be written that the one from the Gupta family gave
> thousand billion away in this Kali age. But he (Amoghavarṣa) gave away
> his kingdom, a little thing, multiple times - what to say of external
> things?- blushed a bit even listening to the report that the giver is
> the best of the exalted Rāṣṭrakūṭa family.

The second one is[8](#footnote-8):

> Text within this block will maintain its original spacing when
> published
>
> ``` text
> sāmarthye sati ninditā pravihitā nai’vāgraje krūratā
> bandhustrīgamanādibhiḥ kucaritair āvarjitaṃ nā’yaśaḥ ।
> śaucāśaucaparāṅmukhaṃ na ca bhiyā paiśācyam aṅgīkṛtaṃ
> tyāgenā’samasāhasaiś ca bhuvane yas sāhasāṅko’bhavat ॥
> ```

Or in English:

> Though he had the power, he didn’t act cruelly towards his elder
> brother. Neither did he earn ill-fame by such wicked deeds as sleeping
> with his kinsman’s wife. He did not abandon ideas of purity and
> impurity to take up demonic conduct. Through generosity and boundless
> courage in this world he (Govinda IV) was known as the “Courageous”.

The first one, by Amoghavarṣa I, is at least somewhat understandable, since he had retired from his rule in his later years to hand the kingdom to his son but the second one, by Govinda, is just lying through its teeth. His elder brother Amoghavarṣa II had ruled just for 1 year when Govinda defeated him and took the kingdom for himself. Amoghavarṣa did die after this defeat. I don’t know what else can this mean other than Govinda killing his elder brother[9](#footnote-9)?

In any case, if we take both verses in consideration it seems that some king from the Gupta family killed his elder brother, married his brother’s widow and was somehow implicated in some black magic accusations. Could this be historic? Can we find out who this might be?

During the 20th century, as more and more theoretical works on Sanskrit poetry and drama were edited, it was found that there was substantial amount of quotations from a previously unknown drama called *Devīcandragupta* (The Queen and Candragupta) by Viśākhadatta. This was a drama in ten acts, and we have quotations from the first five. The general story of the first five seems to be something like this:

> An unnamed Scythian king (Śakapati) has defeated a king named
> Rāmagupta. Both are now in their respective camps. The peace terms
> include that Rāmagupta give up his chief queen, named Dhruvadevī, to
> the victor. His younger brother, prince Candragupta, takes it upon
> himself a solution to such a shameful situation. He disguises himself
> in Dhruvadevī’s clothes, goes to the Scythian King’s camp and kills
> him. The remaining extract shows Candragupta about to perform a
> tantric rite. Then he is, for some unknown reason, acting as if he has
> gone mad. In another extract he is depicted as showing erotic feeling
> towards Dhruvadevī.

The quotations are all from the first five acts. A considerable portion of the plot must remain for the last five. What could have happened there?

If the accusations in the Rāṣṭrakūṭa plates are any indication, Candragupta probably killed Rāmagupta and married his sister-in-law. Is the story historical? The Candragupta of the play must have been Candragupta II because we know from official Gupta genealogy that his chief queen and the mother of the next Gupta Emperor was named Dhruvasvāminī. We also know that he did defeat and conquer the Scythian Western Satraps in the 390s.

When V.S. Raghavan[10](#footnote-10) compiled all the quotations from the *Devīcandragupta* and discussed the possible historicity, there was no evidence for any Gupta ruler named Rāmagupta. Scholars had tried to link him to other known Gupta princes but inscriptions found since have shown that a Gupta ruler named Rāmagupta, who did assume imperial titles, existed in late 4th century. But whether the references in the Rāṣṭrakūṭa plates as well as some other literary references that survive refer to real historical events or to Viśākhadatta’s drama are hard to know. And how historical the drama is cannot be known until better evidence is discovered. The characters are historical. The plot? Who knows! In any case, the glory of the Moon King, it seems, wasn’t entirely unblemished.

### In Stone and in Anthology



Aśoka the Maurya, in the third Century BC, had caused to be engraved a series of edicts in pillars and rocks that are found all over the subcontinent from Afghanistan (in Greek and Aramaic!) to the deep south of India. One such pillar which was in the Himalayan foothills was brought to Delhi by Sultan Firoz Shah (r. 1351-1388 A.D.). There are, beside Aśoka’s inscriptions, others of later date. One of these is from the reign of Vigraharāja IV (r. 1150-1164 A.D.) of the Cāhamāna dynasty. It consists of four verses besides the prose passages containing the date of Thursday, 9 April, A.D. 1164. The last two verses are[11](#footnote-11):

> Text within this block will maintain its original spacing when
> published
>
> ``` text
> ā vindhyād ā himādrer vviracitavijayas tīrthayātrāprasaṅgād
> udgrīveṣu praharttā nṛpatiṣu vinamatkandhareṣu prasannaḥ ।
> āryāvarttaṃ yathārthaṃ punar api kṛtavān mlecchavicchedanābhir
> ddevaḥ śākambharīndro jagati vijayate vīsalakṣoṇīpālaḥ ॥
>
> brūte saṃprati cāhamānatilakaḥ śākambharībhūpatiḥ 
> śrīmadvigraharāja eṣa vijayī santānajān ātmanaḥ ।
> asmābhiḥ karadaṃ vyadhāyi himavadvindhyāntarālaṃ bhuvaḥ
> śeṣasvīkaraṇāya mā’stu bhavatām udyogaśūnyaṃ manaḥ ॥
> ```

Or in English:

> The Lord, King of Śākambharī, Vīsaladeva, is victorious in the world
> -he who has conquered from the Vindhya-s to the Himālaya-s as if going
> to a pilgrimage, who uproots disobedient kings but is favorable to
> those who bow down to him, and he has made the land of the Ārya-s true
> to its name again by uprooting the barbarians.
>
> Now the victorious Vigraharāja, King of Śākambharī, best of the
> Cāhamāna family, speaks to his descendants: “We have made tributary
> the land between the Himālaya-s and the Vindhya-s. Let not your mind
> be without effort in conquering the rest.”

The wish of Vigraharāja (Vīsaladeva is his vernacular name) wouldn’t come true at all. In fact, history had just the opposite in store for them. Less than thirty years from the date of this inscription, the successors of the ‘barbarians’ whom he had routed (Ghaznavids) would return and entirely uproot his descendants and establish the Delhi Sultanate. It is not entirely without irony that the pillar bearing such an inscription would later be brought to Delhi by the descendant of his enemies.

The text of this inscription would be interesting even if there were nothing more to it. True, it is ironic to think about, but there are other such cases. For example, a Sanskrit drama called *Lalitavigraharājanāṭaka* depicting Vigraharāja’s victory over the Ghaznavids as well as another drama named *Harikelīnāṭaka* written by Vigraharāja himself were inscribed on the walls of a temple which would be destroyed by the Delhi Sultans and a new mosque, one of the earliest of the Sultanate, would be built to show the superiority of the *mu’min* over the *kāfir*. The parts of the drama-s that survive do so only because the plundered stones from the earlier structure was used for the Mosque.

There is, however, another interesting aspect to it. Śārṅgadhara compiled his great anthology of Sanskrit poetry, *Śārṅgadharapaddhati*, in 1363 A.D. In the section called *Viśiṣṭarājavarṇana* (“Description of Specific Kings”), he includes the two verses we quoted above (in nos. 1255 and 1256). It is followed by the ascription “*etau* *nṛganṛpatipāṣāṇayajñayūpapraśasteḥ*” or “These two are from the praśasti in King Nṛga’s sacrificial pillar made of stone.”

It seems clear that Śārṅgadhara thought the ancient pillar of Aśoka to be a sacrificial pillar made by the mythical King Nṛga. And, as we have seen, it is true that the verses are from the *praśasti* on the stone pillar but how did Śārṅgadhara found out about it? Did he read the inscription himself? Where? Śārṅgadhara’s work dates exactly in the reign of Firoz Shah who brought the pillar to Delhi and had his pandits try to read what it said. They couldn’t read the Aśokan inscriptions but the script of Vigraharāja’s inscription should have been easily legible. Did the content of the inscription then spread in manuscript form?

When the main line of the Cāhamāna-s was uprooted by the Ghurids in 1193 A.D., a branch of the family fled to the great fortress of Raṇastambhapura and ruled the surrounding areas. The last king this latter line was Hammīradeva. In the late thirteenth century, he gave refuge to a Muslim Mongol who had plotted against the Sultan of Delhi and fled when his plans were revealed. The siege and last stand of Hammīradeva in 1301 A.D. has been famous ever since. Śārṅgadhara’s grandfather Rāghavadeva was the *guru* of this Hammīradeva. Could it then be that either our author or his grandfather had access to manuscript transcription of the inscription from the original archives of that dynasty. I don’t know how such archives worked in this period, but in early modern Nepal, all such inscriptions were prepared together with manuscript copies, which would be saved in the government’s archives. Something along those line shouldn’t be impossible. What do you think, dear reader?
