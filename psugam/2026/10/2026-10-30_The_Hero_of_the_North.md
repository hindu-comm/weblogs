# The Hero of the North

### A poem on Sigurd and Brynhild

I am not well-read in, well, particularly anything. I usually either like, or at least don’t hate, the things I read or don’t finish if I dislike a book. But there are only a few things I really, really love and of those one is the story of the Volsung family.

You might or might not know the story of the Volsung but have perhaps heard at least of Sigurd the Dragonslayer. The Volsung Cycle was perhaps the most widespread legendary story during the middle ages in Germanic Europe. Besides being told in about half of the poems of the *Poetic Edda* in Old Norse, there is a full length treatment in the Saga of the Volsungs. There is the *Nibelungenlied* in German. There’s even a short mention in *Beowulf*. In modern times, Richard Wagner’s *Der Ring des Nibelungen* might be the most famous work in this tradition. If you know your Tolkien, you might know that he not only wrote his own poems[1](#footnote-1) on the story but that many parts of the *Legendarium* itself are inspired by the Volsung story.

What follows is an original poem in Sanskrit (and its English translation too) based on a particularly heightened scene in the Volsung story. The Valkyrie Brynhild has found out that she has been tricked into believing that Gunnar, whom she then married, had entered the ring of fire but it was actually Sigurd in disguise. She then shuts herself in a room where Sigurd comes and tries to persuade her but she is determined to kill both Sigurd, whom she loves, and herself.

I’m usually very bad at summaries. If you don’t already know the story, here’s a brief summary of the relevant part from [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%B6lsunga_saga#Sigurd_and_the_Gjukingar). No notes follow the poem but I’ve included a few thoughts on the form itself.

> Sigurd rides to the estate of Heimr, husband of Bekkhild, Brynhild’s
> sister. Sigurd catches sight of Brynhild weaving a golden tapestry in
> the castle. Alsvid tells him to not think about women, but after
> Brynhild saying they are not fated to be together, they renew their
> vows.
>
> Meanwhile, a skilled sorceress named Grimhild is married to King
> Gjuki, and together they have three sons: Gunnar, Hǫgni, and Guthormr.
> Their daughter Guðrún has a dream about a golden hawk, which Brynhild
> interprets as her future husband. They then talk of Sigurd’s
> excellence and the prophecies about him before his birth. Then Guðrún
> has a dream about a handsome stag, which Brynhild interprets as
> Sigurd. Brynhild prophesies she will marry Sigurd, lose him, marry
> Atli the Hun, lose her brothers, then kill Atli.
>
> Sigurd comes to Gjuki with his horse Grani and his treasure from the
> dragon Fáfnir. Grimhild gives him a drink that makes him forget about
> Brynhild, wanting him to marry Guðrún. Gunnar and the others swear
> brotherhood with Sigurd, and he marries Guðrún. She eats some of the
> dragon’s heart, and bears Sigurd a son, Sigmund. Meanwhile, Grimhild
> encourages Gunnar to marry Brynhild. Sigurd and the three brothers
> ride to King Buðli for Gunnar to ask for Brynhild’s hand. She is
> inside a hall surrounded by fire and has vowed an oath to marry
> whoever can ride through the flames, believing only Sigurd could be
> brave enough to do so. Gunnar can’t take on this task, so he and
> Sigurd exchange shapes, and Sigurd rides through the fire and asks
> Brynhild to marry him as Gunnar. Brynhild reluctantly agrees because
> of her oath and leaves her daughter Aslaug by Sigurd to be raised with
> Heimr.
>
> Later, Brynhild and Guðrún are arguing about whose husband is better,
> and Guðrún shows her the ring which Brynhild had given Sigurd.
> Brynhild recognizes the ring and realizes she has been tricked. She
> tells Gunnar she knows he deceived her and that she will kill him and
> seek revenge on Grimhild. Brynhild takes to her room and Sigurd comes
> to try to make amends by asking her to marry him, but she rejects his
> offer, wanting to die and bring doom upon everyone involved.
>
> Gunnar consults with his brothers whether they should kill Sigurd to
> keep Brynhild or not. They decide to give snake’s and wolf’s meat to
> Guthormr to turn him violent and kill Sigurd. He goes into Sigurd’s
> bed chamber and stabs him while asleep. Sigurd wakes up and before
> dying, throws Gram after him as he leaves, cutting Guthormr in two.
> Brynhild laughs when she hears Guðrún sobbing, and Guðrún tells Gunnar
> he made a mistake by killing Sigurd. Brynhild also tells Gunnar he has
> made a mistake and stabs herself. But before she dies, she foretells
> the rest of Gunnar’s and Guðrún’s future. Gunnar fulfills Brynhild’s
> last request, that he put her on a bonfire with Sigurd, Guthormr, and
> Sigurd’s 3-year-old son.

> perform sorcery on me, I bear no enmity.

> The manliest of men would’ve entered the fire
> but the horse did not run. It was not his fault.
> What must happen will happen. Sons of Aditi
> haven’t stopped that, either for you or for me.
>
> Brynhild
> Crooked words will not turn me now. At once,
> a knife will surely pierce through your chest.
> I don’t want to see you embracing another
> woman, even as a creeper embraces a tree.
>
> Sigurd
> Then I shall place my own heart with yours.
> Let the two of us, same-minded, have a single
> body. If that is what you want, I will, unwilling,
> do that. Do not look at the unreturning path.
>
> Brynhild
> Wretch! Wretched are you! Does the daughter
> and sister of over-kings seem a whore to you?
> In no condition would I see two husbands
> under a single roof. I want to kill us both.
>
> Sigurd
> Day after day I fall upon them as lightning; some
> enemy somewhere may strike me down during
> battle, either today or tomorrow. Such fears
> hold me not. Let your wish be fulfilled quickly.
>
> If you want to do deeds that shouldn’t be done,
> I will even let go of her whom I led in marriage.
> Come! Come! Don’t go, O Apsaras, to that place
> whence mortals return not. Let us make terms.
>
> Brynhild
> I shall not abandon my faultless husband.
> I love you. I hate you. I’ll kill you, loved one.
> Except with my own death, or yours, they will
> not find an end to this. I am a shield-maiden.

### Some Thoughts



I’m quite fond of the Poetic Edda but the sort of poetry it contains doesn’t really have parallels in Sanskrit tradition. Even setting aside the obvious question of comparing languages and cultures so different, there seem to be hardly any common points between Eddic poetry and *kāvya*. That said, there is, I think, a comparable genre in Sanskrit but from a far earlier time.

In the Ṛgveda there are a dozen or so specimens of what modern scholars, following ancient scholarship, call saṃvāda or dialogue. These seem to me to be the closest analogue to Eddic poems. If, to take an example, a competent Old Norse scholar were to take the dialogue of *[Pūruravas and Urvaśī](https://vedaweb.uni-koeln.de/texts/rv/browse/66695ac77dbde8fe2678b1e1)* and put it into Norse, it would fit right in. Or at least, that’s what I think. In any case, I have tried to emulate the form of a *saṃvāda* here.

The language is mostly Classical Sanskrit but a handful of Vedic archaisms have been thrown in. But because I am not great in the language, there probably are many errors in the text. If you spot some, feel free to correct me.

I have mostly avoided personal names in the text because I don’t like using transliterated names in Sanskrit while coming up with Sanskrit equivalents of those names seemed more trouble than it’s worth. No attempts at using any cognate or anything have been made. But I did use *Apsaras* for *Valkyrie*. Like Valkyries (literally slain-choosers) taking the slain vikings to Valhalla (slain-hall), it was commonly believed that the Apsaras would literally pick up slain warriors and take them up to heaven. Medieval Sanskrit poetry is full of such images. In South India, there are literally thousands of memorials called hero-stones which mark the place where a man died in battle. Many of these contain images of the dead being carried upwards by women.

Of course, well-read readers will surely spot the Vedic references and even the obvious Catullus nod though generally I have tried not to be too frank with such references for, as a Middle Vedic proverb says, “The Gods love the cryptic and hate the obvious.[2](#footnote-2)”
