Have you ever read the Mahābhārata in full, from cover to cover? Have you ever read the Mahābhārata in full, from cover to cover, *in Sanskrit*?

Well, I can’t say for certain because I don’t know you. Maybe you have read it but the most likely answer to either question is “No”. On the one hand, it is immensely long and on the other, it is like a full-on attack against the literary sensibilities of most people. The main narrative doesn’t start until you’re some fifty chapters or some couple hundred pages in and even then there are digressions at every possible moment. The characters seem to burst out a story every couple of minutes and the characters in those stories will tell some other stories and so that at many crucial points in the narratives, you are some five or six levels of embedded narrations. And the digressions are often so long you seem to lose the main plot completely. The language is not only not particularly beautiful but in most places positively tedious. There are innumerable vocatives and formulaic phrases. Each character is called by a dozen patronyms, matronyms and epithets. And on top of that, it is, as I said before, immensely long - the traditional versions claim to be 200,000 lines long. Even the much curated critical edition is some 170,000 lines long. And though it is often called an ‘epic’ and compared to the Iliad and the Odyssey, this comparison is not only unhelpful but actively misleading. I’ve recently re-read Aristotle’s Poetics and it seems to me that for each thing that he praises Homer for, the Mahābhārata does the opposite. Any comparison between the Mahābhārata and Homer is not the topic of this piece, so I’ll pass it by here.[1](#footnote-1)

In these circumstances, it isn’t hard to see why people wouldn’t have read the whole thing even in translation - and good translations are pitifully few even in English - not to say anything of the original and for most people who ‘read’ it, it is in some summary or retelling in the second or third hand.

It is, obviously, understandable that more people don’t read the original. But it is a damn shame nonetheless. For two millennia and more[2](#footnote-2), it has not only influenced but positively shaped the identity of some of the most populated parts of the world. Hundreds of royal houses all over South and South East Asia used to derive their ancestry from the heroes of the Mahābhārata during the medieval period. In Nepal, our kings did so until the end of the monarchy in 2008. Though it is not the only reason, it is primarily due to the popularity of the Mahābhārata that a billion and a half people still live in a country called after a small bronze and iron age tribe that lost not only any semblance of sovereignty but even its identity as an ethnic group in the mid-1^(st) millennium BC. Its capital is not only located in their old tribal territory but in the very heart of it.

Beyond just the literary influence, which however strongly I put it will probably be an understatement, there is something very enticing, almost magical, about the text itself. It is probable, even likely, that this perception is due to my pitifully meagre reading but there’s nothing quite like the Mahābhārata. I, at least, haven’t found anything like it. It’s hard to put into words for me and I’ll say no more here.

You can of course get a pretty good idea of the main plot of the whole thing from a retelling or an abridged edition or even from something like wikipedia. But when you read the whole thing, it’s really a far different experience. It’s got all sorts of things - a faux Vedic hymn in pseudo-archaic language, quite a few sections in simple prose, a riddle contest, a mini version of the Rāmāyaṇa, the story of a mother complaining about his son who has been dethroned and driven out of his kingdom that sounds very much like my mother complaining about me and so on and on. And though the language is usually rather workmanlike rather than beautiful, as I have said above, this is not the case uniformly. Parts of the narrative, like parts of the battle books or even the Bhagavadgītā, are indeed in a more elevated style not entirely akin to the kāvya style, with generally flowing and easy but beautiful language. Often this comes with a longer triṣṭup metre but not always. I particularly love the story of Sāvitrī (III. 277-283) and find the language very beautiful. The story is not very long (some 297 verses or about 647 lines). In any case, I would encourage readers to read the whole thing if they can.

The Mahābhārata is also important for a whole host of other reasons, like being some of the only indications for what pre-classical Sāṃkhya and Yoga philosophies were like. Closer to our topic, it also includes people from a lot of what we would call ethnic groups today. For many of these the first attestation of their names occurs in the Mahābhārata. Many of these are now long gone but even those whose names sound eerily similar to the ones heard today (one naturally assumes, for example, that the people Mahābhārata calls the *Kāśmīra* are Kashmiris today or *Kerala* are the people of Kerala), there are necessarily differences such identifications erase. It is the case not only here but everywhere in history. Still, the temptation for identification is strong. In my own case, the mention in the Mahābhārata is probably the earliest attestation of our ethnic group (an instance will be seen below “… *Khaśa-s by name,…* : all really despicable people.”) before the likely mentions in Pliny the Elder and Ptolemy around northern reaches of Punjab and the Hindu Kush.

With such an ethnic medley, it is not surprising that the text, or the characters therein, would express some negative opinion of some of them. Naturally, then, there are quite a few such passages. But none really come close to the passage that we are going to translate today. It is an almost perfect specimen of ancient xenophobia. And unlike many other xenophobic musings, it is quite long: about 2000 words in my translation. I have included in the name of this article the word “ethnography”, because it reminds me of nothing so much as the descriptions of subjugated or newly ‘discovered’ people by colonial scholars.

The background is this: Dhṛtarāṣṭra and Pāṇḍu are brothers. Their sons, called Kaurava-s and Pāṇḍava-s respectively, are fighting for their ancestral throne. Karṇa is a brother to the Pāṇḍava-s by birth but doesn’t know it and is taken by the Kaurava-s as their champion and placed as the titular king of the Aṅga country in modern Bangladesh and West Bengal. Śalya, the king of the Madra-s in present day Pakistani Punjab, is the maternal uncle of the Pāṇḍava-s and comes to help them with their army during the battle. But the Kaurava-s trick him into joining their side. Still, Śalya promises his nephews that he’ll try to demotivate his new companions on the other side. Finally, when Śalya is chosen as the charioteer of Karṇa, he start praising Karṇa’s enemies and insulting Karṇa. Karṇa, a great braggart himself, cannot bear this and answers. The content of his answer is translated below.

Or, in short, Pakistani vs Bangladeshi.

First, I’ll give the text (VIII.30) itself. A few comments and interpretation will follow the text but I’ll make one point before. The people reviled in the excerpt here are consistently called Bāhlīka-s in the text of the Critical Edition. Bāhlīka literally means Bactrian but is often confused with Bāhīka (without the ‘l’) which is the name of a people-group in the Punjab. It is clear that the latter is meant and the vulgate has the latter word throughout. I’ve kept it as Bāhīka throughout. This word seems, at first glance at least, to be related to the word ‘bahiḥ’ or outside. So, the text throughout has wordplays on this. Anything like ‘outside’, ‘excluded’, ‘outsider’ in the translation refers to that. It is likely that the word Bāhīka is a contemptuous exonym meaning ‘outsider’ and the real name is Āraṭṭa as the text says.

The text is from the Critical Edition but I have added a few verses from the vulgate which are however placed in italics.

### COMMENTS



I’m sure if you change the names for modern groups, this sounds very much like the sort of stupid racist ideas you see on Twitter. “You are degenerates and we are moral people” or “You guys eat weird things” are staples of xenophobic insults even in our supposedly progressive age. Though the characterization of the Bāhīka women as ‘fair-skinned’ does indicate that this is a bygone age, before (and actually also after) the evils of colorism that has plagued society. More on that [here](https://psugam.substack.com/p/black-brahmin-in-kashmir).

Another thing I would like to remind is that the text we have read here is not neutral. I mean this in two senses. The first is that the bias against the Madra-s and Bāhīka-s shown here or their characterization is not some scientific truth freely floating in Platonic Forms. They reflect the views of a certain group of people, in our case likely that of orthodox brahmins in and around the land of the Kuru-s. Secondly, it is not neutral even in the narrative itself but reflects the point of view of one character (Karṇa) with which the other character (Śalya) disagrees. Śalya’s reply, moreover, is the more sensible one. There are good and bad people everywhere. People everywhere like to sing, dance and joke around. What’s wrong with that? And if the customs of Karṇa’s own people are, as Śalya says they are, “abandoning ill people to their deaths, selling one’s own wife and children to slavery”, they seem to me to be much worse than anything Karṇa accuses Śalya and his people of.

One thing I really dislike is that many people, even many scholars who really should know better, take passages like this at face value and would argue that such-and-such region was actually not Hinduized/ Brahmanized or that they were recent arrivals and what not. In fact this very passage has been used as such many times even in fairly recent times. The problem is that these say very little beyond the prejudices of the author and more or less nothing about how a population would have seen itself. Accusations of beef-eating, loose sexual morals or lack of caste are the stock insults of the orthodox against anyone. It is striking that some of the same people who would characterize others of ‘naively’ taking the text on its face take such passages literally. Hilarious.

To get something like Mahābhārata in Greek terms, imagine something like this: Imagine the whole Trojan Cycle as a single long poem, strongly coloured by Neoplatonism, but not completely over the old Gods. It would include not only the genealogies of Hesiod but also a mini-version of the Theban cycle and small scattered sections on virtually all early myths. Odysseus would be the incarnation of Apollo, now the head god, and just before aristeia of Achilles, Odysseus reveals himself and delivers the Timaeus. The whole would not be in a single metre but turn to hexametres only in elevated scenes and be in iambic trimetres otherwise. It would contain large sections even in prose. And all of this is to be composed by Greek elites traumatized by Roman domination and imagining a glorious past for themselves.

This is something like how Mahābhārata is in the Indic context. The comparisons are necessarily subjective but you get the point.

I have deliberately avoided anything as dating or even the mention of Mahābhārata’s supposed ‘oral’ origins. First because I don’t believe in any such ‘oral’ theories. Second, because much of the dating and periodizations are based less on any criteria even vaguely objective than on the whims of the scholar’s taste. Some years ago I was obsessed with scholarship on the Mahābhārata and read an inane amount of scholarly works on the Mahābhārata, not only contemporary works but 19th and 20th century works as well. I have forgotten most of it and don’t really care one way or the other now which is probably for the better as much of the ‘scholarship’ is not worth much more than the paper it’s written on.
