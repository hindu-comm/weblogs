South Asia is home to several significant language families, each with its classical antiquity and heritage. We can categorize them into four major language families and a few language isolates, as mentioned below[1](#footnote-1),

1.  Indo-Iranian (Indo-Aryan) Languages

2.  Dravidian Languages

3.  Austro-Asiatic Languages

4.  Tibeto-Burman Languages

5.  Andamanese Languages

The significant language isolates are the Burushaski, Nihali, Kusunda, and Vedda languages of Srilanka.

The Indo-Iranian was further divided into *Indo-Aryan, Iranian*, and *Nuristani* languages. Major sub-families of Austro-Asiatic languages are *Munda*, *Khasi*, and some *Nicobarese* languages. Contemporary linguists agree that most language families are entrants to the Indian subcontinent at different periods over millennia due to various population migrations. The origins of these languages are still being debated and is a highly politicized topic. With extensive bi-lingual and multi-lingual interactions over a long period of interactions among the speakers, the Indian subcontinent becomes a “***Linguistic Area***” as described by linguist M.B. Emeneau.[2](#footnote-2)

According to the 2011 census data, the distribution of language families spoken in India is as follows: Indo-Aryan languages are spoken by about 78.07% of the population, Dravidian languages by 19.64%, Austro-Asiatic languages (specifically the Munda and Khasi subfamilies) by 1.11%, and Tibeto-Burman languages by 0.8% of the people.[3](#footnote-3)

## Indo-Iranian Languages



The Indo-Iranian language family is a major branch of the Indo-European languages, with a significant presence in South Asia. It comprises three main subgroups: **Indo-Aryan, Iranian**, and **Nuristani** languages.[4](#footnote-4) These languages originated from a common proto-language, Proto-Indo-Iranian, which is believed to have been spoken in Central Asia around 2000 BCE. The earliest attested languages of Ind-Iranian languages are Vedic Sanskrit (sacred language of the Vedic Aryans) of Indo-Aryan, Avestan (sacred language of Zoroastrians) of the Iranian branch, and Old Persian of the Iranian branch.

Many modern Indian languages, including Hindi, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi, and Bengali, evolved from Old Indo-Aryan (Sanskrit) through Middle Indo-Aryan (like Prakrits) to modern varieties. Languages, namely Persian, Pashto, Tajiki, Kurdish, etc., of the Iranian plateau to eastern Afghanistan are part of the Iranian branch of the Indo-Iranian family. The Iranian branch showcases influences from Indo-European roots and interactions with Arabic and Turkic languages for centuries. This smaller and lesser-known group is spoken primarily in parts of Afghanistan and the Chitral region of Pakistan. Nuristani languages are distinct but share roots with the broader Indo-Iranian family.[5](#footnote-5)

The Indo-Iranian languages have shaped South Asia's cultural, literary, and religious traditions. Sanskrit, the classical Indo-Aryan language, is the liturgical language of Hinduism and has had a profound influence on regional literature. Meanwhile, Persian, an Iranian language, played a significant role during the medieval period, especially in courts and literature across South Asia.

This family’s linguistic diversity, coupled with its deep historical roots, makes it one of the most influential language groups in the world today, reflecting the shared history and interactions across South Asia and beyond.

## Dravidian Languages



The Dravidian languages form one of South Asia's oldest and most distinct language families, spoken by around 200 million people. The family includes significant languages such as Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, and Malayalam, each with a rich literary tradition and numerous minor languages like Tulu, Gondi, and Brahui. These languages are primarily concentrated in southern India and parts of northeastern Sri Lanka, but smaller communities also exist in Pakistan (Brahui), Nepal, and Bangladesh.

Linguists have classified it as the South, South Central, Central, and Northern Dravidian branches:[6](#footnote-6)

- **Southern Dravidian:** This includes Tamil, Kannada, Malayalam, and
  Tulu. Tamil is one of the oldest living languages, with an unbroken
  literary tradition dating back over 2,000 years.

- **South-Central Dravidian:** This category includes Telugu and Gondi.
  Telugu, the largest Dravidian language by speakers, is prominent in
  Andhra Pradesh and Telangana.

- **Central Dravidian:** A few languages, namely, Kolami, Naiki, Gadaba,
  Ollari, and Paiji.

- **Northern Dravidian:** Languages like Brahui, Kurukh (Kurux), and
  Malto are spoken in isolated pockets. Brahui is unique as it is spoken
  in Balochistan (Pakistan), far from other Dravidian
  languages.[7](#footnote-7)

The Dravidian languages are believed to have once had a wider distribution, potentially influencing the ***[Indus Valley Civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation)***, though this remains speculative. Over time, these languages interacted with **Indo-Aryan** languages and, later, **Persian.**

Dravidian languages are a key part of South Asia’s linguistic heritage. Through their interactions with other languages and cultures, they reflect deep historical roots and adaptability. With millions of speakers across the Indian subcontinent and diaspora communities worldwide, these languages continue to thrive today.

## Austroasiatic Languages



The Austroasiatic language family, one of the world's oldest linguistic groups, is primarily spoken across parts of South and Southeast Asia, with significant representation in India, Bangladesh, and Nepal. It has two major branches: **Munda** (found in South Asia) and **Mon-Khmer** (dominant in Southeast Asia). In South Asia, the Munda languages are particularly prominent, spoken by various indigenous communities across central and eastern India, such as the Santals, Mundas, and Ho tribes.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mjy9!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F197da5df-23ca-43aa-96aa-6e45c078bad2_540x615.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mjy9!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F197da5df-23ca-43aa-96aa-6e45c078bad2_540x615.png)





Three significant branches—Munda, Khasi, and Nicobarese—are spoken in South Asia. Recent ancient DNA research has shed light on the migration of Austroasiatic language speakers from Vietnam to the Odisha region in India around 2000 BCE.[8](#footnote-8) The Munda languages are grouped into North and South Munda languages. In contact with other languages of India, over time, the Munda branch has acquired some Indic language features such as retroflex and SOV syntax.

Overall, the Austroasiatic languages reflect both the ancient roots and dynamic evolution of the linguistic landscape across South Asia, forming a bridge between South and Southeast Asian cultures.

## Tibeto-Burman Languages



The Tibeto-Burman languages are a large and diverse group within the Sino-Tibetan language family. They are primarily spoken across South Asia and parts of East and Southeast Asia, with a notable concentration in Nepal, northeastern India, Bhutan, northern Myanmar, and the Himalayan regions. They cover significant portions of Southeast Asia, the Himalayan region, and the Tibetan plateau. Tibeto-Burman languages typically have fewer speakers than Indo-Aryan languages. Documentation is scarce for the Tibeto-Burman languages compared to the other major language families of South Asia; most languages are still undescribed.

Many Tibeto-Burman languages have small speaker populations and are under pressure from dominant regional languages, such as Nepali, Hindi, or Mandarin. Despite this, some languages—like **Burmese**—have achieved national status, with Burmese serving as the official language of Myanmar. Burmese is among the most extensively documented and historically attested Tibeto-Burman languages, with literary records dating back to the 12th century.

These languages reflect a long history of contact, migration, and evolution in the Himalayan region, making them a key focus for historical and typological linguistics.

## Andamanese Languages



The Andamanese languages represent a group of indigenous languages spoken by isolated tribes on the Andaman Islands in the Bay of Bengal. These languages are highly endangered, with most varieties already extinct or spoken by only a handful of elderly individuals.

Subgroups of Andamanese languages,

- **Great Andamanese Languages**: Historically, there were around ten
  languages divided into northern, central, and southern varieties.
  Today, only a koiné version called *Present Great Andamanese* (PGA)
  survives, blending several northern dialects (like Jeru, Sare, Bo, and
  Khora). Despite this fusion, only a few semi-speakers speak PGA
  fluently on Strait Island. The language exhibits unique structural
  features, including complex possession markers and polysynthetic
  morphology, but it is critically endangered due to the rapid shift
  toward Hindi for daily communication.

- **Jarawa and Onge Languages**: These are spoken by the hunter-gatherer
  tribes of the South and Middle Andaman Islands. The Jarawa, for
  example, remain relatively isolated, resisting external contact, which
  has helped preserve their language to some extent. Meanwhile, the Onge
  population has dwindled, with only a few speakers remaining, making
  their language highly vulnerable to extinction.

- **Sentinelese Language**: This language is unclassified due to the
  tribe’s strict isolation on North Sentinel Island. As a result, no
  linguistic data exists about the Sentinelese language, though it is
  presumed to be distinct from other Andamanese languages.

The Andamanese languages offer a unique glimpse into human prehistory. The tribes are believed to be descendants of some of the earliest human migrations out of Africa, around 70,000 years ago. However, preserving these languages in the face of modern challenges remains daunting, with most now classified as critically endangered or functionally extinct.

## Language Isolates



A language isolate is defined as any natural language that has no demonstrable genetic relationship to any other known language.

### Nihali



Nihali (also called Nahali) is one of the rarest languages in the world, classified as a language isolate, meaning it has no confirmed genealogical relationship with any other known language. A small, marginalized tribal community primarily speaks it in the northern parts of the Bhudana district of Maharashtra. According to the 1961 census, there were 3,600 speakers of this language.[9](#footnote-9) The current speaker population is estimated to be fewer than 2,000, most of whom are multilingual and speak Korku, Hindi, or Marathi. Currently, the Nihals speak Nimari, an Indo-Aryan language. This shift is due to the ongoing linguistic changes within their speech communities.

The origins of the Nihali language remain a mystery. There is speculation that Nihali could be a remnant of an ancient linguistic stratum that existed before the expansion of Indo-Aryan and Dravidian languages in the Indian subcontinent. The word Nahal means either a tiger or lion in their language. The tribal community that speaks is often linked to the ancient community known as Nahalka, mentioned in the Padma Purana as a branch of [Nishadha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nishadha_kingdom) (Niṣadha).

This unique linguistic outlier has long puzzled researchers with its uncertain origins and highly mixed vocabulary. Over 70% of its vocabulary comes from neighboring languages such as Korku, Munda, Dravidian, and Indo-Aryan. However, the core elements of Nihali, such as basic grammatical structures and some essential words, do not align with any known language family.

### Burushaski



Burushaski is a language isolate, primarily spoken in the northern regions of Pakistan, in the remote mountainous areas of Gilgit-Baltistan. The Burusho people, who speak the language, have traditionally lived in these isolated areas, contributing to the preservation of their unique linguistic heritage. The origins of Burushaski are shrouded in mystery; some linguists have speculated that Burushaski may be a remnant of ancient Central Asian languages that predate the arrival of Indo-Aryan languages, and a few other linguists suggest it could have connections to languages in the **Caucasus** region, based on typological similarities. However, no conclusive genetic links have been found yet.

Burushaski is a linguistic puzzle—an island of mystery in a sea of well-classified languages. Its distinct grammatical structure, complex morphology, and unknown origins make it of immense value to linguistic researchers.

### Kusunda



Kusunda is a fascinating and rare language isolate spoken by a small group of people in the mountainous regions of western Nepal, particularly in the districts of Baglung and Syangja. It is considered a "language isolate" because it has no known relatives, meaning it cannot be definitively linked to any other language family. The Kusunda language holds particular interest for linguists because of its isolation, history, and the unique cultural context in which it has survived.
