*Reconstructing Indian Population History*, a paper published in *[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature08365)* by Reich et al. (Sep 2009)[1](#footnote-1), was probably the first landmark study on South Asian population genetics. In collaboration with the *[Centre for Cellular and Molecular Biology (CCMB)](https://www.ccmb.res.in/), [Reich's Lab](https://reich.hms.harvard.edu/)* genotyped 132 samples of Indians from 25 different caste groups from six different states and five different language families in India.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!RqcP!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F729afe4a-a3c3-4ba2-ad6c-16c8096c0854_573x648.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!RqcP!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F729afe4a-a3c3-4ba2-ad6c-16c8096c0854_573x648.jpeg)





This paper proposed a model for genetic model for the South Asian population, categorized them into Ancestral North Indians (ANI), closer to Middle Eastern, West Eurasian, and European ancestry, and Ancestral South Indians (ASI), closer to the Andamanese population distinct from East Asian and ANI ancestry. This article aims to explain the answers to some questions regarding the recent genetic studies in South Asia. It discusses the implications of these studies on our current understanding of human history in the region and sheds light on what they reveal about the Dravidians.

## Acronym War



Acronyms in ancient DNA studies have left everyone with more confusion than providing a clearer picture of the origin and ancestry of the South Asian population. In the last decade, starting with the paper by Reich et al., the use of ANI, ASI, and AASI to describe the ancestry of Indian people became the standard. One might wonder what they mean by those acronyms; how does someone who lives in the farthest corners of India understand this acronym? One must understand how Geneticists have categorized ancient populations worldwide with this recent aDNA research phenomenon.

To understand human migration and the population genetics of South Asian ancestry, we should learn about significant ancient populations that gave rise to the present-day South Asians. Some of the populations that we discuss in this article are as follows,

1.  Aboriginal Indians, aka Ancient Ancestral South Indians (AASI)

2.  Mesolithic/Pre-Neolithic Iranian Hunter-Gatherers

3.  Indus Valley Cline

4.  Western Steppe Herders (WSH) or Steppe Ancestry

5.  Southeast Asian Farmer (SEA) Ancestry or Austroasiatic Ancestry (AA)

6.  Tibeto-Burman Ancestry

### Aboriginal Indians or **South Asian Hunter-Gatherers (SAHG)**



One must travel back 40,000 years ago to understand the original inhabitants of the Indian subcontinent. The geneticists labeled the ancient population of original (again in relative terms) inhabitants of India as **Ancient Ancestral South Indians (AASI)**, which again isn’t an exclusive South Indian population. This ancient ancestral population probably lived in the entire subcontinent as hunter-gatherer societies. This ancient ancestry is sometimes labeled **South Asian Hunter-Gatherers (SAHG)**. This ancestry formed around 40,000 years ago from an Early East Eurasian (ENA) or East Eurasian or **Eastern Eurasian** who were associated with the **[Initial Upper Paleolithic (IUP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_Upper_Paleolithic)** wave of migration following the Out-of-Africa human migration ~60Kya.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!hsw_!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F301a2983-4a3e-4c24-a9ce-7ebe5034a9f7_1840x1164.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!hsw_!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F301a2983-4a3e-4c24-a9ce-7ebe5034a9f7_1840x1164.png)





This population gave rise to the Andamanese Aboriginal ([Sentinelese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentinelese)), [Ancestral Australasians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aboriginal_Australians) (AA), and Ancestral East- and Southeast Asian (ESEA) populations, who continued to migrate into the Indian and Southeast Asian lands and practiced a hunter-gatherer lifestyle. These populations show the closest genetic relationship to each other. This migration of the early human population contributed primarily to the major modern populations of present-day South and Southeast Asia.[2](#footnote-2) This AASI/SAHG ancestry contributes to the modern-day South Asian population at varying percentages, peaking in the tribal populations (i.e., Irula, [Paniya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paniya_people), Gond, etc.).

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CQ8e!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F38a6a982-e8ae-4339-adaa-b025b7e97a63_1920x1590.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CQ8e!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F38a6a982-e8ae-4339-adaa-b025b7e97a63_1920x1590.png)





No ancient DNA was extracted from this AASI population, who might have lived from 40,000 to 20,000 years ago. But the modern-day Andamanese population (Sentinelese and Onge) is closest to AASI/SAHG. As a result, it is used as a proxy population for genetic admixture studies. Research shows significant divergence between Andamanese and SAHG populations.[3](#footnote-3) Onge from the Andamanese population is used as the reference population.[4](#footnote-4) From anthropological data, the present-day Irula, Paniya, or similar groups are closer to the SAHG population with a significant craniometric specialization variation and the deep divergence between the two.[5](#footnote-5) AASI/SAHG ancestry is present at a low level in almost all modern-day Indian populations, specifically among the South Indian population.

### Mesolithic/Pre-Neolithic Iranian Hunter-Gatherers



Anatomically, modern humans with zero archaic hominin (Neanderthal) ancient population originated from the Southern parts of the Middle East or the Arabian peninsula, considered sister lineage to the Eurasian ancestries called “**Basal Eurasians**” 50,000 to 60,000 years ago.[6](#footnote-6) Similarly, another ancient component named “**[Ancient North Eurasian (ANE)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_North_Eurasian#:~:text=In%20archaeogenetics%2C%20the%20term%20Ancient,from%20Afontova%20Gora%20in%20Siberia.)**” formed about ~24,000 years ago in Northern Siberia, related to [Mal'ta–Buret culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mal%27ta%E2%80%93Buret%27_culture)[7](#footnote-7). There's no need to delve into this ancient ancestry for our topic. Still, it provides a perspective to understand some parent populations contributing to South Asian migrations and ancestry. Sometime after the [Late Glacial Maximum (LGM)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Maximum), these ancient ancestries gave rise to the Mesolithic/Pre-Neolithic Iranian Hunter-Gatherers in the [Zagros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagros_Mountains) region of present-day Iran. It is inferred that the admixture of the Pre-Neolithic Iranian Hunter-Gatherers was around 66% of the Basal Eurasians and the rest from the ANE ancestry[8](#footnote-8). There is still a lack of clarity regarding the formation of this population, which is frequently conflated with the **Caucasus Hunter-Gatherers (CHG)** and the significance of ANE admixture in the Iran_Meso/N population. We can skip the details of this conundrum as it is irrelevant to our discussion. It's important to note that the Mesolithic and Neolithic populations were completely different from the current Iranian population, so there should be no confusion between them.

This Iran_Meso/Iran_N population started showing up near the northwestern parts of South Asia around 12,000-8,000 years ago. This eastern Iran_N population shows some inclination towards the AASI/Andamanese ancestry, suggesting an early-AASI mixing with the Iran_N population. This admixture might have occurred in the. Southern Central Asia or Western provides a pathway for early mixture between AASI and Eurasian ancestries.

### Indus Valley Cline



We now move on to the third and most significant population contributing to contemporary South Asians. During the pre-[Neolithic era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) (8,000-4,500 years ago) in the Northwestern parts of the South Asian boundary, present-day Pakistan and Afghanistan, both of the above-mentioned populations started mixing to form a population that went on to establish the **[Indus Valley Civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation)** (3300 BCE to 1300 BCE) or Harappan Civilization.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gQuY!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffb2357cc-cca8-41ea-b193-254178587535_2047x1356.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gQuY!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffb2357cc-cca8-41ea-b193-254178587535_2047x1356.jpeg)





An ancient DNA sample from a person from the ***Hotu and Kamarband Caves*** (Belt Cave) dated to ~10,000 BCE in Iran, and the sample from ***Ganj Dareh*** dates to ~8,000 BCE, both pre-dates agriculture in the Iran plateau, the Iranian component that contributed to the Indus Cline, pre-dated these both samples pointing to a earlier date of Iranian Hunter-Gatherer ancestors and independent development of farming in the Indus region.[9](#footnote-9) The research paper conducted by Shinde et al. concluded that agriculture was not introduced to the Indus region by the Iran_Meso/Neolithic people. Instead, it was an independent development that occurred when the AASI population mixed with the local population in the Northwestern region of India.

Neolithic settlements at ***Mehrgarh** (7000-2500 BCE)* in the Balochistan region of present-day Pakistan are one of the earliest farming and herding societies, dating back to 6000 BCE.[10](#footnote-10)

### Western Steppe Herders (WSH) or Steppe Ancestry



The name ***Western Steppe Herders (WSH)***, *Western Steppe Pastoralists*, or just ***Steppe Ancestry*** refers to a unique ancestral Chalcolithic population from the Steppe regions of the northern *[Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus)*. This ancestral population emerged around the beginning of the 5th millennium BC and then spread to various areas of Europe and Central Asia. The genetic makeup of the *[Western Steppe Herders (WSH)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Steppe_Herdersf)* is believed to have resulted from a fusion of two different earlier ancestral ancestries named *[Eastern Hunter-Gatherers (EHGs)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_hunter-gatherer)* and *[Caucasus Hunter-Gatherers (CHGs)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus_hunter-gatherer)*. The *WSH* ancestry is characterized by a combination of *EHG* and *CHG* ancestral components, with a significant portion of the Y-DNA haplogroup contribution originating from EHG males. The EHGs themselves are a blend of two other ancestral populations called *Western Hunter-Gatherers (WHG)* and *Ancient North Eurasians (ANE).* The WSH ancestry would eventually give rise to many Bronze Age cultures, namely *Yamnaya, Corded Ware, [Bell Beaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Beaker_culture), [Abashevo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abashevo_culture), [Sintashta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sintashta_culture),* etc., across Europe and eventually reached the present-day Central Asia regions.

By 3300 BCE, the Yamnaya culture emerged from the WSH population with some additional Neolithic Farmer population in the northern part of the Black Sea and embarked on an expansion across Eurasia.[11](#footnote-11) This population is generally referred to as the *Steppe Early and Middle Bronze Age (Steppe EMBA).* According to prominent linguists and archaeologists, the dispersal of this population is believed to have contributed to the expansion of Indo-European languages across Eurasia.

### Southeast Asian Farmer (SEA) Ancestry or Austroasiatic Ancestry (AA)



The last significant ancestral population we need to account for is the *Southeast Asian (SEA) Farmer* or Austroasiatic ancestry. The Bronze Age population migrated from Southeast Asia in two phases. The first migration occurred to Northeast India 5.2 ± 0.6 thousand years ago, followed by a second phase of migration to the East India region (Odisha) at 4.3 ± 0.2 thousand years ago, as indicated by a genetic study.[12](#footnote-12) It is widely accepted that these migrations brought the *[Austroasiatic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austroasiatic_languages)* of the Indian subcontinent, such as *[Munda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munda_languages)* and *Santali*.

### Tibeto-Burman Ancestry



Another significant ancestral population in contemporary South Asian ancestry is the population speaking Tibeto-Burman languages in the Northeastern and Himalayan ranges of the Indian subcontinent. The Tibetan-Burman populations in the Tibetan Plateau originated from farmers who migrated from the middle Yellow River basin during the Neolithic period, around 7000 years ago. While the Tibeto-Burman ancestry does not play a significant role in explaining the admixture of the major Indus Valley Cline, it is crucial to recognize the importance of the major ancestral populations that contributed to the formation of contemporary South Asians.
