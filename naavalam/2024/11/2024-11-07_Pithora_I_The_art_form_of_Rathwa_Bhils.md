*[Pithora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pithora_(painting))* is a vibrant mural art tradition still extant among the *Rathwa Bhils* and *[Bhilala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhilala)* tribal communities in the regions of *[Chhota Udaipur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chhota_Udaipur), Jabugam,* and *[Nasvadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasvadi)* taluks of the erstwhile *[Vadodara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vadodara_district)* district by river Narmada in Gujarat, India. The paintings are typically drawn on the walls of the Rathwa people's homes, and the historical significance of this practice may have originated from the cave and rock paintings. The creation of these stunning paintings forms a crucial part of an intricate ritual aimed at fulfilling vows and seeking blessings from their primary deity, ***Baba Pithora***.[1](#footnote-1)

## Ritual behind the tradition



When the wind isn’t blowing in the direction of wealth and prosperity, the head of the household, called ‘*Ghardhani*’ of the Ratwa community, seeks help from the village chief priest, called ‘*Badva,’* consulted to initiate an elaborate ritual to paint Pithora at their homes in the hope of getting boon from their chief deity Baba Pithora. The process begins with unmarried girls from the village or household, known as "*Panduva Lavu*," plastering three chosen walls with cow dung for seven days, usually ending on a Tuesday. On the eighth day, the walls are replastered, known as "*Pandu Dholavanu*," using "*Pandu Maati*" (white clay), before the Ghardhani and the Badva summon the "*Lakharas*," men trained in Pithora painting. The traditional size of the painting is 11x9 feet with decorated borders in triangular geometric patterns called “*heem*,” and it is drawn on "*Osari*," the three walls by the verandah. The rectangular canvas features an entrance with an arch at the center of the bottom, known as “*jhanpo*”, with the four corners symbolizing the end of the earth. During this communal celebration, the Ghardhanis offered food for the feast, while the Lakharas offered khichadi, oil, and chilies on khakhra leaves.

Lakharas mixes the color powder, typically red, green, orange, and blue, with cow’s milk or alcohol made from *[mahua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhuca_longifolia)* flowers. Once the painting is finished, a goat is sacrificed in front of the wall, followed by Badva entering a trance state with a sword. The Ratwas consider Badva possessed by Baba Pithoro himself and foretell through him. He checks for flaws in the painting by pointing the sword at it. There are two main types of Pithora paintings: **Addho (half) Pithora** and **Akkho (complete) Pithora**.[2](#footnote-2) The kind of painting depends on the situation in the household, and the ritual involves the guidance of the *Badva* (spiritual leader), who reads and interprets the vow step by step. Addho Pithora performed in homes facing hardships such as family conflicts, health problems, or poor farming and cattle issues. Akkho Pithoro is a complete Pithora painting focused on life preservation, health, agriculture, and the welfare of the community.

## Baba Pithora, Myth and the Legend



The motifs in Pithora paintings are highly symbolic, illustrating the mythological stories, daily life, and natural elements important to the Rathva. Central figures include ***Baba Ganeh*** (a father figure worshipped first in every ceremony), ***Baba Ind*** (the rain god), and ***Baba Pithora*** (the chief god associated with creation and prosperity). Other key characters include ***Pithori Rani*** (Baba Pithora’s wife), ***Rani Kajal*** (his foster mother), and ***Baar Matha no Dhani*** (a protective deity with twelve heads).

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!K3UR!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0d280615-e4dc-41ed-906b-682d00c45a21_900x565.jpeg)





The paintings also depict **animals** (such as horses, buffaloes, and lions), **celestial elements** (like the sun and moon), and scenes of human activities (such as rituals, farming, and festivals). Nature holds deep significance, with trees like **kadam and tad** playing a role in ceremonies and daily life. **Ancestor worship** is represented through carved horses and village feasts.

The legend behind the Pithora art is that ***Baba Ind***, the ruler of the gods, had seven sisters. One day, one of his sisters, ***Rani Kali Koyal*** *(Kadi Koyal)*, ventured into the forest, where she encountered ***Raja Kanjurana** (Kunjul or Kundu Rano)*. They had an affair, and after nine months and nine days, she gave birth to a son. Fearing her brother *Baba Ind's* reaction, she decided to let the child drift into the Yamuna river on a lotus flower since she was still unmarried. That day, ***Rani Kajal*** and *Rani Makher*, Baba Ind’s other sisters, went to the stream to fetch water and discovered the baby crying. *Rani Kajal* fed the child milk from the flower of the Banyan tree and bathed him with seven auspicious items. She named him "*Pithora*" and took him back to the palace with her.[3](#footnote-3)

Pithora grew into a young boy. One day, while playing, he accidentally broke Rani Kajal’s earthen pot. Angry, she scolded him, saying, “Your maternal uncle controls the kingdom,” hinting that Baba Ind was his uncle. Pithora was determined to learn about his parents, so he went to Raja Indra’s court and introduced himself. King Indra, pleased with his story, welcomed him into the family and offered to find him a bride. However, Pithora wanted to uncover his parents' identity first. Raja Indra organized a grand court gathering, inviting all the gods, kings, and respected citizens. When Pithora entered, he identified Raja Kanjurana as his father. Following much celebration, Pithora married Pithori in a grand wedding attended by the gods, who arrived on horses and elephants. This is reflected in Pithora's painting, showing the gods' arrival alongside Pithora and Pithori. Pithori is the daughter of Abho Kunbi and Mathari, a farming couple, typically drawn at the bottom of the art and believed to be the creators of agriculture.

## The Narrative of Baba Pithora



The artwork is divided into three main scenes: the top depicts the celestial realm, the middle shows the wedding procession, and the bottom illustrates the lifestyle of people on Earth. The upper part symbolizes the realm of gods and always includes a Sun (Huriya Dev) and a Moon (Handrayo Dev).

Baba Ganeh is the first motif, typically drawn on the right side of the painting. He is often depicted in blue and carries a ‘hukka’. Important festivals and occasions among the Rathva community begin with the worship of Baba Ganeh. The Rathvas also refer to him as Ganpati Dada, who is characterized by having an elephant’s trunk. In Pithora paintings, Baba Ganeh is portrayed as a father figure.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!rdGK!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F15225b9a-30e9-495b-9cbe-f14adc082928_1000x828.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!rdGK!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F15225b9a-30e9-495b-9cbe-f14adc082928_1000x828.jpeg)





Baba Ind is typically the second motif drawn. He is the lord of rain and the protector of all animals. In Pithora paintings, he is depicted as strong, youthful, and brave, often holding a parrot in his hand. The Rathvas vow to Baba Ind for blessings such as better health for their families and livestock, improved crop yields, and the gift of a child. Every five years, a festival honoring Baba Ind is celebrated in villages of Chhota Udepur and Panchmahal districts in Gujarat, featuring traditional songs, dances with dhol and pehi music, and rituals performed by the Badva.

Baba Pithora is depicted as young, strong, and masculine, holding a parrot and a flower in his hand. For the Rathva, Baba Pithora is a combination of various gods, animals, nature, and all the creations of the universe.[4](#footnote-4)

Pithori, Baba Pithora’s wife is drawn holding a fan in her hand. She is revered during periods of unpredictable monsoons. The painting depicts her as a celestial figure and a devoted wife prepared to support her husband in all his endeavors.

Rani Kajol is drawn at the left end of the art and holds a comb, symbolizing her power to cleanse the world. She is also revered as Kuldevi, the goddess of the clan. The temple of Rani Kajal is situated on a mountain near Mathvad village, along the banks of the Narmada River, at the border of Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh.

Baar Matha no Dhani ('The Enlightened One with 12 Heads') is a fascinating figure with twelve heads holding a snake in one hand and a sword in another or snakes on both hands. The twelve hands are believed to symbolize providing protection from all twelve directions to Rathwas. He is known for offering protection to Rani Kali Koyal during her labor, which is why he is a significant and recurring character in many Pithora paintings. One can’t ignore the uncanny similarity in the iconography between Baar Matha no Dhani and Ravana from Ramayana.

In the third segment at the bottom, Raja Bhoj is on his elephant with ambari in the middle. There is also a representation of a farming couple, Abho Kunbi and Mathari, who are believed to be the first people to start agriculture on Earth. On some frescos, in the top right corner a man in a blue horse-drawn, who is supposed to be the messenger of gods, is Handol.

## Discussion



Most researchers overlook it as a tribal art form that needs preservation. They have never considered that it may carry the memories of their ancestors. Thirty years ago, art historian Jyotindra Jain examined Pithora as a ritual art form, integrating it with the formal elements of painting. He concluded that this rich ornamental art form was a pictorial conception of the total body of myth and ritual collectively inherited from generation to generation.[5](#footnote-5) Even though he tried to dismantle the notion that all regional cultural phenomena are vernacularized offshoots of the Sanskritic great tradition, he connected Baba Ind to the ancient Vedic and Puranic agricultural cult of Indra.

A more recent work by anthropologist Vishvajit Pandya interprets the paintings with a politico-economic significance of the Rathwa Bhils or Adivasis against the feudal and colonial past.[6](#footnote-6) One must question the previous interpretations and the connections to the Adivasis of central India.
