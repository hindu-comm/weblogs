### Purāṇic Connection



*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* describes the lineage of King Yadu as follows. Yadu had four sons, *Sahasrajit*, *Kroṣṭu*, *Nala*, and *Raghu*. *Sahasrajit’s* son was *Śatajit*, and his three sons were *Haihaya*, *Hehaya*, and *Veṇuhaya*. *Haihaya* had Dharma, his son was Dharmanetra. Dharmanetra had Kunti and *Sāhaṃji* in the next generation. His son was *Mahiṣmant*; according toHarivamsha(33.1847), the city *Māhiṣmatī* was founded by him. *Veṇuhaya* had four sons, *Kṛtavīrya*, *Kṛtāgni*, *Kṛtadharma*, and *Kṛtaujas*.

*Kṛtavīrya’s* son was Arjuna, the lord of seven continents, with the appellation Sahasrabahu; he was also called *Chakravartin.*[4](#footnote-4) In *Arjuna* *Kṛtavīrya’s* lineage, who was believed to be killed by *Paraśurāma*, comes the *Vṛṣa* and his son Madhu. Their descendants are called *Vṛṣṇi*, and the descendants of Yadu are called *Yādavas[5](#footnote-5)*. *Kṛṣṇa* is one of the essential heroic figures among the different clans of Yādavas. The children of Kroṣṭu, the second son of Yadu, were *Caṣibindu*, *Ciyamaha*, and *Vidarbha*; from the last son, the *Vidarbha* lineage emerged, and *Cidi*, the third son of Vidarbha, founded the *Cedi* dynasty.

The different tribes of the *Yādavas* with the Purāṇic accounts and the dynasties of early-historic to the medieval periods of India trace their lineage back to Yadu and descend the *Candravaṃśa* or the Lunar Dynasty. Out of these eclectic Yādava tribes, the *Ābhiras*, *Andhakas*, *Vṛṣṇis*, *Vidarbhas*, *Cedis*, and *Sātvatas* are some notable clans with partial purāṇic narratives and natural history.

## The Dravidian Perspective



Historically, *Yādavas* are associated with the cattle-rearing clans across pre-historic India.

#### Vṛṣṇi Vīras in pre-Historic Tamiḻakam



One of the *gaṇās* (clans) among the *Yādavas* was the *Vṛṣṇis*, and the *Vṛṣṇi* *Vīra* cult is well known in both north and south India. In pre-historic India, the *Vṛṣṇi Vīras* were either worshipped individually or in groups (triads, tetrads, or pentads).

TheSaṃkarṣaṇais later known to be*Balarāma* (बलराम), son ofVasudeva (Anakadundubhi), *Vāsudeva* (later identified as *Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa*), and *Ekānaṁśā*triad was the most popular among the pentadSaṃkarṣaṇa, *Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa* (वासुदेव),*Pradyumṉā* (प्रद्युम्न), son of*Kṛṣṇa*, *Sāmba* (साम्ब), another son of*Kṛṣṇa*, and *Aniruddha* (अनिरुद्ध), son of *Pradyumṉā*. Their prominence is around Mathura in the north and the Tamiḻ country from the early historical period[6](#footnote-6).

#### Māyōn, the Pastoral God



*Kṛṣṇa* is known as *Māyōn, Nēmiyōn, Māl(ōn), and Neṭiyōn* in the Caṅkam literature*.* *Māyōn* is a compound word from the Dravidian root PDr.*\*mā*, with the primary meaning of 'black/dark/cloud' \[DEDR#3918\][8](#footnote-8) and the third person male honorary suffix used to denote kings/gods*-ōn (*`3sg.m`).

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!10w6!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fbucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc22bdc20-f694-419a-b143-7a384bf0c4c1_2462x350.png)

As the Sanskrit word*Kṛṣṇa's* adjective**meaning 'black/blackness/dark blue/attractive,'[9](#footnote-9) *Māyōn* corresponds the same.The historicity and relevance of the*Kṛṣṇa's*presence in southern India are controvertible, while his origin story seems to be in Mathura. One of the critical elements to be assessed here is the tale of*Kṛṣṇa*stealing Gōpis' clothes described in Bhāgavatapurāṇa (10.22). ScholarsFilliozat (1962)[10](#footnote-10) and Hardy (1983)[11](#footnote-11) place the Bhāgavatapurāṇa in the 10th century CE and 8-9th century CE, respectively. The Dravidian scholarM. B. Emeneu argues that the Caṅkam literature portrays this motif in one of the poems 3-4 centuries before Bhāgavatapurāṇa where Māyōn climbed up the tree to provide Gōpis the leaf dress.[12](#footnote-12) Moreover, the scholars argue that Bhāgavatapurāṇa was penned in the southern part of India as well, where the pre-existing chronologies could have played a role in influencing the narratives mentioned in Bhāgavatapurāṇa.

#### Nappiṉṉai



Nappiṉṉai is introduced as*Kṛṣṇa's*spouse in Cilappatikāram; both *Cilappatikāram* and *Maṇimēkalai* mention that*Kṛṣṇa* wedded**Nappiṉṉai in a traditional *Āyarkuṭī* custom.

If we were to wonder what is the relevance of the Yādavas in a Dravidian context and the connection of Vṛṣṇi Vīras with the Tamiḻ lands, we have to explore the yore of Vēḷirs in pre-historic Tamiḻakam.

#### Vēḷirs of Tamiḻakam



The history of*Vēḷirs*can't be ignored when exploring the history of Tamiḻakam.*Vēḷirs*were the chieftains of Tamiḻakam who were contemporaneous to the*mūvēntars, Cēra, Cō*ḻas, and*Pāṇdyas*during the Caṅkam (300 BCE - 300 CE) period. Next to*Cēra, Cō*ḻas, and*Pāṇdyas*in the Caṅkam poems, the*Vēḷir*chieftains were eulogized equally by the poets and pāṇars. Instances where Caṅkam poems mention*Vēḷirs*and*Vēntars*together; '*iruperu vēntaroṭu vēḷir cāya* - *Maturaikkāñci 55*', '*vel pōr vēntarum vēḷirum oṉṟu moḻintu - Patiṟṟuppattu 49*', etc.[18](#footnote-18) Before the formation of the Tamiḻ kingdoms by the*Vēntars*post-second-urbanization of south India, the*Vēntars*regarded Vēḷirs. The poets' descriptions of them, such as the well-known one, "*tolkudi* - the ancient clan," reveal their antiquity.

##### The Vēḷir Conundrum

We could reasonably establish a juncture that there appeared to be a narrative in ancient Tamiḻakam that the people who identified themselves as Vēḷirs were migrants from the north, particularly from Dvārakā[21](#footnote-21), present-day Gujarat (Kathiawar). Though we can't substantiate these claims with corroborating evidence through modern scientific inquiries, we could reason that this erudite proof of Caṅkam aligns with the Purāṇic claim concerning sage Agastya. Furthermore, the*mūvēntars*,*Cēra, Cō*ḻas, and *Pāṇdyas* frequently asserted their ancestry as Vēḷir as well[22](#footnote-22). However, the connection between the Vēḷir of Tamiḻakam and the Yādava tribes in the north remains a puzzle. To study the relationship between the Vēḷir and Yādavas, we should explore the relations beyond Tamiḻakam (present-day Tamilnadu and Kerala). Now let us start exploring the list of *Vēḷir* chieftains and kings of Tamiḻakam. *Ciṟupāṇāṟṟuppaṭai*, a *Caṅkam* work, extols seven charitable *Vēḷir* chieftains (*vaḷḷal*) of Tamiḻakam, who were *Vēḷ Āy* of Potikai hills, *Vēḷ Naḷḷi* of Naḷimalai, *Vēḷ Pāri* of Paṟampu hills, *Vēḷ Pēkan* of Potiṉi (Paḻaṉi), *Vēḷ Atiyaṉ* of Takaṭūr, *Vēḷ Ōri* of Kolli hills, and *Kāri Vēḷ* of Tirukkōvilūr; similarly, *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 158 poem by *Peruñcittiraṉār* adds *Vēḷ Eḻiṉi* added *Atiyaṉ* instead.*Vēḷ Āy* of Potikai hills, also known by different epithets '*Māvēḷ Āy*', '*Tērvēḷ Āy*,' and his abode praised as "*ā'ay maḻaitavaḻ potiyil* - Kuṟuntokai. 84", "*teṉticai āykkuṭi*". We infer from these exalted acclaims that his capital was *Āykuṭi*, located in the southern end of Tamilnadu. According to Tolkāppiyam, one of the monikers that the people of*Mullai*, the cattle-rearing pastoral lands known by are*Āyar*and*Āycciyar*. *Āy Aṇṭiran* was Vēḷ Āy's other name, which helps us to hypothesize that one name of the *Vēḷir* clans could have been *Āy*, and they were known as *Āyar*.

It appears the Āy tribes have a semantic connection to the early society's agro-pastoralism. If we delve into the history of the name Yādava, we can learn about the origins of various clans that evolved from Dravidian pastoral terms. For example, the word for goat in Proto-Dravidian is \*yāṭu and someone who rears a goat is known as "yāṭavan (yāṭu + avan (3sg. masculine suffix)", which eventually led to the present-day term Yādava. FC Southworth, a scholar of Davidian linguistics, also suggests a similar explanation for the name Yādava since there is no confirmed Indo-European etymology[24](#footnote-24). Ātan (ātan \< yātan \< yāṭan) and Māṭan are common names from the early historic periods.

We should oppugn the etymology of *Āndhra*, the modern state in India, and its historical origins at this juncture.*Saurapurāṇa*(*सौरपुराण*) documents about an ancient country*Āndhra*which must be shunned along with *Aṅga, Vaṅga, Kaliṅga, Surāṣṭra, Gurjara, Ābhira, Kauṅkaṇa, Draviḍa,* *Dakṣiṇāpatha*, and *Magadha*, polities of ancient India, for not practicing*dharma*and unholy[25](#footnote-25). One could infer that this*Śaiva Purāṇa*could have been composed in the heartlands of Āryavarta[26](#footnote-26), as it expels the clans that were outside of it. According to Monier Williams Sanskrit dictionary,*āndhra*means 'belonging to the Andhra people, Andhra being the king' and*andhra*being the Andhra tribe[27](#footnote-27).

*Aitareya Brahmana (ऐतरेय ब्राह्मण)*of*Ṛgveda*depicts that Andras left the banks of Yamuna to settle in the south and also mentions that the sons of*Viśvā-Mitra*were banished from the*Brahmavarta,*the holy land of Āryan tribes[28](#footnote-28). Although one could overlook these affirmations as Purāṇic myths, it is worth an inquiry to reckon if the migration of Vēḷirs mentioned in Old Tamiḻ Caṅkam literature. I want to draw a parallel and analogize the***Āy Anṭiran***'s journey with the claim from*Aitareya Brahmana*to propose that the tribe of***Aṇṭiran***migrated from the north and the early settlers in both Tamiḻakam and Āndhra-deśa.

According to the census report from 1911, Ahirs or Āhirs were a tribe of shepherds or herdsmen who identified themselves as Gōpas of God Kṛṣṇa.[29](#footnote-29) They are a caste of cowherds and cattle breeders historically located in the Khandesh, Kathiawar, Nāśik, and Palanpūr regions.[30](#footnote-30) Generally, the name Ahir is believed to be derived from Ābhira, a tribe name cited in ancient Purāṇas and historical, literary sources such as*Periplus of the Erythraean Sea.*Therefore, the reasoning is Ahir, a Prakrit version of Sanskrit Abhira. Still, I would like to propose that***Āhirs***were the***Āyars***mentioned in Tolkāppiyam as the people of*mullai*(the pastoral lands). Note Ābhira is also one of the tribes said ousted from Āryavarta in*Saurapurāṇa.*

A 9th-century CE bilingual Tamiḻ and Sanskrit stone inscription from the *Koṅku* region in Tamilnadu explicitly translates *Vañci Vēḷāṉ* (a descendant of the *Vēḷir* clan) as *Vañci* *Vṛṣṇi*.[31](#footnote-31) It is proof that the medieval Sanskrit scholars associated the *Vēḷirs* with the *Vṛṣṇis* of the north.

The ***Ābhiras*** are an interesting tribe that has been mentioned in some of the oldest known literature. For instance, the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* identifies them as the people who lived in the Sindh region of present-day Pakistan. The origin and meaning of *Ābhiras* is a topic of ongoing scholarly debate with no clear consensus. One theory proposed by A.P. Karmarkar suggests that the name may have come from the Dravidian word Āyir,[32](#footnote-32) meaning 'cowherds.' Cow herding has been a part of human society for centuries, as evidenced by references in ancient texts such as the Aitreya Brahmana, where they are referred to as *Vaṣāh*. However, there is some confusion as they are sometimes inaccurately interpreted as Vatsa or Vaṃśa. In early Vēdic literature, the term *vaṣa* generally referred to cows. In Harivamsa, it is mentioned that *vraja* and *ghoṣa* were both used as enclosures for cow herds. Additionally, ghoṣa is referred to as *Ābhirapalli*[33](#footnote-33). According to the Caṅkam poems, there is a tribe called *Āviyar*, and the *Vēḷir* kings are associated with this tribe as their rulers. One*Puṟanāṉūṟu* poem extols a *Vēḷir* king named*Pēkaṉ* as '*āviyar kōvē*.’

I believe that the *Āhirs* and *Ābhiras* may have originated from the *Dravidian* tribes *Āyars* and *Āviyars*. The interchange of -v- to -b- is regular in the Indo-Aryan-speaking languages of India. So it's possible these tribes were originally Dravidian-speaking and later assimilated into the Indo-Aryan-speaking community.

##### The Vēḷir Migration

As we delve into the *Vēḷirs* of *Caṅkam*, a question arises about whether *Āhirs* and *Ābhiras* were the only *Vēḷir* tribes or if there were others. Additionally, it is worth considering if they migrated from the Mathura region to the South of India. The answer lies within the kingdoms that traced their lineage to *Yaduvaṃśa* (also known as *Yādava* *Vaṃśa*) or *Candravaṃśa*. Major tribes that traced their origins to Yadu were the *Haīhayās*, *Ābhiras*, *Andhakas*, *Vṛṣṇis*, *Vidarbhas*, *Cedis*, *Bhōjas*,*Śūrasenas,*and *Sātvatas*. According to the *Vaṃśānucarita* sections in most Purāṇas, the *Yādavas* were initially located in various areas such as the Aravalli, Gujarat, Narmada, the Northern Deccan, and the eastern Ganges regions.[34](#footnote-34) The *Mahābhārata* mentions the Yadus or Yādavas, a group of different clans that ruled the Mathura region. They were exiled from Mathura and eventually settled in Dvārakā. The *Mausala Parva* section in *Mahābhārata* recounts the fratricidal war of the *Yādavas* and *Pāṇdavas*, resulting in their downfall.[35](#footnote-35)

During the *Vēdic* period in ancient India, the *Chedi* kingdom was one of the sixteen *Mahājanapadas* in the *Majjhimadesa* (Middle Country). The *Chedi* Kingdom was founded by the *Haihaya-Vrṣhnis*. *Chidi*, the son of *Kaisika* and ruler of *Vidarbha*, established the *Chedi* dynasty within the kingdom.[36](#footnote-36) The 1st century B.C. monarch of Kalinga deśa, Kharavela (Khārabēḷa), claimed to be from the Cedis lineage, and his dynasty was known as*Mahāmēghavāhana*. The first line of the well-known Hathihumpa inscriptions reads, "chetaraja-vasa-vadhanena," which refers to someone who expanded the Chedi kingdom. Scholars have provided various interpretations for the name *Kharavela*, but I would elucidate that as a compound of two Dravidian words, "*kāra* (kārāḷaṉ *காராளன்*)" and "*vēḷaṉ* (*vēḷāḷāṉ* *வேளாளன்*)," which both refer to agriculture.

In the early 1st century A.D., the region between *Toṇṭai Nāṭu* and *Cōḷa Nāṭu* was called *Cēti Nāṭu* in Tamilakam. The *Vēḷir* kings of the *Malayamān* family suffixed *Chēdirāyan* as their title and ruled during the *Caṅkam* period.[37](#footnote-37) In the Yēwūr inscription from 1077 A.D. of the Western Chāḷukya period, a*Rāṣṭrakūṭa*king is referred to as 'aracan-māraḥ-chēdi-chēdiya'’ which suggests that the*Rāṣṭrakūṭas*were establishing associations to the *Chedis*.[38](#footnote-38) The *Chaḷukyas* also have their roots in the *Yaduvaṃśa (in more detail for a different post)*. Numerous town and village names that were either *Vēḷūr* or *Bēlur* in the Deccan region help to connect the dots on how the migration of *Vēḷirs* from the north left a strand on the path. Ellora could possibly be a Vēḷūr once. The contemporary cētu- name (i.e., cētupati) is derived from this.

### Discussion



Drawing a parallel between the migration of Vēḷirs and the migration of Dravidian languages after the decline of the Indus-Valley Civilization could reveal answers to the mysteries surrounding the past of the IVC. The current geographical areas where the Dravidian language is spoken suggest that the language may have spread south and eastward from a northwestern region. It could be suggested that the emergence of a synthesized culture between the Indo-Aryan-speaking Aryans and Dravidians was the reason for this.
