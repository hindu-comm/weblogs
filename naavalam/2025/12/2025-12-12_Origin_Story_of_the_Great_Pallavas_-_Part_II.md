The principal reason for investigating the contemporary dynasties of the early Pallavas lies in their role as critical missing links for resolving the enigma of Pallava origins. Having examined two emergent polities, the Śālaṅkāyanas and Bṛhatphalāyanas, another key dynasty warrants further exploration before advancing conclusive postulates.

## Āndhra Ikṣvākus



The Āndhra Ikṣvākus (c. 225–325 CE) emerged as immediate successors to the Sātavāhanas in the eastern Deccan (modern Andhra Pradesh and Telangana) following the collapse of imperial Sātavāhana authority. Ruling from their capital at Vijayapurī (modern Nāgārjunakoṇḍa), the dynasty pursued political legitimation through claims of descent from the Sūryavaṃśa of Ayodhyā, thereby asserting *Kṣatriya* status. However, historical evidence and Purāṇic references identifying them as Śrīparvatīyas (rulers of the Nallamala hills) indicate that they were indigenous chieftains who had previously functioned as mahātalavaras (feudatories).

The dynasty was founded by **Vāsiṣṭhīputra Cāṃtamūla I**, who asserted sovereignty through the performance of the *Aśvamedha* sacrifice and consolidated his position via matrimonial alliances with the Cuṭu-Nāgas of Banavāsī. His successor, **Vīrapuruṣadatta**, oversaw the dynasty’s political zenith, notably forging a marriage alliance with the Śakas of Ujjayinī (Rudradāman’s lineage). The later reign of **Ehūvala Cāṃtamūla** is characterized as a “Golden Age” for architecture, marked by the construction of early structural Hindu temples such as the Puṣpabhadrasvāmi temple.

The Ikṣvāku era is distinguished by a unique gender-based religious patronage system. Male rulers adhered staunchly to Brahmanical orthodoxy, worshipping deities such as Mahāsena (Kārttikeya) and Śiva while performing Vedic rituals like the *Vājapeya* and *Aśvamedha*. Conversely, royal women served as devout patrons of Buddhism, financing the extensive construction of *stūpas* and *vihāras* at Vijayapurī.

The Ikṣvākus, despite claims of Sūryavaṃśa lineage, maintained deep cultural and biological ties to Nāga tribes of the Kṛṣṇa delta, evidenced by intermarriages with the Cuṭu-Nāgas and prevalent Nāga iconography (e.g., Mucaliṇḍa Nāga) in the Amarāvatī school at Nāgārjunakoṇḍa. The dynasty proved short-lived, collapsing c. 325 CE before the expanding early Pallavas under Siṃhavarman I, who annexed the Kṛṣṇa valley into Toṇḍaimaṇḍalam.

### Mahātalavaras



A defining characteristic of the Āndhra Ikṣvāku administrative system was the prominence of the ***mahātalavara***, a high-ranking dignitary office frequently held by indigenous feudatories within the Kṛṣṇa river valley and its periphery.

The title *mahātalavara* is a compound formed by the Sanskrit prefix *mahā* (”great”) and the term *talavara*. While early Indologists like Vogel initially struggled to define the term, referencing its appearance in the Jain *Kalpasūtra* as a “knight” or “bodyguard” (Vogel, 1929), later epigraphical analysis places it firmly within a Dravidian substrate. D.C. Sircar (1966) and subsequent linguists identify *talavara* as a Sanskritized derivative of a Dravidian etymon. It is cognate with the Tamil *talaivaṉ*, signifying “headman,” “chieftain,” or “leader,” derived from the Proto-Dravidian root \**tal* or *talai* (”head”) (Burrow & Emeneau, *DEDR* 3103). Consequently, within the administrative lexicon of the Deccan, *mahātalavara* denoted a “provincial governor” or “high chieftain,” representing the integration of indigenous Dravidian lineage heads into the Sanskrit/Prākṛt bureaucratic framework of the post-Sātavāhana polities.

The mahātalavaras were integral to the consolidation of Ikṣvāku sovereignty, functioning not merely as bureaucratic appointees but as crucial political allies incorporated into the royal structure through systematic matrimonial alliances (Gopalachari, 1941). Mirroring the diplomatic strategies of the Sātavāhanas, who forged dynastic ties with the Western Kṣatrapas, the Ikṣvāku monarchs cemented their authority by intermarrying with mahātalavara families.

Epigraphic evidence from the *Āyaka* pillars at Nāgārjunakoṇḍa highlights this practice. Notably, *Cāntisirī*, the sister of the dynasty’s founder *Vāsiṣṭhīputra Cāṃtamūla*, was given in marriage to the *mahātalavara* Skandaśrī of the *Pūkīya* clan (Epigraphia Indica, Vol. XX). As Hanumantha Rao (1973) observes, these feudatory families were instrumental in the religious landscape of Vijayapurī, serving as prolific patrons of Buddhism and financing the construction of major *mahācaityas* despite the Brahmanical orthodoxy of the reigning monarchs.

Interestingly, the mahātalavaras were primarily associated with three dominant chieftaincies:

1.  **The Pūkīyas** (Skt. ***Puṣyakandīyas***): Controlling the tracts
    south of the Kṛṣṇa River, corresponding to the modern Guṇṭūr and
    Prakāśam districts (Sarma, 1982).

2.  **The Hiraññakas**: Located in the dominance of the modern Kadapa
    (Cuddapah) and Kurnool regions, likely associated with the
    Hiraṇya-rāṣṭra mentioned in later Pallava plates.

3.  **The Dhanakas**: Associated with the Nalgonda region and parts of
    the erstwhile Hyderabad state.

Among these three families or clans, the Hiraññakas and Dhanakas may be set aside, as their primary regions of habitation fall outside the scope of Pallava origins research. The third clan, however, the Pūkīyas (Skt. *Puṣyakandīyas*), as attested in the Sanskrit charters of the Ikṣvāku plates merits closer scrutiny.

## Pūkīyas **(**or Puṣyakandīyas)



Pūkīyas (or Pūgīyas) constituted a clan or family settled in the Andhrāpatha region. D. C. Sircar identifies Andhrāpatha (“the Andhra province”) as the territorial core south of the Kṛṣṇa River, bordering the Śālaṅkāyanas of Veṅgī, extending across the Nellore and Prakāśam districts up to the Peṇṇā River in the south, and lying to the west of the Śrīparvata hills previously ruled by the Ikṣvākus (Sarma, 1982). This area was historically known as Pūki-nāḍu (or Pūgi-nāḍu; Skt. *Pūki-viṣaya*), a toponym that persisted into the Eastern Cālukya period. The Puṣyakandīyas (Skt. पुष्यकन्दीयाः) represent the same social group as the Pūkīya (or Pūgīya) family, designated under a Sanskritized form of the name in later historical records.

The Pūgīyas appear in the Prākṛt inscriptions of the early Ikṣvāku period as *Pūgīyānaṃ*, while *Puṣyakandīyānaṃ* occurs in later Sanskrit charters.

> *Mahātalavarasa Vāsiṭhīputasa **Pūkiyānaṁ***
>
> *Mahātalavarasa Vāsiṭhīputasa **Pusyakaṁdīyānaṁ***

Exploring whether *Pūkīya* represents a Prākṛtized form of the Sanskrit word *Puṣya* (referring to the star or month) reveals no standardized phonological shift (/ṣy/ to /ky/ or /gy/) in South Dravidian or Prākṛt phonology. The Sanskrit cluster *ṣya* (ष्य) typically undergoes assimilation, smoothing into a simpler sound, rather than hardening to a velar *k* or *g*. For instance, in Tamil, Skt. *Puṣya* (पुष्य) evolves as *Pūcam* (பூசம்), with /ṣy/ simplifying to /c/, whereas Prākṛt attests *Phussa*, where /ṣy/ assimilates via gemination to /ss/.

The more plausible derivation of *Pūkīyas* lies in Skt. *Pūga*, denoting a guild or territorial assemblage of people.

### Pūṅgavrōlu



Scholars such as K.V. Rāmeśvara Śarma and C.A. Padmanābha Śāstrī have proposed that the name of the modern city Oṅgōle represents an eroded form of the ancient Pūgīya capital, reconstructed as *Pūṅga-vrōlu*. In the phonological evolution of Old Telugu (and Dravidian languages generally), a hard stop like *g* commonly acquires a preceding nasal (*Pūga \> Pūṅga*), as seen in the reflex *Teluṅgu* for *Telugu*. The suffix *vrōlu* derives from standard ancient Telugu *prōlu* (ప్రోలు), denoting a “fortified city,” “capital,” or “seat of government,” which softens to *vrōlu* following a nasal or vowel (*Pūṅga + prōlu \> Pūṅga-vrōlu*).

This pattern recurs in historic Telugu toponyms like Bhaṭṭiprōlu (Bhatti’s town/Pratipārudrapura), Mayidavōḷu, and Niḍubrōlu. Thus, *Pūṅga-vrōlu* literally translates to “the capital city of the Pūgīyas,” aligning Oṅgōle with Pūgi-nāḍu/Pūki-viṣaya in post-Ikṣvāku records.

## Significance of Gaṇa, Śreṇi, and Pūga in Ancient Polity



*Kāśikāvṛtti* (commentary on Pāṇini’s *Aṣṭādhyāyī V.3.112*) and the legal text *Kātyāyana Smṛti* explains the definition of *Pūga* as follows:

> *nānājātīyāḥ aniyatavṛttayaḥ artha-kāma-pradhānāḥ saṅghāḥ pūgāḥ*

*Pūga*s represent associations of individuals from diverse castes (*nānājātīya*) and indeterminate professions (*aniyatavṛttayaḥ*), united primarily for the pursuit of wealth and pleasure. In contrast, a *gaṇa* or *kula* denotes a connection through a common ancestor (*ekajātī*). The *Yājñavalkya Smṛti* (II.30) delineates an ancient judicial hierarchy wherein the *pūga* served as a crucial appellate body, positioned above trade guilds but below the king.

> *Nṛpādhikṛta Pūga Śreṇī Kulāni*
>
> *Raja (King) \> Pūga (Neighborhood/Town Assembly) \> Śreṇī
> (Professional Guild) \> Kula*

The *Mitākṣarā* commentary (*vivṛti*) on the *Yājñavalkya Smṛti* elucidates that the *pūga* holds precedence over the *śreṇī*, as it encompasses the entire public of a locality (across all castes), whereas the *śreṇī* pertains solely to a specific trade interest. Kauṭilya’s *Arthaśāstra* (Book 9, Chapter 2) advises the king on recruiting diverse troop types, explicitly enumerating *śreṇī-bala* (guild levies) alongside forces raised from *pūga*s. The terms *pūga* and *gaṇa* are closely related in that both fall under the broader category of *saṃgha* (corporate bodies) in ancient India. Yet they denote fundamentally different forms of social organization: the *pūga* is primarily territorially based, whereas the *gaṇa* is organized on the basis of kinship.

Based on the evidences, the Pūgīyas of Pūgi-nāḍu (modern Oṅgōle/Guṇṭūr) likely originated as a territorial confederation of local tribes, clans, or *jātī*s, with their evolution from a “local assembly” (*pūga*) to a “ruling house” (*Pūgīya*) paralleling the trajectory of medieval merchant guilds (e.g., the Aiṉṉūṟṟuvar), which eventually commanded armies and controlled territories.

The region around Masulipatnam, encompassing the Kṛṣṇa–Godāvarī doab and the lower Kṛṣṇa valley, was a major centre of organized commerce and merchant activity from at least the late Mauryan and early post-Mauryan periods. Archaeological and numismatic evidence from nearby sites such as Bhattiprolu, Amarāvatī, Ghāṇṭasāla, and Nāgārjunakoṇḍa attests to intensive long-distance trade, including Indo-Roman exchange, which in turn implies the presence of structured mercantile bodies (*śreṇī*, *pūga*/*nigama*) coordinating production, credit, and maritime ventures.

### Āyudhajīvī Saṃghas



In Pāṇini’s *Aṣṭādhyāyī* (c. 5th–4th century BCE), political units are classified into two broad categories: *ekarāja* (monarchies) and *saṃgha* (republics or oligarchies). Among the *saṃghas*, he specifically identifies a subcategory termed *āyudhajīvi-saṃgha* (आयुधजीविसङ्घ), denoting “a community (*saṃgha*) that lives by the profession of arms (*āyudha-jīvin*)”. According to Pāṇini, these warrior republics or guilds were located in the Vāhīka region (roughly modern Punjab and parts of Pakistan).

> *Āyudhajīvisaṅghāññyaḍvahīkeṣvabrāhmaṇarājanyāt* \|\| 5.3.114 \|\|

Pāṇini further lists specific tribes under this *āyudhajīvi-saṃgha* category:

1.  *Vṛkāṭṭeṇyaṇaḥ* (Vṛka tribe, literally “wolves”), a warrior clan
    likely specializing in weaponry.

2.  *Dāmanīyāditrigartaṣaṣṭhacchaḥ* (referring to the Trigartaṣaṣṭha, or
    “Six Trigartas,” a confederation of six warrior republics in the
    Jālandhara/Kaṅgrā region, whose members, per the *Kāśikāvṛtti* were
    the Kauṇḍoparatha, Dāṇḍaki, Krauṣṭaki, Jālamāni, Brahmagupta, and
    Jānaki)

3.  *Parśvādibhyaḥ yaudheyādibhyaś ca aṇañau* - the Parśus and, notably,
    the renowned Yaudheyas.

According to V. S. Āgrawala, Pāṇini’s differentiation of Vāhīka *saṃgha*s is significant, as these groups were renowned for their martial autonomy and relative absence of rigid caste-based monarchies, in contrast to the Gaṅgātic plains. Pāṇini observes that while these communities functioned as Kṣatriyas (by virtue of warfare), they did not invariably conform to the orthodox Vedic definition of *rājanya* (consecrated monarch or noble), hence the grammatical exclusion *abrāhmaṇa-rājanyāt*.

These *āyudhajīvi* groups are frequently correlated with the “autonomous tribes” encountered by Alexander the Great in 326 BCE, such as the Malloi and Oxydrakai who resisted fiercely.

Interestingly, Pāṇini discusses another categogy, Rājanyādi-gaṇa, which refers to communities governed by a consecrated warrior-noble class (*Rājanya*) and mentions that Śālaṅkāyanas belonged to this category in Gaṇapāṭha list (Udumbaras, etc.)

> *Rājanyādibhyo vuñ* \|\| 4.2.53 \|\|
>
> *Atha rājanyādiḥ \| Rājanya \| Bābhravya \| Śālaṅkāyana ...* \|\|

## Discussion 1: Southern Federation of Āyudhajīvī Saṃghas



The Andhra Ikṣvākus claimed descent from the legendary Sūryavaṃśa, but the academic consensus overwhelmingly points to an indigenous tribal origin in the Kṛṣṇā valley. The Vāyu and Matsya Purāṇas do not call them “Ikṣvākus”; instead, they categorise them as Śrīparvatīya Andhras. Interestingly, Pāṇini mentions another āyudhajīvi group in the northwestern region named Parvatīya, and the Kāśikā commentary lists Hṛd-golīyas and Rohitagirīyas as the tribes who lived by the sword in the hilly region.

> Andhrāḥ Śrīparvatīyāśca te dvi-pañcāśataṃ samāḥ
>
> *Ayudhajīvibhyaścha parvate* (IV.3.91)

K.P. Jaiswal argued that these arājatā warrior republics of the Vahīka Saṃghas, such as the Kṣudrakas (Oxydrakai), Mālavās (Malloi), Yaudheyas, Parvata Saṃghas such as the Aśvakāyanas, and Pūgas, along with the rājanyādi-gaṇa Śālaṅkāyanas from the northwestern corridors, from the Indus to Pañjāb, migrated into the Deccan and Central India, where this was reflected during the Sātavāhana rule. He identified the Bhojas and Raṭṭhikas of the Dakṣiṇāpatha as the southern equivalents of the northern āyudhajīvin societies, governed by “leaders” and “assemblies” rather than absolute kings.

I would like to further substantiate that the warrior republics, once powerful in the northwestern parts of India, migrated south during the Iron Age under pressure from the Śakas and Kuṣāṇas, probably after Alexander the Great’s campaigns, and established similar republics in the Dakṣiṇāpatha, extending all the way to the Kṛṣṇā valley. Comparing this hypothesis with the Vēḷir migration legends of Tamil literature (eighteen clans of warriors who migrated from Dvārakā under Sage Agastya) suggests that the memories captured in Tamil literature could hold true, though the Vēḷir migration likely occurred during the early Iron Age. The Raṭṭhikas of the Sātavāhanas are linguistically and functionally the ancestors of the Mahāṭṭalavaras and the Raṭṭhis of the Āndhra region (present-day Reḍḍis).

### Story of Mahātalavara to Mahārāja



Vāsiṣṭhīputra Cāntamūla I, the founder of the Ikṣvāku dynasty, served as a **Mahātalavara** to the Sātavāhana king Pulamāvi III and seized the opportunity of the disintegrating Sātavāhanas to establish his monarchy. Aśvamedha, once an exclusive privilege of cakravartins, was a tradition broken by Mahātalavara Cāntamūla to declare (aśvamedhayājin) his legitimacy as a **Mahārāja**. The Rentala Pillar Inscription proves that by c. 230 CE, Sātavāhana authority had been completely wiped out in the Palnāḍu region.

Cāntamūla ensured and followed the strategic partnership through marital alliances, like Sātavāhanas with his contemporary feudatories. He married his sister, Cāntisirī, to the Mahātalavara Skandaśrī of the powerful Pūkīya clan, establishing a strong partnership without realizing that it would backfire in four generations to form a new dynasty.

I would like to propose that Siṃhavarman I of the powerful Pūkīya warrior clan or republic, with its capital at the ancient Pūṅgavṛolu (present-day Oṅgōle), emerged as the early Pallavas after victory over the Ikṣvākus with the support of mahārāja Jayavarman of the Bṛhatphalāyanas, whereupon they moved their base to Kāñcipuram to establish the dynasty.

The emergence of the Bṛhatphalāyanas, Śālaṅkāyanas, and early Pallavas followed the demise of the Ikṣvāku dynasty, with all three declaring independence through Vedic legitimacy yajñas conducted around the same timeline. These groups shared strikingly similar administrative structures, linguistic features, onomastics, and royal emblems. The only gap in this interconnected picture remains the Pūgīyas suggests a pivotal role in post-Ikṣvāku power shifts, awaiting fuller epigraphic corroboration.
