The origins of the **Pallavas** remain among the most debated subjects in South Indian historiography. Modern academic consensus favors an Āndhra provenance, viewing them as successors to the Sātavāhanas who expanded southward to Kāñcipuram after the Āndhra Ikṣvāku decline, based primarily on linguistic and administrative evidence in their earliest Prakrit inscriptions, such as the Mañcikallū, Mayidavōḷu, and Hirahadaḡalli plates of Śiva-Skandavarman (c. 275–300 CE). Alternative theories posit local Tondaimaṇṭalam roots or northern (Pahlava) migrations, though epigraphic data from Āndhra sites like Guṇṭūr and Bellary districts lend the strongest support to the former.

This study is an attempt to examine the prevailing scholarly consensus on Pallava origins critically.

## Principal Inscriptional Evidence



The three principal inscriptional sources of the Early Pallavas are the *Mañcikallū*, *Mayidavōḷu*, and *Hirahadaḡalli* copper plates. The *Mañcikallū* plates, from a village in Andhra Pradesh’s *Palnāḍu* district (near *Ikṣvāku* capital *Vijayapurī*/*Nāgārjunikoṇḍā*), are in Prākṛt and issued by Siṃhavarman I (*Palavāṇaṃ Sīhavammaṇa*), c. 290–320 CE. Their explicit “victory” grant (*vaijayike*), distinct from typical *puṇya* or *dharma* endowments, suggests a military votive offering, positioning Siṃhavarman I as the conqueror who sacked *Vijayapurī* and terminated *Ikṣvāku* rule; the phrase *Palavāṇaṃ Sīhavammaṇa* marks the earliest epigraphic attestation of the Pallava name, while the absence of *mahārāja* implies he was then a general or emergent warlord.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!E88h!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F90a229f0-1922-4e7a-89c7-3efcac8dd7db_1280x960.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!E88h!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F90a229f0-1922-4e7a-89c7-3efcac8dd7db_1280x960.png)





The Mayidavōḷu plates, also in Prākṛt from Śiva-Skandavarman (year 10, c. 300 CE) while he was still a crown prince (Yuvamaharaja), likely during the reign of his father, Siṃhavarman I, record a land grant in the Puṣyapuṉguḍi region near modern Nellore, employing administrative terms like viṣaya and Brahmin gotra settlements that mirror Sātavāhana-Ikṣvāku practices. This inscription furnishes the earliest irrefutable epigraphic attestation that the Pallavas established **Kāñcipuram** as their primary capital while administering the Andhra region, specifically the Guṇṭūr and Kṛṣṇa districts as a northern province.

> *Kāñcīpurato Yuvamahārājo Bhāradāya-ya-sagotto Pallavāṇaṁ
> Sivakhaṁdavammo…*

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oB15!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F75221916-e4b0-45c2-b8f9-b38aa1933f2d_1024x559.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oB15!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F75221916-e4b0-45c2-b8f9-b38aa1933f2d_1024x559.png)





This inscription constitutes yet another military votive dedication commemorating the annexation or conquest of Dhanyakāṭa (Amarāvatī), the erstwhile eastern capital of the Sātavāhanas, and further attests that the early Pallavas ascended to power by overthrowing the Andhra Ikṣvākus.

> *Dhaṃñakaḍe vāpataṃ āṇapayati…*

Despite being the conquerors, the early Pallavas adopted the administrative framework (districts designated as *rāṣṭras*, officials termed *amātyas* and *rāṣṭrikas*) and linguistic conventions (initially Prākṛt inscriptions, transitioning to Sanskrit) of their Sātavāhana and Ikṣvāku predecessors. The Āndhra Ikṣvākus (*Ikkhākūṇaṃ*, as attested in Prākṛt epigraphs; c. 225–325 CE) succeeded the Sātavāhanas directly in the eastern Deccan, emerging likely as local chieftains functioning as feudatories (*mahātalavaras*) under Sātavāhana suzerainty.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!DKTl!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F630d1663-3976-427b-9054-1a7bf86507f3_1280x337.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!DKTl!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F630d1663-3976-427b-9054-1a7bf86507f3_1280x337.png)





Similarly, the Hirahadaḡalli plates from Bellary district (c. 280–300 CE) attest early Pallava presence north of the Tuṅgabhadrā, with shared onomastics (e.g., Skandavarman) and bull (*Ṇaṇḍi*) emblems linking them to contemporaries like the Śālaṅkāyanas.

## Toṇḍaimaṇḍalam of Tamiḻakam



At the core of the Pallava realm lay Toṇḍaimaṇḍalam (Toṇḍai Nāḍu), the historic region centered on the capital city of Kāñcipuram. Although it likely originated as the tribal territory of the Kurumbas, its emergence as a political heartland is traditionally associated with the legendary chieftain ***Toṇṭaimāṉ*** ***Iḷam*** ***Tiraiyaṉ***, from whom the inhabitants came to be known as Toṇṭaiyar. Toṇṭaimāṉ occupies a distinctive position in Tamil historical tradition, functioning as a pivotal intermediary figure linking the early Sangam Cōḻas with the emergent Pallava polity. Far from being a purely legendary ruler, he is securely attested as a poet-king, celebrated as the central hero of the Sangam work *Perumpāṇāṟṟuppaṭai* and credited with compositions in the anthologies *Puṟanāṉūṟu* and *Naṟṟiṇai*.

His name and lineage are framed by a distinctive origin narrative preserved in the *Maṇimēkalai*. The epic recounts a union between the Cōḻa king Kiḷḷivaḷavaṉ (Velvērkiḷḷi) and the Nāga princess Pīlivaḷai, daughter of Vaḷaivaṇan; when compelled to depart the human realm, Pīlivaḷai sends their infant son across the sea to his father, marking him by tying a *toṇṭai* creeper around his neck, so that the child, borne safely to shore by the waves (*tirai*) together with this vine, acquires the name Toṇṭaimāṉ Iḷam Tiraiyaṉ and is remembered as the progenitor of a new royal line.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!IQm1!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F69ec7b5c-56c8-4fbe-9078-92595718f5d2_1024x559.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!IQm1!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F69ec7b5c-56c8-4fbe-9078-92595718f5d2_1024x559.png)





The Mackenzie Collection of compiled manuscripts (early nineteenth century) records the figure of ***King Ādondai*** (or *Toṇṭaimāṉ Cakravartti*), described as a son of Kulōttuṅka Cōḻaṉ and credited with the “civilizing” of Toṇḍaimaṇḍalam. According to these traditions, he achieved this by subjugating the Kurumbas, portrayed as a “wild tribe” of putative northern Indian origin who had earlier occupied this sector of the Dandaka forest after exterminating its prior inhabitants. Ādondai Cakravartti is frequently identified with the Cōḻa-Nāga ruler Toṇṭaimāṉ Iḷam Tiraiyaṉ, who is said to have established a polity at Kāñcipuram, and it is plausible that the inhabitants or descendants of Toṇṭaimāṉ’s line were known as Toṇṭaiyar, as attested in Sangam literature.

## Progenitor of the Pallavas



Historians and historical linguists have drawn on this narrative tradition to propose that the Pallava dynasty originated from the lineage of Toṇṭaimāṉ Iḷam Tiraiyaṉ. Whether this constitutes definitive proof that Toṇṭaimāṉ Iḷam Tiraiyaṉ was the actual progenitor of the Pallavas remains doubtful, not least because the extant epigraphic record, beginning with Simhavarman I, renders any direct linkage highly complex and methodologically precarious. Among the figures presented as his contemporaries in the Sangam corpus is ***Kaḷvar Kōmāṉ Pulli*** of Vēṅkaṭa Nāṭu (the present-day Tirupati region), and *Akanāṉūṟu* 295 characterizes Pulli’s domain at the northern fringe of ancient Tamiḻakam as a borderland where the spoken language had already diverged significantly from classical Tamiḻ.

> *புடையல்அம் கழல்கால் புல்லி குன்றத்து…. மொழிபெயர் தேஎம்*
>
> *puṭaiyalam kaḻalkāl pulli kuṉṟattu…. moḻipeyar tē’em*

Sangam sources further attest the northward extent of the Toṇṭaiyar, indicating their presence as far as Vēṅkaṭa Nāṭu. One verse, in particular, evokes the martial Toṇṭaiyar “with battle-famed elephants” dwelling up to the lofty, white-cascaded heights of Vēṅkaṭam, thereby situating this group within the northern borderlands of ancient Tamiḻakam.

> *வினைநவில் யானை விறற்போர்த் தொண்டையர் இனமழை தவபம் ஏற்றரு நெடுங்கோட்டு ஓங்குவெள்
> ளருவி வேங்கடத் தும்பர்க்…*
>
> *viṉainavil yāṉai viṟaṟpōrt toṇṭaiyar iṉamaḻai tavapam ēṟṟaru
> neṭuṅkōṭṭu ōṅkuveḷ ḷaruvi vēṅkaṭat tumpar…*

During the Sangam period (c. 300 BCE–300 CE), the region of **Vēṅkaṭam**, corresponding roughly to the present-day Tirupati area, appears to have been inhabited by multiple groups, such as the Kaḷvars, Kuṟumpars, and related clans who are collectively subsumed under the ethnonym Toṇṭaiyar in the poetic record. By contrast, the territories lying to the north of Vēṅkaṭam are designated as Vaṭuka Nāṭu, whose inhabitants (Vaṭukar) are described as speaking a language that had diverged from Tamiḻ (*moḻipeyar tē’em*), suggesting that these regions fell outside the cultural-linguistic ambit of ancient, unified Tamiḻakam.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ztSB!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4bc90683-118b-40f8-8acc-62af70a45658_1024x559.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ztSB!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4bc90683-118b-40f8-8acc-62af70a45658_1024x559.png)





On a cautious reading, this implies that the lands beyond Vēṅkaṭam during the Sangam age were not Tamiḻ-speaking and were not conceptually integrated into the Tamil macro-region, aligning instead with northern Deccan polities. Correlating this literary picture with external political history, the tract north of Vēṅkaṭam can be associated with the Āndhra sphere: initially under Sātavāhana rule in the first century CE and, following the decline of Sātavāhana authority in the later second century due to internal fissures and external pressures, subsequently under the emergent Āndhra Ikṣvākus along the Kṛṣṇa River between roughly 180 and 320 CE.

## Contemporaries of Pallavas



Before attempting any firm conclusions regarding Pallava origins, it is necessary to learn more about the contemporary dynasties.

### Śālaṅkāyanas



The Śālaṅkāyanas, who rose alongside the early Pallavas in the aftermath of Ikṣvāku's decline, ruled the Veṅgī region north of the Kṛṣṇa River, with their capital commonly identified with present-day Peddavegi near modern Elluru. Modern scholarship has frequently treated the Śālaṅkāyanas and Pallavas as “sister” dynasties, noting a cluster of shared features: both employed shared Bull (*Ṇaṇḍi*) as royal emblems, inaugurated their rule with ‘victory’ inscriptions plausibly linked to the fall of the Ikṣvākus, issued their earliest copper-plate charters in Prākṛt, and a Prākṛt-to-Sanskrit linguistic shift at a later stage.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!7y25!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdf796f8e-3f6d-425a-b91f-52385cd35e2e_1024x559.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!7y25!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdf796f8e-3f6d-425a-b91f-52385cd35e2e_1024x559.png)





Further convergences include the adoption of analogous Brahminical *gotra* affiliations, self-representation as Brahma-Kṣatriyas, and striking parallels in royal nomenclature; for example, the Pallava names Siṃhavarman, Skandavarman, and Nandivarman correspond closely to the Śālaṅkāyana Devavarman, Hastivarman, Skandavarman, and Nandivarman, and finally, six generations of each dynasty issuing grants without mutual acknowledgment or conflict. These affinities bolster the Andhra provenance, potentially portraying Pallavas as fragmented Sātavāhana successors alongside Śālaṅkāyanas.

The testimony of Ptolemy, who refers to a group called the *Salakenoi* living north of the *Maisoloi* (usually associated with the Masulipatnam/Kṛṣṇa region), suggests that this clan was already settled in Veṅgī by at least the second century CE and functioned for some time as local chiefs before attaining wider political prominence in the fourth century. This, in turn, indicates that the Śālaṅkāyanas were established north of the Kṛṣṇa River from at least the second century onward.

### Br̥hatphalāyanas



The Br̥hatphalāyanas constituted a short-lived but historically significant post-Ikṣvāku polity that ruled portions of the Kṛṣṇa delta, particularly around Pithunda near modern Machilipatnam and the Tenali region, in the early fourth century CE. Their sole securely attested ruler, Mahārāja Jayavarman, is known from the **Kondamudi** copper-plate charter, a Prākṛt inscription issued from his victorious camp at Kūdura (Guduru) that records the grant of the village Pāṇṭūra (Patur) to eight Brahmins and portrays him as a devotee of Maheśvara; paleographic and political synchronisms place his reign circa 300–330 CE, broadly contemporary with the early Pallavas and Śālaṅkāyanas.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ylHY!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fde31f178-f05f-4673-887c-19b81f2a4237_1024x559.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ylHY!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fde31f178-f05f-4673-887c-19b81f2a4237_1024x559.png)





The dynasty’s name, Br̥hatphalāyana, generally interpreted as a Brahminical *gotra* designation meaning “descendants of the Br̥hatphala clan,” is often linked to the *Bahaphala* lineage mentioned in Ikṣvāku records, though a minority view derives it instead from *br̥hat* + *palāyana* (“great migration”), implying a possible history of mobility. Emerging likely as local officials or feudatories under the Ikṣvākus who asserted autonomy in the Kṛṣṇa district after the latter’s collapse, the Br̥hatphalāyanas were soon constrained by the expansion of the Śālaṅkāyanas in Veṅgī to the north and the Pallavas advancing from the south, and their territory was ultimately partitioned between these successor powers by the mid-fourth century CE.
