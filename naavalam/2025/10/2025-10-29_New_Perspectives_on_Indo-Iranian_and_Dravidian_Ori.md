## A Paradigm Under Scrutiny



The origin and spread of Indo-Iranian and Dravidian populations has long been one of the most contentious topics in South Asian prehistory. Traditional models, heavily influenced by linguistic reconstruction and archaeological evidence, have posited a Bronze Age migration of Indo-Iranian speakers from the Pontic-Caspian steppes into Iran and the Indian subcontinent around 2000-1500 BCE[1](#footnote-1). However, recent genetic studies are painting a far more complex picture that challenges fundamental assumptions about these population movements and raises profound questions about the relationship between language, culture, and genetics.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!q4Ix!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F12ff7770-aa0b-498f-abc9-38ea002b6736_3194x1478.png)





Two groundbreaking studies from 2024-2025 have thrown particularly striking wrenches into the conventional narrative. The first, examining ancient DNA from the northern Iranian Plateau spanning 4700 BCE to 1300 CE, has revealed an unexpected complete absence of the R1a y-haplogroup, traditionally considered the genetic signature of Indo-Iranian migrations, in all historical period samples. The second, focusing on the Koraga tribe of Southwestern India, has identified a previously unknown “*Proto-Dravidian*” ancestral component dating to approximately 4400 years ago, suggesting a more complex demographic landscape during the Indus Valley Civilization period than previously imagined.

## The Iranian Plateau Paradox: Continuity Without Steppe Signatures



### The Missing R1a Mystery



The study by Ala Amjadi et al. (2025) analyzed 50 samples from nine archaeological sites across Iran, with particular focus on the Achaemenid, Parthian, and Sassanid periods (355 BCE–460 CE) in northern Iran[2](#footnote-2). The most striking finding was the complete absence of the R1a haplogroup in all 19 historical period male samples. This is particularly puzzling given that modern Western Iranians carry significant proportions of R1a Y-DNA lineages, and the Parthians themselves claimed origins among Central Asian nomadic tribes where R1a-Z93 dominates.

Instead, the study documented J1 and J2 dominance from the Bronze Age through historical periods, alongside G, L, and T haplogroups. This genetic profile shows remarkable continuity with local Chalcolithic and Bronze Age populations rather than any significant steppe ancestry. The historical samples demonstrated 45-51% ancestry from Caucasus Hunter-Gatherer (CHG) and Early Neolithic Iranian sources, positioning them as intermediates on an east-west genetic cline across the Persian Plateau.

### The Gol Afshan Tepe Revelation



Perhaps equally significant is the Early Chalcolithic genome from Gol Afshan Tepe (5th millennium BCE), which predates all other published Chalcolithic genomes from Iran. This individual showed predominantly Early Neolithic Iranian genetic ancestry with an intriguing 8-10% additional Andamanese Hunter-Gatherer (AHG) or South Asian Hunter-Gatherer (SAHG) ancestry. This AHG-like ancestry appears to represent a previously uncharacterized genetic element that distinguished the Iranian Plateau’s population from more western groups, suggesting greater genetic diversity within prehistoric Iran than previously recognized.

The presence of this component, which the authors suggest is not gene flow from the Indo-Iranian borderland but rather reflects “unascertained genetic diversity within the prehistoric Iranian Plateau,” adds another layer of complexity to our understanding of the region’s demographic history.

## Alternative Models for Indo-Iranian Dispersal



### The Zagros Hypothesis



The alternative model proposed for Proto-Indo-European (PIE) origins places the homeland not in the Pontic-Caspian steppes, but in the North Mesopotamia-Zagros region during the 7th millennium BCE. This theory suggests that PIE peoples formed through the mixing of North-Central Zagros herders with Northeastern Mesopotamian farmers, developing a strong pastoral culture already well-connected through obsidian trade networks.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0fIk!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5607eef6-03b7-48f7-8782-66f74dedc6a9_1200x724.png)





According to this model, the spread of Indo-European languages was intimately connected with the distribution of Chaff-Tempered pottery (also known as Vegetal-Tempered or Organic-Tempered ware). This pottery tradition, with its distinctive slab or coil construction methods, allegedly spread from the North Mesopotamia-Zagros region to:

1.  **South Caucasus** - forming the Shomu-Shulaveri culture, which
    ultimately contributed to the Core Yamnaya formation

2.  **Eastern regions** - reaching Mehrgarh II phase (5000-4000 BCE) and
    subsequently the Indus Valley Civilization

3.  **Central Anatolia** - forming the Hittite civilization

4.  **Southwest Central Asia** - giving rise to Tocharian speakers

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!dezK!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7213fdfb-156a-4e68-b15d-4f94465f076b_3396x1852.png)





### The Mehrgarh Connection



The archaeological evidence from Mehrgarh II is particularly compelling in this alternative model. The appearance of Chaff-Tempered pottery at Mehrgarh II, showing exact matches in construction techniques (Sequential Slab Construction) with pottery from the Lake Urmia region, suggests direct cultural transmission. The admixture dates between Iranian farmer and AASI (Ancient Ancestral South Indian) ancestry in Indus Valley samples coincide with the Mehrgarh II timeline, supporting this connection.

Furthermore, the migration of specific Y-haplogroup subclades (L1a, J2a-M410, and J2b-M102) from West Asia to South Asia during the Mehrgarh II period provides genetic corroboration for these population movements. This suggests that Indo-Iranian languages may have arrived in South Asia much earlier than traditionally thought, potentially as early as 5000 BCE rather than 1500 BCE.

## The Dravidian Enigma: A Fourth Ancestral Component



### The Koraga Discovery



The study of the Koraga tribe by Sequeira et al. (2025) has identified what may be a crucial missing piece in the South Asian genetic puzzle[3](#footnote-3). The Koraga, a marginalized indigenous community speaking a North Dravidian language, carries a distinct ancestral component that the researchers term “Proto-Dravidian.” This component:

- Dates to approximately 4400 years ago (2370 BC)

- Coincides with the Mature Harappan period

- It is distinct from the three previously identified ancestral
  components (Iranian plateau farmer-related, Pontic-Caspian steppe
  pastoralist-related, and Andamanese hunter-gatherer-related)

- Shows connections to 10,000-year-old Neolithic samples from the Zagros
  mountains

### The Elamo-Dravidian Connection



The genetic findings align remarkably well with the Elamo-Dravidian linguistic hypothesis, which posits a connection between the ancient Elamite language of Iran and Dravidian languages. The 10,000-year-old ancestry connection with Neolithic samples from Iran’s Zagros mountains matches the time depth linguists ascribe to the hypothetical Elamo-Dravidian linguistic phylum.

The study demonstrates that this Proto-Dravidian component is widely distributed among modern Indian populations, with notable exceptions being the Önge and Irula tribes. It appears in higher proportions in southwestern and Dravidian-speaking populations and is present in both Dravidian and Indo-European speaking groups. Crucially, it appears from the Mature Harappan phase onwards in ancient DNA samples, contributing approximately 23% of ancestry in modern Dravidian-speaking groups.

## Reconciling the Evidence: A Synthesis



### Multiple Waves, Multiple Sources



The emerging picture suggests that the demographic history of Iran and South Asia cannot be reduced to a simple model of steppe pastoralist invasions bringing Indo-European languages. Instead, we see evidence for:

1.  **Deep Local Continuity**: The Iranian Plateau shows remarkable
    genetic stability from the Chalcolithic through historical periods,
    with minimal steppe impact even in regions that served as major
    trade and migration corridors.

2.  **Early Agricultural Spread**: The distribution of Chaff-Tempered
    pottery and associated genetic signatures suggests early population
    movements from the North Mesopotamia-Zagros region that may have
    carried Indo-European languages long before the Bronze Age.

3.  **Complex Admixture Events**: The Mature Harappan period (2600-1900
    BCE) appears to have involved multiple structured admixture events
    between farming groups carrying varying proportions of Iran
    Neolithic, AASI-related, and Proto-Dravidian ancestries.

4.  **Post-4.2k Climate Event Dynamics**: The 4.2 kiloyear climate event
    may have forced steppe populations to move to southern regions where
    they were absorbed by local populations rather than replacing them,
    explaining the limited genetic but significant linguistic impact.

## Implications and Future Directions



### Rethinking the Steppe Hypothesis



While the steppe hypothesis remains valid for Northwest Indo-European branches (Italo-Celtic-Germanic and Balto-Slavic), its applicability to Indo-Iranian languages needs serious reconsideration. The evidence suggests that:

- Steppe ancestry alone is insufficient to explain the Indo-European
  language spread

- The role of the Iranian Plateau as a source rather than a recipient of
  population movements may have been underestimated

- Multiple models may be needed for different branches of the
  Indo-European family

### The Harappan Question



The identification of the Proto-Dravidian component and its appearance during the Mature Harappan period raises fascinating questions about the Indus Valley Civilization:

- Was the IVC multilingual, with both Proto-Dravidian and early
  Indo-Iranian speakers?

- Did different occupational groups speak different languages?

- How did the collapse of the IVC affect linguistic geography?

### Modern Implications



These findings have profound implications for understanding modern South Asian populations:

- Most Indians retain traces of a “Dravidian substratum” regardless of
  their current language

- The genetic landscape reflects deep historical continuities rather
  than recent replacements

- Social stratification may preserve ancient genetic signatures longer
  than previously thought

## Conclusion: Embracing Complexity



The new genetic evidence forces us to abandon simplistic models of mass migration and population replacement. Instead, we must embrace a more nuanced understanding of human prehistory that acknowledges:

- The persistence of local populations even in the face o f cultural and
  linguistic change

- The importance of geographic corridors and ecological niches in
  shaping genetic diversity

- The complex relationship between biological ancestry, cultural
  identity, and linguistic affiliation

The story of Indo-Iranian and Dravidian origins is not one of conquest and replacement, but of interaction, adaptation, and synthesis. The Iranian Plateau and Indian subcontinent emerge not as passive recipients of external migrations, but as active centers of demographic and cultural innovation that shaped the genetic and linguistic landscape of Eurasia.

The absence of R1a in ancient Iran and the discovery of Proto-Dravidian ancestry remind us that the past is often stranger and more complex than our models predict. Rather than seeing this complexity as a problem to be solved, we should embrace it as a more accurate reflection of the rich tapestry of human history.

The convergence of genetics, linguistics, and archaeology is revealing a past where rigid boundaries between populations, languages, and cultures dissolve into networks of interaction and exchange. In this new paradigm, the spread of Indo-European and Dravidian languages appears less as a series of dramatic invasions and more as a gradual process of cultural negotiation, elite adoption, and selective integration spanning millennia.

Future research priorities should include:

- Sampling from underrepresented regions and time periods

- Developing models that can accommodate language shift without genetic
  replacement

- Investigating the role of social stratification in preserving genetic
  signatures

- Exploring the relationship between ecological changes and population
  dynamics

Only by embracing this complexity can we hope to understand the true story of how the diverse populations of Iran and South Asia came to be, carrying within their genomes and languages the echoes of migrations, meetings, and mixtures stretching back to the dawn of agriculture and beyond.
