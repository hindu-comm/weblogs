# Two was a Coincidence, Three is a Conspiracy: The Story of the Guardian Rider at the Boundary

### How One Dead Warrior became a God in a hundred villages

A thousand miles apart, in two languages that share not a single root, two village gods ride out after dark on the same clay horse. For a long time the obvious question was how the idea traveled between them. The better question is what they both grew out of.

Walk to the ragged edge of a village in the red-dirt Rāḍh (রাঢ়) country of western Bengal, past the last house, to where cultivation gives way to scrub. Often you will find an *aśvattha*, a peepul, and beneath it a few stones. Not a statue or a carved face. Just a lump of rock, rounded, smeared rust-red with vermilion, anonymous as a riverbed cobble.

That is a god, and his name is Dharmaṭhākur (ধর্মঠাকুর), and he is the high lord of the village.

And around him, crowding the platform, sometimes spilling into the dirt, lies a clustered herd of terracotta horses. Long-necked, some no bigger than your hand, some taller than a child, baked the color of dried blood. Left there, one by one, by people who got what they prayed for.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ufpR!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F856adb9c-83e0-461c-90b6-e82dcc06346c_1024x768.jpeg)





Now travel south, across the Vindhyas, across the Deccan, cross into the flat green Tamil country where the paddy meets the palmyra. Walk again to the edge of the village, this time toward the irrigation tank, usually to the west, and you’ll find another shrine. The god here is Ayyaṉār (ஐயனார்), and just outside, in the dark, stands his enforcer, Karuppaṇṇasāmi (கருப்பண்ணசாமி), the Black Lord, sword in hand, the one who drinks the blood his master is too kingly to touch.

And ranked in rows facing the wilderness, staring out at everything that wants to come in are the horses. Again, terracotta. Some of them monstrous, six meters, ten meters, brick-and-plaster giants. Left there, one by one, by people who got what they prayed for.

The villagers in Bengal will tell you Dharma rides his horse at night to guard the fields. The villagers in Tamil Nadu will tell you the same thing about Ayyaṉār, and the old priests will swear they’ve heard the hooves.

The resemblance is arresting, in both regions a male guardian deity occupies the village edge; in both he is served by non-Brahmin priests; in both he is approached through possession and oracular speech; in both his characteristic votive is a clay horse made by a caste of potters; in both a solar undertone shadows the cult; and in both the deity is, comparatively late, drawn upward into the Sanskritic pantheon and glossed as a form of, or kinsman to, Śiva.

Two gods at the two ends of the subcontinent, with two unrelated languages spoken as the mother tongue. Two histories that, as far as anyone can document, never once touched.

So how do they have the same horse?

## The God who refused to become a Statue



Dharmaṭhākur (ধর্মঠাকুর), also Dharmarāj or simply Dharma, is the high *grāmadevatā* (village deity) of large parts of the Rāḍh region of western West Bengal, Bāṅkuṛā, Birbhum, Bardhaman, and adjoining districts. And his single most defiant feature is that he has no face. Across a millennium of Brahmanical pressure to give every folk god a body, a consort, a marble image, and a towered temple, Dharma held the line as a shapeless vermilion stone under the open sky or under a tree, and only sometimes enshrined in a built temple. The folklorist Jawhar Sircar calls this one of the most impressive expressions of autochthonous stubbornness in all of Bengali religion, the god who would not be turned into a statue.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!EPbX!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F072f03ae-d0cd-4005-aaf6-0a6bf67a9cb6_771x1024.jpeg)





Dharma is worshipped in the dry, hot Bengali months of Baiśākh, Jyaiṣṭha, Āṣāṛh when the land is gasping for the monsoon, and occasionally on the last day of Bhādra. He is a deity of justice and death, but also, and this is structurally central, a deity of agricultural and human fertility, of rain, and, by a widely reported interpretation, of the sun. He’s been fusedover centuries with the*Sūrya*andwith *Varuṇa*, *Viṣṇu*, *Yama*, *Śiva*, and even the *Buddha*. The horse is Dharma’s *vāhana*, and the votive horse is offered to fulfill wishes; an assembly of terracotta horses of various shapes and sizes, representing sacrifice for wish fulfillment, is perhaps peculiar to Dharmaṭhākur.

Who serves this god? Not Brahmins. The Bāuri, Bāgdi, Ḍom, Hāḍi, and Kuḍmi are the communities at the bottom of the caste ladder. Brahmin priests, the sources say, historically kept their distance for fear of losing caste by association. Sit with that for a second. The Brahmin was afraid the folk god would contaminate him.

## The Roar in the Spring



Once a year, the quiet stone explodes and gets into the festive mode. The festival is Gājan (গাজন), Dharmer Gājan, and it falls at the parched end of the Bengali year (Caitra). Lower-caste men take vows, become temporary ascetics, and put their bodies through hell, fasting, possession, fire, the hook-swinging of Carak, where men hang from flesh-piercing iron.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!AO15!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2ccf1706-f2fe-4b31-b942-67650baacceb_1024x684.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!AO15!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2ccf1706-f2fe-4b31-b942-67650baacceb_1024x684.jpeg)





The name itself may come from *garjan* (”roar”), the sound the devotees make to wake the god. The sound-world of the festival is itself coded by caste; the *ḍhāk* drum is played by lower-caste *ḍhākis*, a point Sarbadhikary uses to argue that religious belief is constituted through drum-sound experience. And the anthropology here is razor-sharp. Sukanya Sarbadhikary, building on Ralph Nicholas, frames Gājan as the anti-Durgā-Pūjā, where the goddess’s festival is urban, Sanskritic, Brahmin-run, and ruthlessly hierarchical. Gājan is rural, communitarian, low-caste, and flat, a festival where men don’t worship the god from below so much as become him. Sarbadhikary states plainly that this Śiva festival was originally associated with the pre-Aryan, non-Brahmanical deity, Dharma, and that even after later Brahmanical orders replaced Dharma with a combination of the classical Purāṇic god Śiva and a Bengali form of the peasant god, Gājan, its essential composition, rituals, and ontology remain strongly indigenous.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pzK3!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4b5b158e-db1d-4931-b506-0c9150acb819_1024x722.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pzK3!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4b5b158e-db1d-4931-b506-0c9150acb819_1024x722.jpeg)





The relationship between Dharma and Śiva is the hinge of the cult’s Sanskritization. In many Rāḍh villages, temples of Śiva and Dharma exist side by side, and Gājan is celebrated for both, with the difference that the horse is essential to Dharma’s Gājan but not to Śiva’s. Now, for the most famous and most overreached claim about Dharma. In the 1890s, the great scholar Haraprasad Śāstrī announced that the Dharma cult was a Buddhist survival, a relic of the lost Buddhism of Bengal, hiding in plain sight. His evidence included the tortoise-like shape of some Dharma stones, which he read as buried stūpas. It’s a romantic, electric idea and also, in Sircar’s blunt verdict, something that could hardly be substantiated.

The literary monument of the cult is the Dharmamaṅgal (ধর্মমঙ্গল), a sub-genre of the medieval Bengali *maṅgal-kāvya* devoted to glorifying Dharmaṭhākur, traditionally attributed to the poet Mayūra Bhaṭṭa and composed largely between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. Unlike the Manasāmaṅgal and the Caṇḍīmaṅgal, in which deities dominate and humans are incidental, the Dharmamaṅgal centers on human beings, the warrior Lāusen’s struggle with social conflict and political rivalry to the fore and the supernatural incidental. In this reading, the Dharmamaṅgal popularized the deity as an instrument of Brahmanism precisely where it was most needed: in Rāḍh, where, Rohini Kar argues, a peasantizing society resisted Islamization and where Dharma received Brahmanical sanction and prominence only after the advent of Islam. The Śūnya-purāṇa’s chapter “Niranjaner Ruṣmā” is repeatedly cited as registering the Dharma worshippers’ antagonism toward both Brahminism and the new Islamic power.

## The Long-Necked Horses



The material heart of the Bengali cult is the *Bāṅkuṛā* horse, produced above all in the village of Pañcmuṛā (পাঁচমুড়া) in Bāṅkuṛā district by the Kumbhakār community. The horse is offered as a votive, a symbolic sacrifice to Dharmarāj and the snake-goddess Manasā. The craft is long-established, and according to the Geographical Indications Journal No. 101 (2017), Pañcmuṛā has been known for crafting artifacts such as *bonga haathi* (elephant), *manasa chali* (symbolizing the snake-goddess Manasā), and the horse, in terracotta for over 300 years, with the tradition associated with Kumbhakār production under Malla rule centered at Bishnupur, whose terracotta temples mark the regional zenith of the clay arts. In the twentieth century, the long-necked Pañcmuṛā horse became the emblem of the All India Handicrafts Board, established in 1952 by the cultural activist Pupul Jayakar and abolished in 2020.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pbQg!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9d9d7807-e9dc-43b9-9657-b2d2f4fdee4a_815x1024.jpeg)





In the Rāḍh countryside, there is no end to the symbolic use of terracotta and wooden horses, and theassemblyof dozens of horses deposited together at a shrine is the signature of Dharmarāj worship. The horse-as-vāhana, the dry-season worship, and the solar identification together form the cluster that invites the southern comparison.

Dharma sits within a dense field of Bengali *grāmadevatās*. In the Sundarbans he is also known as Barothakur and Shanithakur, and is invoked for protection against leprosy and other afflictions; he is associated with the peacock and the *aśvattha*/peepul (*Ficus religiosa*). The same folk pantheon includes Manasā (snakes), Śītalā (smallpox), Dakṣiṇ Rāy (the tiger-lord), and Bonbibi (the forest-protectress of the Sundarbans), a roster of disease- and danger-managing local deities within which Dharma’s protective, boundary-keeping function is typical rather than unique.

## The Distant Cousin from the South



Now Ayyaṉār. If Dharma is the stranger, Ayyaṉār is the soldier.

His shrine sits in a forested area near the village boundary, usually to the west and close to a tank, thus linking it to the village's prosperity. His job, in the words of a temple priest to the ethnographer Eveline Masilamani-Meyer, is twofold: to guard the village and to guard the water. The boundary location is not incidental but constitutive; he is the *ūr-kāval*, the watchman of the settlement.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!zS7G!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Faa26c0d5-8135-4ab8-8190-855ca1b7a09c_899x1024.jpeg)





The defining image is nocturnal patrol. Ayyaṉār, on the white horse, and Karuppaṉ, on the brown horse; sometimes he too has a white one, are said to ride around at night, and some old priests claimed they could hear the sound of their horses. The terracotta horses lined up outside are not decoration. They are the fleet, the steeds given up to the god for his nighttime perambulations, exactly as in Rāḍh the horse is the instrument by which the deity is imagined to move. Each year the god needs fresh mounts. The village supplies them. The horses pile up.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FYtW!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F98cf54c0-6d95-4b62-a574-773801b81eea_676x1024.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FYtW!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F98cf54c0-6d95-4b62-a574-773801b81eea_676x1024.jpeg)





Who makes the horses, and who serves the god? The same people. In the Madurai region, most Ayyaṉār priests are *Vēḷār*, potters who also make terracotta figures of the gods in temples, as well as numerous votive offerings, including cows, horses, and other animals. The hands that mold the horse are the hands that consecrate it and the hands that speak for the god.

This is Bengal’s Kumbhakār all over again, the potter-priest, the craft and the cult fused in one non-Brahmin body. And like Dharma’s cult, this one is served from below: the *pūcāri* is non-Brahmin, and in the legends, Dalit priests stand at the center. One origin-myth ends with a Brahmin’s furious curse: “From now on, may a Harijan be your priest.”

Then there is Karuppaṉ, *karuppu*, literally “black.” Ayyaṉār is the kingly, near-vegetarian sovereign who will not dirty his hands. Karuppaṉ is the one who *does the* violence, takes the blood, the meat, the liquor, the cigar, enforces the perimeter. And in Śaiva interpretation he gets glossed upward as *Mahākāla*, the Great Black, the Great Time, a face of Bhairava. The *Kanda-purāṇam* even has Ayyaṉ dispatch a commander named Mahākāla to protect a goddess from a demon.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Qgmn!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd0a8eb72-9968-47f5-ba20-63fc64990a46_641x1024.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Qgmn!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd0a8eb72-9968-47f5-ba20-63fc64990a46_641x1024.jpeg)





So the fierce boundary-enforcer of the Tamil south gets absorbed into Śiva-as-Bhairava, precisely the same fierce-Śaiva machinery that, up north, recruits Dharma’s stone into the Gājan and reglosses it as a *liṅga*. Same gear, different gearbox.

## The Third Herd on the Hill



If two terracotta horse-armies were a coincidence, three should be impossible. So here is the third.

Travel west, far from both Bengal and Tamil Nadu, into the Aravalli foothills of northern Gujarat, to a tribal town called *Poshina* in Sabarkantha district, near the Rajasthan line. Walk to the edge of the settlement. Find the tree, a neem this time, and underneath it: horses. Terracotta, not dozens. Thousands*.* One shrine is reckoned to hold more than two thousand of them, ranked in silent rows with pointed ears and round, O-shaped mouths, an army of baked clay staring out at the hills.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FxT5!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F84f6a92f-e0a2-4390-8d19-41a4fe7993a5_1080x607.jpeg)

![Votive Clay Horses of Poshina - Photos by Ravi](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FxT5!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F84f6a92f-e0a2-4390-8d19-41a4fe7993a5_1080x607.jpeg "Votive Clay Horses of Poshina - Photos by Ravi")





Same logic, beat for beat. The horse is promised to the god in supplication and delivered when the wish comes true: a child, a cure, a sick cow recovered, rain on time, a hard harvest pulled through. The makers are the hereditary *kumbhār* potters, the same potter-as-priest-of-the-clay pattern as Bengal’s *Kumbhakār* and Tamil Nadu’s *Vēḷār*. The worshippers are ādivāsīs, Garasia, Bhil, Koli, Rathva, and the officiant is the *bhadwa*, a tribal shaman-priest, not a *purohit*. And the substrate is the same defiant aniconism that makes Dharma a faceless stone, and these communities, the reporting says, mostly hold that their gods cannot be imaged at all, and plant a post or a pot, or, when a vow is paid, a horse instead of a statue.

Who is the god? Sometimes a mountain lord, Bakhar Bhavsingh, whose mount is a horse. Sometimes a local goddess. Which is the point, not a problem, and the slot is what is stable: guardian, boundary, clay horse, vow, and the region fills it with whatever deity is to hand.

The romantic claim you will hear that these potters’ technique runs in an unbroken line from the Indus Valley Civilization is circulating-unconfirmed, the same too-good-to-check story attached to half the potteries in India. And the Garasia themselves are a layered Rajput-Bhil people whose religion already mixes Purāṇic story into older tree-and-ancestor cults, so Poshina is no pristine tribal original either. It is another formation, like the rest.

But here is what it does to the argument. Poshina sits between the worlds, west of the Deccan, nowhere near either Bengal or Tamilakam, in a tribal society with no documented contact with either, and it produces the same clay horse, under the same kind of tree, for the same kind of vow. A traveler standing in front of those rows wrote, with no scholarly axe to grind, that they reminded him instantly of Ayyaṉār’s horses a thousand miles south. He was right to feel it and right not to imagine a caravan carried it there. Three armies. One factory. If you were still tempted to hunt for the secret road connecting these cults, Poshina is where you would expect to find the wheel-ruts. There are none.

## The Lord of Terracotta



One striking similarity among all three votive-horse cults is that the horses were made by regional variants of the potter community —the Kumbhakārs in Bengal, the Kumbhārs in Gujarat, and the Vēḷārs in Tamil Nadu, who were also the officiants of those cults, serving as priests. Across a wide belt of non-Brahmanical village cults, the attested officiant is the potter, and the Brahmin is the documented latecomer, or the deliberately excluded party.

Among the Vēḷārs the role is inherited patrilineally, sometimes with as many as eight family members holding the same position. Nicholas Dirks records the ritual mechanics precisely: the head of the potters, who is also the principal priest for Ayyaṉār, takes a handful of clay (*piṭimaṇ*) from the village tank to begin fashioning the horse, and the horse's installation precedes and inaugurates all the other village festivals. At the two-day *Kutirai Eṭuppu*, the raising, or consecration, of the horse, the terracotta offerings are brought to life through rituals, processions, trances, and sometimes animal sacrifice. The same hands mold the god, consecrate him, and are entered by him. This is the genuinely remarkable datum: a craft caste holding full sacerdotal and oracular authority.

And the Tamil tradition knew it early. In the Sangam corpus there is a poem in the *Puṟanāṉūṟu*, the anthology of the heroic age, that should be read by anyone who thinks the potter was ever a minor figure in Tamil religion. In poem 256, a widow stands before the village potter and makes a request. Her husband is dead. She asks him to shape the burial urn, and to make it wide enough for two, so that she may be laid in the earth beside the man she followed through every wilderness in life, clinging to him, she says, like a small white lizard stuck to the spoke of a cart’s wheel, carried wherever it went.

And how does she address the man she is begging? **கலம் செய் கோவே** (*kalam cey kōvē*) - “O potter, the lord, who makes the vessel.” But the word is *kō*: lord, king. Not a servant, not a tradesman. A lord. The maker of the burial urn is hailed with the same honorific a poet would give a chieftain.

Across the ancient Tamil country, at Adichanallur, at Irugur on the edge of Coimbatore, wherever the spade still turns up the great *muthumakkaḷ tāḻi*, the “urns of the ancestors,” the dead were folded into vast earthen jars and buried in the ground. And the symbolism was not morbid but generative: the urn was a womb, the body within it a fetus, and death a passage back toward rebirth. The potter who threw that urn on his wheel was not decorating a funeral. He was building the vessel of a dead man’s transformation, the technician of the crossing from death into whatever came next.

Now place that beside everything else. The hero-stone tradition takes a dead warrior and, through a fixed ritual, makes him a god; the *naṭukal* is the divinization of the dead. And here, in the oldest layer of Tamil literature we possess, is the man who presides over the material side of that same passage: the potter, addressed as lord, shaping the womb-urn that carries the dead toward rebirth. The figure who makes Ayyaṉār’s clay horses today, who is also his priest and his oracle, is no late curiosity. He is the lineal heir of the *kalam cey kō*, the lord of terracotta who stood at the threshold of death and divinity two thousand years ago, long before any *purohit* arrived to gloss the rite in Sanskrit.

The potter-priest, then, is not merely a Tamil curiosity but the visible older stratum of village religion: scratch the Sanskritic surface, and the officiant beneath is the maker of the god. And the terracotta he works is the tradition’s own instrument of divinization, the burial-urn that wombs the Tamil dead toward rebirth, the horse the deified guardian rides. By the time the Brahmin arrived to read the rite in Sanskrit, the potter was already shaping the vessel of the crossing.

## The Surface Trail is Cold



So where is the link? Let us actually look.

Whenever the two gods get mentioned in the same breath, the comparison is typological, never genealogical. Scholarship pairs them as two instances of the same*type*: Ayyaṉār, the South Indian watchman, and the similar male deity Dharmaṭhākur in Bengal. The word is similar. Nobody claims contact. The framework behind that pairing is Henry Whitehead’s century-old *The Village Gods of South India* (1921), which invented the “village deity” as a category precisely so that one could independently file gods from opposite ends of the country under it.

And each god’s own scholarly trail is hermetically regional. Dharma’s historians, namely Śāstrī, Dasgupta, Sen, Chakrabarti, Korom, Sircar, trace him through the Pāla-Sena Buddhist twilight, the Dharmamaṅgal, the encounter with Islam. None reaches south. Whereas for Ayyaṉār, Clothey, Dumont, Whitehead, Masilamani-Meyer, Inglis trace him through the Tamil hero-stones, the*Cilappatikāram*, the*Brahmāṇḍa-purāṇa*. None reaches north.

And the cruelest little detail, the one that ambushes every amateur: where a Dharma *does* surface in the south, it is the intra–South Indian Dharma-Sāstā / Ayyappaṉ of Kerala, not a thread back to Bengal’s Dharmaṭhākur. And it gets worse for the transmission-hunter. Go to tribal Odisha, and you will find yet *another* Dharma, named *Dharma Pinnu*, a smallpox goddess the Kandha and Didayi peoples invoke against the disease. Three Dharmas, three unrelated gods: a faceless Bengali boundary-stone, a Keralan son-of-two-gods, an Odia pox-goddess. The shared word dharma is a false friend three times over.

Names will betray you every time. But "no link on the surface" is not the same as "no link."The surface is where the late regional Sanskrit overlays live and, of course, those do not match; each region painted its own. The question is what lies underneath all of them. And underneath, there is something the silos and the false friends cannot touch: a single, old, attested layer that every one of these cults is sitting on top of. To see it, you have to stop reading the gods sideways, Dharma next to Ayyaṉār, and start reading them downward, through the floor, to the thing they all grew out of.

## The Warrior who became the God



Underneath all these riders is one of the oldest religious ideas in India, older than the temples, older than the Sanskrit parentages, old enough to reach back to the warrior-brotherhoods of the late Vedic world: a consecrated warrior can cross over and become a god.

Start with the stone you can actually dig up. All across the south and west of India, from at least the Iron Age, people raised hero-stones, *vīrakkal* and *naṭukal* in Tamil (hero stone), *vīragallu* in Kannada, *pāliyā* in Gujarat and Rajasthan. A man dies in battle, in a cattle-raid, defending the village against raiders, or a tiger, and they plant a carved slab for him. But it is not just a gravestone. The old Tamil grammar, the *Tolkāppiyam*, lays out the ritual in six stages: choose the stone, bring it, bathe it, plant it, build a shrine for it, and, finally, divine it and worship it. The hero-stone becomes a deity once the hero's spirit is invoked to reside within it. The fallen warrior, fed and praised, becomes a god. And here is the line that should stop you cold, straight out of the Sangam scholarship: *naṭukal* worship was the beginning of village-deity worship. The village guardian does not come from nowhere. He comes from the dead hero on the stone.

Now go down one more layer. Where does that idea, the warrior who becomes a god, come from? The trail runs back to the *Vrātyas*: the consecrated, roaming warrior-brotherhoods of the late Vedic period, worshippers of the wild outsider-god Rudra — the rough draft of Śiva-Bhairava, whose whole ritual world turned on one goal that scholars put in almost exactly these words: the divinization of man. The Vrātya is the template, the warrior, consecrated, becoming divine. The hero-stone is that template applied to the dead warrior. And the village guardian is the hero-stone grown into a standing cult.

That is the root, in three layers:

**Vrātya warrior-god → deified-dead hero-stone → village guardian**

And now watch the riders snap into place along it. The fierce guardian keeps getting absorbed, late, into exactly the Rudra-Bhairava register; Khaṇḍobā of Maharashtra literally is Sun-Bhairava; Karuppaṉ gets glossed as Mahākāla, a face of Bhairava; Dharma’s stone gets called a *liṅga* and his festival folded into Śiva’s. That is not a coincidence of late paint jobs. If the root was Rudraic to begin with, then every cult becoming Śaiva at the end is not converting; it is going home.

Does the old coincidence reading still have a job? A small one, at the edges. The raw materials really were lying around everywhere: the clay-votive habit, the boundary-guardian logic, the horse-as-power, so the Indian village really can spin up a fresh guardian on its own when it needs one. You can even watch it happen: in Naopukuria, Murshidabad, the Ḍoms worship Mā Ḍomnī, a goddess born from the tears of a discarded Ḍom bride, served by their own community, no Brahmin required, nobody carried her there. So yes, the village is also a factory that can build guardians from scratch. But the specific rider we keep meeting- deified warrior, on a mound, fed at the boundary, riding a horse- that one is not generic. That one has a root you can dig up. And the horse is the part of him that traveled.

The clay horse at the village edge, then, is not a Bengali idea that walked south, or a Tamil idea that walked north. It is the mount of the divinized warrior, the hero’s horse inherited wherever his cult took root, made by the potter who was so often also his priest. Not a coincidence repeated a dozen times. One root, and its horse.

## A Schema that became the Fabric



We have the root - the deified warrior, the hero-stone, the Vrātya idea that a consecrated man becomes a god. Now watch it surface, station by station, the whole width of the country because the figure that keeps walking out of the ground is unmistakably one figure: a male guardian, planted at the edge of the settled world, made of earth or stone rather than carved into a face, fed at the boundary, and very often a dead human being promoted to a god.

Go to the villages around Banaras, the Bhojpuri heartland of eastern Uttar Pradesh and western Bihar, and the guardian has a name so plain it is almost an address. He is the Dīh (डीह), used interchangeably with Dīhā and Dīhwār, the protector of the whole village unit. Diane Coccari, who surveyed these shrines in the 1980s, records that the Dīh is the *gāṅv kā rakṣak*, the village protector; the head (*mālik*,*mukhiyā*); the village police chief (*kotwāl*); or the *kṣetrapāla*, the guardian of the field and region. Nothing supernatural crosses the village boundary without his permission: “A ghost must also ask the Dih’s permission to enter the village,” one of her informants told her. His shrine is an aniconic mound under a holy tree at the village edge; he receives the first wheat of the harvest; and in 2020, when COVID came, the women of dozens of Banaras villages walked the boundaries pouring milk and offering *neem* leaves to Dīh Bābā, to turn the disease away at the perimeter. That is the Ayyaṉār function exactly the *ūr-kāval*, the watchman who guards the *ūr* against disease and the unquiet dead wearing an eastern name.

And in the city of Banaras the Dīh almost always turns out to be a Bīr (वीर, hero), a Bīr Bābā, one of hundreds of small shrines to the deified dead: martyred warriors, murdered champions, men who died protecting the powerless, now mounded up at the edge and worshipped to keep the boundary safe. The guardian, in other words, is very often a person, a hero killed and then enlisted, permanently, into perimeter duty.

In Mithila, north Bihar, spilling into the Nepal *tarai*, the Dusadhs, a Dalit community, worship Rājā Salhesh (also Sahlesh, Shailesh), a folk-king remembered in Dusadh tradition as a protector of the powerless who defied the feudal lords. He is the deified human hero in his purest form, in which a low-caste champion is raised to godhood and worshipped with his companions, his commander, Chauharmal, and his beloved, Dauna Malin, in temples across the Mithila region. And the diagnostic details are all present: in Darbhanga, the potters make equestrian figures of Salhesh and his companions, which the Dusadhs install and worship. A deified hero, a horse, a potter-made votive, a cult served from below, the same object as Pābūjī in the desert and Ayyaṉār in the paddy, now standing on the very corridor that runs from the Bhojpuri Dīh country toward Bengal. Salhesh is the eastern rider the thesis predicts, and he is riding in exactly the right place.

Now go the other way, west, to Rajasthan, and the same figure rides out again, this time openly on a horse. The *bhomiyā* (guardian of village boundaries), the *jhūṃjhār* (the deified dead who fell in battle), the *kṣetrapāla* again by name and above all the great horse-riding hero-gods Pābūjī and Tejājī, Rajput and pastoral champions who died protecting cattle and were raised to godhood, worshipped by Rabari, Raika, and Bhil herders, sung through the night by Bhopa priest-singers before a painted *phaḍ* scroll. Pābūjī, on his black mare Kesar Kālmī, dying for a promise and a herd of cows, is the same essential object as Ayyaṉār on his white horse and Dharma on his clay one: the mounted guardian of the margin, fed and remembered so that he will keep the watch.

And right next to those Rajasthan heroes sit the Poshina horse-shrines we already discussed, Garasia and Bhil *ādivāsīs* in the Aravalli, piling clay horses under a neem for a mountain-god and a goddess. The Rajasthan hero-cults and the Poshina horse-cults are not two things; they are one cultural zone, the western desert-and-hill frontier doing the boundary-guardian in both a deified Rajput hero register and a tribal clay horse register at once.

## The Corridor



This is not two riders from Bengal and Tamil Nadu anymore. This is a belt of guardians running the whole width of the subcontinent, a common schema that spread across the country and was expressed differently by the people of each land. Every one of them is a boundary-keeper; most are aniconic or mounded; several are explicitly the deified dead; several ride horses; and several take the clay horse as the standard votive. And it does not sit in a random scatter. It runs along the two great corridors of movement in Indian history: the western corridor, down through Gujarat and the Aravallis into the Deccan and the south, and the Gangetic corridor, eastward through Uttar Pradesh and Bihar into Bengal.

That is the shape the root predicts. The warrior-god substratum, Vrātya → hero-stone → village guardian, spreads with the mobile, pastoral-martial societies that carried the megalithic culture across the peninsula, surfacing as Khaṇḍobā in the Deccan, Mallanna in Andhra, as the deified hero-gods of Rajasthan, as Ayyaṉār in the south. The horse rides with it the whole way.

## One Root, Many Crowns



Whichever way the truth finally falls, the thing that should stay with you is the figure himself, because he is one of the most persistent characters in the entire religious imagination of India, and he has been hiding in plain sight at the edge of every village you have ever driven past.

He stands at the boundary. He is made of earth, or mounded stone, or fired clay, rarely a carved face, because his power is older than iconography. He guards the line between the safe inside and the dangerous outside: disease, theft, beasts, ghosts, the unquiet dead. He is fed with blood, or milk, or meat, or cigars, or neem leaves, or a clay horse, by people who are usually not Brahmins and who serve him themselves. He is very often a hero, a martyr, an ancestor, drafted into permanent sentry duty. And again and again he rides, because a guardian who patrols needs a mount, and so the horse becomes his emblem and his gift. Call him Pābūjī in the Marwar desert, Khaṇḍobā at Jejuri, Ayyaṉār in the Tamil paddy, Dīhwār in the Bhojpuri mofussil, Bīr Bābā in a Banaras lane, Dharmaṭhākur under a Rāḍh peepul; the costume changes, the manifestation changes, but the body underneath is recognizably one body.

Whether that body was born many times from the same soil, or born once and carried, is the question, at last; we can now see far enough down to answer it for most of the figure. He has a root you can dig up: the hero-stone, the planted memorial of a warrior who becomes a god, and behind it the Vrātya idea that a consecrated man crosses into divinity. That root visibly fed the guardians of the Deccan, Rajasthan, Gujarat, and the Tamil south. The horse is the mount it took with it.

One figure, with a real root, attested at its base and across its western crown, and all the way to its eastern crown in Dharmaṭhākur.

The clay horse at the Bengali shrine, the clay horse at the Poshina neem, and the great terracotta steeds of the Tamil south are not a dozen coincidences. They are the hoofprints of one old thing, walking the length of the subcontinent, and we can now see where most of the trail leads, and exactly which last stretch of it still has to be walked.
