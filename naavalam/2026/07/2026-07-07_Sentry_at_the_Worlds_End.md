# Sentry at the World's End

### Upulvan, the guardian god of Sri Lanka who lost his name to Viṣṇu

*In the late 1580s, a Portuguese raiding party under Thomé de Sousa d’Arronches razed one of the greatest shrines in Sri Lanka, the Pagode of Tanauarê, as they knew it, a temple their own chroniclers would remember as a shrine of Viṣṇu. Which it was. Or was it?*

Stand at Dondra Head, the southernmost point of Sri Lanka. The land simply runs out here. Beyond the lighthouse, there is nothing but open ocean until Antarctica: no island, no shoal, no landfall. If a god were going to stand sentry over an island, this is where you would post him.

For close to a millennium, one did.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!O-y4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd1460088-973f-4ac1-9b43-43d77faa4ae4_1024x559.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!O-y4!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd1460088-973f-4ac1-9b43-43d77faa4ae4_1024x559.jpeg)





The town is still called Devinuvara (Sanskrit *Deva-nagara*; Tamil *Teṉavarai தெனவரை*; the Portuguese mangled it into Dondra). The god who stood sentry was *Upulvan* (Sinhala උපුල්වන්; Pāli *Uppalavaṇṇa*; Sanskrit *Utpalavarṇa*), the one coloured like the blue water-lily. Outside the island, almost nobody has heard of him. On the island, almost nobody uses his name anymore.

Five centuries ago, the god who stood there as the guardian of Lanka was absorbed, completely and almost without residue, into Viṣṇu. Today he is Viṣṇu deviyō, the “Buddhist Viṣṇu,” enshrined beside the Buddha in temples across Sri Lanka. Historians of South Asian religion know this plot by heart: a local god of the soil, too powerful to ignore, is drawn upward into the Sanskritic pantheon, rebaptized as a form of one of the great gods, and slowly forgets, or is made to forget, his own name. It has happened hundreds of times across the subcontinent. What makes the Sri Lankan case extraordinary is the paper trail. Almost nowhere else can you watch the transformation happen in dated documents, the two gods held apart in one century’s inscriptions and poems, merged in the next, the native name surviving on a copper plate even as its own modern editor has to write Viṣṇu over it to make the line legible.

But before the name was lost, he was given a job. Let us start there, because the job he was assigned to do is the whole point.

## A guardian by appointment



The earliest secure record of Uppalavaṇṇa is from *Dīpavaṃsa*, the Pāli chronicle of the fourth century CE. The scene is the Buddha’s deathbed. With his last breath, as the chronicles tell it, the Buddha entrusts the island of Laṅkā to Sakka, king of the gods. And Sakka does not keep the job. He delegates it downward:

> *sambuddhassa vaco sutvā devarājā Sujampati / Uppalavaṇṇassa ācikkhi
> dīpaṃ ārakkhakāraṇaṃ.*
>
> *“Sujampati, the king of gods, having heard the Sambuddha’s command,
> committed to Uppalavaṇṇa the business of guarding the island.”
> (Dīpavaṃsa IX.24)*

Look at the machinery of that sentence. There is a chain of command from Buddha → Sakka → Uppalavaṇṇa. There is a rank: Uppalavaṇṇa is a devaputta, a god of the retinue class, not a sovereign. And there is an official name, a technical term that will follow this god for a thousand years: *ārakkha*, the guardian. He is not the lord of Laṅkā, but the designated watchman.

Sanskrit has a precise word for this, *kṣetrapāla* (Pāli *khettapāla*), the guardian of the field, the keeper of a bounded territory. It is one of the oldest and most widespread religious ideas in South Asia, the protective power posted at the edge of the settled world, holding its authority on commission from something higher. Here, that idea appears in full Buddhist dress: authority delegated downward from the apex of the cosmology to a guardian stationed at the boundary. It will turn out to be the single most durable thing about this god, outlasting his shrine, his festival, and eventually his name.

## The God who treats Prince Vijaya on his arrival



A century or two later, the fuller *Mahāvaṃsa,* c. 5-6 century CE, retells the delegation and then adds a scene the Dīpavaṃsa does not have, and it is one of the most quietly astonishing scenes in South Asian religious literature.

Prince Vijaya and his seven hundred followers, the legendary settlers from the Indian subcontinent, make landfall on Laṅkā. At the foot of a tree, they find a figure seated in ascetic guise. They ask him what island this is. He tells them, reassures them, *“there are no men here, and no danger will befall you,”* and then he does two things:

> *iti vatvā kuṇḍikāya, te jalena nisiñciya. / Suttañ ca tesaṃ hatthesu,
> lagetvā nabhasāgamā...*
>
> *“So saying, he sprinkled them with water from his water-vessel, and
> having fastened a thread upon their hands, he vanished into the air.”
> (Mahāvaṃsa VII.8–9)*

The ascetic is Uppalavaṇṇa, already at his guardian's work. And stop on what he actually does. The first recorded action of the national god of Sri Lanka is an apotropaic boundary rite performed for arrivals at the shoreline: water is sprinkled from the*kuṇḍikā*, and a protective thread isbound at the wrist. Wilhelm Geiger, the chronicle’s great translator, glossed the thread in his own footnote as a *paritta*, a protective charm against demons.

Anyone who has attended a temple festival in Tamil Nadu will feel a jolt of recognition: the *kāppu (காப்பு)*, the protective wrist-thread whose tying opens the festival and marks its participants as under divine guard, and behind it, the whole pan-Indian *rakṣā*-cord complex. The guardian’s work begins at the edge, on bodies crossing into his territory. Vijaya’s men are being processed at the Laṅkā border checkpoint, and the customs officer is none other than god Uppalavaṇṇa himself.

And notice the direction of growth, because it matters for everything that follows. The guardianship is older than the rite. The fourth-century Dīpavaṃsa gives us the guardian-with-no-shoreline scene; the fifth- to sixth-century Mahāvaṃsa builds the water-and-thread ceremony onto that already-existing office. The deity is a guardian first and acquires his elaborated edge-ritual second, exactly the direction you expect if a ritual apparatus of the boundary is accreting around a pre-existing territorial power.

One later text sharpens the picture further. The *Vaṃsatthappakāsinī*, the great commentary on the Mahāvaṃsa (c. 8th- 9th centuries CE), carries a striking variant where Prince Vijaya begs Uppalavaṇṇa’s aid to destroy the *yakkhas* of Laṅkā, and the god is propitiated with ritual and moving weapons. That is a martial guardian, a warrior-protector invoked for war on the *yakkhas* of the land, a fiercer figure than the serene ascetic with the water-pot. The commentary cannot push the god’s date back, but it shows that within the living tradition he carried a weapon-propitiated, fighting register that the gentle Buddhist frame otherwise mutes.

## The God who floated in from the ocean



Gods of this kind usually come with an origin story for their cult image, and Uppalavaṇṇa’s is a small masterpiece.

As the Kōṭṭe-era chronicles and poems tell it, a log of kihiri wood, Sinhala*kihiri*, Sanskrit*khadira*,*Acacia catechu*, the red-hearted ritual hardwood, came floating over the open ocean and beached at Devinuvara. The king, forewarned in a dream, recovered the drift-log, and the god’s image was carved from it and enshrined. Shavings of the log, the tradition adds, cured disease: the cult image doubling as a pharmacy.

It would be easy to file this as late poetic garnish. It is not, and a datable inscription is what saves it. The monastery at Devinuvara was already named *Kihireli Piriveṇa,*the*Khadira*monastery, in a ninth-century inscription from the shrine grounds. And by 1344, on a royal copper plate a hundred miles inland, the wood has fused onto the god’s own name: he is inscribed as *Kihiräḷi-Upulvan*, “Upulvan of the kihiri-wood.” The drift-log is not an embellishment; it is anchored in the god’s very theonym in a dated royal grant. His material is his identity.

And think about what that material says. The guardian of the island’s last shore is made of wood that crossed the sea to reach him; his substance is itself a boundary-crossing, matching his office as the processor of boundary-crossers. Gods do not often carry their job descriptions in their flesh. This one does.

The shrine that housed him became one of the great religious establishments of the island, traditionally founded by a prince of Rohaṇa in the seventh or eighth century, and rebuilt by Parākramabāhu II in the thirteenth; its Äsaḷa festival was famous long before Kandy’s.

By the fourteenth century, Uppalavaṇṇa stands at the head of the classic Sinhala constellation: the*satara varan deviyō*, the gods of the four warrants, namely Upulvan, Saman, Vibhīṣaṇa, and Kataragama. The Sinhala *varan* means exactly what it sounds like: a warrant, a sanction of divine authority delegated from the Buddha. The four national protectors of Buddhist Laṅkā hold their godhood on commission.

The earliest datable attestation is a rock inscription and a copper plate of 1344 from the Laṅkātilaka temple near Gampola, which list the god-shrines built into the temple beside the Buddha’s image and name their occupants; first among them is Kihiräḷi-Upulvan, who has assumed the protection of Laṃkā. It is the Mahāvaṃsa’s *Laṃkārakkhaṃ*, the guardianship-of-the-island formula, echoed almost verbatim a thousand years later. The office survived, in its own technical vocabulary, from a fourth-century Pāli chronicle to a fourteenth-century royal grant. In the same century, a Kōṭṭe strongman built shrines to the four warrant-gods directly into the city’s fortifications, guardian gods literally embedded in the rampart.

The set itself is a study in absorption. Kataragama is the Tamil war god Murukaṉ, worshipped to this day with hook-swinging and fire-walking. Vibhīṣaṇa is a *demon-king from the Rāmāyaṇa,* serving as a Buddhist national protector. A pantheon of borrowed and promoted gods, each holding a Buddhist warrant, and at its head, the drift-log guardian of the southern shore.

Then, in 1409, the outside world knocked. The Galle Trilingual Slab, carried across the Indian Ocean by the treasure fleets of Zheng He, records in Chinese, Persian, and Tamil the offerings of the Yongle Emperor of Ming China, and while the Chinese face honors the Buddha and the Persian face an Islamic saint, the Tamil face pours out gold, silver, silk, sandalwood, and anointing oil to the god of Devinuvara. A Ming emperor, hedging his cosmological bets across three religions, made offerings to our boundary-guardian.

Two details on that slab earn their keep. First, the Tamil text calls the god by two competing honorifics in the same breath:*Teṉavarai-nāyaṉār (தெனவரை நாயனார்)*and*Teṉavarai-āḻvār**(தெனவரை ஆழ்வார்)*, and as Paranavitana himself noted,*nāyaṉār*is the title ofŚaivasaints while*āḷvār*belongs to theVaiṣṇava. In 1409, the god is caught mid-absorption, wearing both Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava titles simultaneously, a century before the Viṣṇu identification hardened. Second, and file this away for the discussion below: the emperor’s own words, as the Tamil face gives them, praise the Lord of Teṉavarai because men, whencesoever they come thither, have their obstacles removed through his divine grace. That is the guardian-of-arrivals function, the Mahāvaṃsa’s shoreline office, restated in Tamil a millennium later, in an inscription paid for by an emperor whose sailors had just crossed an ocean to get there.

## Watching a god lose his name



Now, the heart of the story, call this an erasure or transformation, is better documented here than almost anywhere in South Asia.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ylEO!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F90b8a96f-b6a8-43db-9244-3428e68c3a08_802x1024.webp)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ylEO!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F90b8a96f-b6a8-43db-9244-3428e68c3a08_802x1024.webp)





Through the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the Sinhala *sandēśa* poems treat Upulvan and Viṣṇu as two distinct gods. The *Paravi Sandēśaya* of the great scholar-monk Toṭagamuvē Śrī Rāhula even gives Upulvan a family of his own: a consort, San̆davan Bisō, and a son, a bow-god named Dunu (Sanskrit *dhanus*, “bow”).

Then, at the end of the fifteenth century, under the heavily Brahmanized court of Kōṭṭe, Upulvan is formally identified with Viṣṇu plausibly via the shared dark-blue body. The identification wins totally. Upulvan-as-Viṣṇu images enter the Buddhist image-houses; the deity becomes essential to royal consecration; and the name Upulvan simply falls out of use. When Thomé de Sousa d’Arronches’s Portuguese force sacked and razed Devinuvara in the late 1580s, they knew it only as the Pagoda of Tenavaram, a place-name; the Portuguese historian Queyroz, writing a century later, would record that its great deity had been Viṣṇu and by then that was true, the older name having dropped away entirely.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TICw!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F14e735e8-b628-4fc6-a579-2a1ca5baf943_1024x576.jpeg)





Three great Kandyan-region Viṣṇu temples standing today, Laṅkātilaka among them, began as Upulvan foundations and were converted only in the seventeenth century. And when the Devinuvara shrine itself rose from its own rubble, rebuilt by Rājasiṃha II of Kandy (r. 1635–1687), a couple of generations after the Portuguese burned it, it rose as a Viṣṇu devālaya. The old god’s name did not return even to his own foundation; the shrine that stands there today, painted blue like Viṣṇu, is dedicated in the name that replaced him.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vsAK!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0c36eea5-7b6a-42a0-b695-2dd74cf5cc2d_623x1024.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vsAK!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0c36eea5-7b6a-42a0-b695-2dd74cf5cc2d_623x1024.jpeg)





And here is the jewel of the transformation, a single document that catches the merger *i*n the act. The *Palkumbura Sannasa*, a copper-plate land grant of King Bhuvanaikabāhu VII (r. 1521–1551), records a dedication to the village god, who is named on the plate itself as *Upulvan devi-rajjuru-sāmi*, “Lord Upulvan, the divine king.” But watch what the plate’s modern editor, Paranavitana, has to do to publish it: in his summary he calls it a payment to *Viṣṇu*, and in his translation he renders the figure “the lotus-coloured divine king Viṣṇu“ and Viṣṇu is his own insertion, because by the time the plate was edited the equation was so automatic that the god’s actual inscribed name had to be *glossed* to be legible. The plate says Upulvan. The apparatus says Viṣṇu. A god losing his name, in a single line of copper.

Even the plate’s closing benediction is in on it. The grant ends with a Sanskrit verse, *dānāt svargam avāpnoti pālanād acyutaṃ padam*, "by giving one attains heaven; by protecting, the imperishable state,” exalting *pālana*, protection, at the foot of a grant to the protector god. And *acyuta*, “imperishable,” is a stock epithet of Viṣṇu. In one word, the verse puns the old guardian’s function onto the incoming Viṣṇu identity. The merger performs itself in the grammar.

Set the three inscriptions in a row; *Kihiräḷi-Upulvan* at Laṅkātilaka in 1344, *Teṉavarai-nāyaṉār/-āḻvār* at Galle in 1409, *Upulvan devi-rajjuru-sāmi* at Palkumbura in the 1520s–40s and you can watch, in dated stone and copper, a process that elsewhere in South Asia must usually be inferred: two gods held apart in the documents of one era, and the smaller one’s name overwritten by the larger one’s within a few generations. At Laṅkātilaka, the substitution is complete on the ground: the god who entered the 1344 shrine-list as Kihiräḷi-Upulvan is, in the living temple, simply Viṣṇu, while a separate Viṣṇu named elsewhere in that same 1344 inscription has vanished. They stood side by side in one document. Then one of them ate the other.

## Who was he before, and what was his origin?



So who was the god before the Buddhists gave him his warrant?

The only full-length study of the shrine remains Paranavitana’s 1953 monograph, and his answer was an audacious claim that Upulvan is a survival of the Vedic *Varuṇa*, the lord of waters. But his argument, read directly, is stranger and more interesting than its usual summary; his hypothesis is not primarily about the sea. But it is about the bow. Upulvan’s son is a bow-god; the *Paravi Sandēśaya*, on Paranavitana’s reading, links the god to Rāma “of the Bow”; in the oldest stratum of the *Rāmāyaṇa* svayaṃvara the divine bow is given by Varuṇa; the *Mahābhārata* hands Varuṇa the bow Gāṇḍīva and identifies the divine bows with the rainbow, which in Sinhala is *dev-dunna*, “the god’s bow.” Paranavitana’s Upulvan is a rain-making rainbow-deity of Varuṇa’s lineage, worshipped through his weapon.

He even rebuilt the god’s name to fit. Finding that the transparent reading, *Utpala-varṇa*, blue-lily-colored, gave no satisfactory sense for a Varuṇa-descended god, he derived Upulvan instead from *Uda-pāla*: *uda*, water, plus *pāla*, guardian. According to Paranavitana’s etymology, the god’s name means guardian of the waters.

Later scholarship has been largely unpersuaded, and one can see why: the etymology is a conjectural emendation adopted precisely because the plain reading didn’t fit the thesis; the bow-chain runs through epic passages Paranavitana himself dates late; and above all there is the carrier problem, Vedic Varuṇa had no living cult anywhere in South Asia by the mid-first millennium CE, so a seventh-century southern-Lankan port god cannot plausibly descend from him. John Holt, in the standard modern treatment, sets the Varuṇa thesis aside and reads Upulvan as a deity of probably local, composite origin progressively drawn into the Viṣṇu orbit; much Sri Lankan scholarship simply takes the chronicles at face value as evidence of an indigenous protective power promoted into the Buddhist cosmological civil service.

The question is genuinely contested, and this article will not pretend otherwise. But notice what all three positions concede: a pre-existing guardian power, absorbed upward, twice. First Buddhicized, then Vaiṣṇavized. On the mechanism, everyone already agrees. The fight is only over where the guardian came from.

And there is a fourth possibility that, so far as I can find, no scholar has stated in this form, though Paranavitana, whose own index circles restlessly around *Varuṇa*, *Dravidian*, and *Ocean*, came within sight of it.

## Discussion: The Sea God Across the Strait



The fatal objection to Paranavitana’s thesis, remember, is the carrier problem: no living Varuṇa cult existed to transmit the god. But that is only true if you insist on the Vedic Varuṇa. Because there was one place in the first-millennium world where Varuṇaṉ was the living, worshipped name of a god, and it was two dozen miles across the water from Sri Lanka.

The oldest surviving Tamil grammar, the Tolkāppiyam, organizes the entire world of classical Tamil poetry into five landscape types, the*tiṇai*, each with its own terrain, people, emotional register, and presiding god. To the *neytal* tiṇai, the seashore: the world of the Paratavar fisher-folk, the pearl-divers, the salt-traders, the port-towns, the grammar assigns a sea god it names, in so many words, Varuṇaṉ

> *varuṇaṉ mēya perumaṇal ulakam*
>
> *“the world of sand dunes where Varuṇaṉ dwells.”*

The later Tamil tradition calls this figure *Kaṭalōṉ*, he of the sea. And the Dravidianist consensus on this Varuṇaṉ is that the Sanskrit name is an overlay, a Vedic label laid over an indigenous littoral deity of the fishing coasts. In other words, the same upward-absorption machine that would later swallow Upulvan into Viṣṇu had already run, centuries earlier, within the Tolkāppiyam itself: the Tamil sea god was already wearing a borrowed Sanskritic name long before Upulvan would.

Now reroute Paranavitana’s evidence through that Varuṇa, and watch the pieces reorganize themselves.

Devinuvara is a neytal site to the letter: a sea-facing port shrine in a fisher-trader milieu, at the literal end of the land, its cult image arriving as driftwood from the ocean. Its Tamil-facing texture is not conjecture but epigraphy: the Galle slab addresses the god in Tamil as Lord of Teṉavarai; a copper plate of Vijayabāhu VI names a Brahmin of the shrine *Teṉuvarapperumāḷ*; Paranavitana’s own monograph discusses the Dravidian architecture of the old Galge shrine. The Karaiyar, the Tamil maritime caste along the Sri Lankan coast, carry the Varuṇaṉ traditions in their lineage name,*Varuṇakulam,* to this day. On this reading, Paranavitana assembled genuine evidence and attached it to the wrong Varuṇa, not the Vedic sovereign surviving impossibly across two thousand years of cultic silence, but the Dravidian sea-god of the facing shore, who had been doing brisk business under Varuṇa’s name the whole time.

Then there is the flower, a lexical detail that deserves to be a finding in its own right. The eponymous bloom of the neytal landscape, the flower the whole seashore-world is named for, is the blue water-lily, *Nymphaea nouchali*. The Pāli and Sanskrit *uppala/utpala* is the same flower. Read the god’s name at face value: Uppalavaṇṇa, coloured like the blue water-lily, and, from the Tamil side of the strait, it says:colored like the neytal flower. The guardian of the island, stationed at its ultimate littoral point, would bear as his name the botanical emblem of the seashore landscape type itself.

Here, honesty compels a complication that actually deepens the picture. Paranavitana, as we saw, rejected the flower-reading in favor of *Uda-pāla*, water-guardian. So there are two competing etymologies on the table, and look where they point. If Paranavitana is right, the name directly encodes the job as *guardian of the waters*. If the plain reading is correct and it is how the ancient bhikkhunī's identical name is universally understood, the name encodes the emblem of the Tamil seashore. Flower of the shore, or guardian of the waters, the name points to the sea’s edge either way. Both roads lead down to the littoral.

There is a way to sharpen this by cross-examining the *other* great body of Tamil devotional verse, the *Nālāyira Divya Prabandham*, the four thousand hymns of the Ālvār poet-saints, the foundational scripture of Tamil Viṣṇu-worship. If Upulvan’s blue-water-lily name really pulls toward the Tamil littoral, that corpus is where the pull should show. And it does, but with a precision that matters. In the Ālvār hymns the sea-word that attaches to Viṣṇu is *kaṭal vaṇṇaṉ*, “coloured like the ocean,” the dark-hued Lord, a *color* epithet, glossed by the classical commentators as both his ocean-dark complexion and his ocean-vast, unfathomable nature. The word *kaṭalōṉ*, “he of the sea,” does not name Viṣṇu; it stays fixed on Varuṇaṉ, the sea-king of the neytal shore. Two different grammatical templates, and they do not cross: *-vaṇṇaṉ*, coloured-like, is the god; *-ōṉ*, he-of,” is the sea. Now set Upulvan’s own name beside them. Pāli *Uppala-vaṇṇa*, Sanskrit *Utpala-varṇa*, is a *-vaṇṇa*/*-varṇa* compound — “coloured-like-the-lily,” a morphological twin of Viṣṇu’s *kaṭal vaṇṇaṉ*, and precisely *not* the “he-of-the-sea” dweller-form that Varuṇaṉ and Kaṭalōṉ wear. The name-shape sorts Upulvan into the color family, the family of the neytal flower and of the ocean-hued Viṣṇu, and away from the sea-god-by-name family. This cuts two ways at once, honestly: it supports the flower reading, a color-name reaching for the shore’s own bloom, and it feeds the Māyōṉ objection below, a color-name reaching for the dark Vaiṣṇava body.

And there is one Ālvār verse that shows why the distinction, far from dissolving the sea-connection, is the very hinge on which the later Viṣṇu-merger could turn. In the opening hymn of Nammāḻvār’s *Tiruvāciriyam*, the poet paints the supreme Viṣṇu reclining in cosmic sleep and describes him as an emerald mountain that seems to rest *kaṭalōṉ kai micai*, “upon the hand, the waves of Kaṭalōṉ,” the ocean-king. The classical commentary is explicit: the sleeper is the Lord Viṣṇu; *Kaṭalōṉ* is Varuṇaṉ, and he is the couch. In the foundational tableau of Tamil Vaiṣṇavism, then, the sea-lord lies directly beneath Viṣṇu, bearing him on the water. A littoral sea-guardian need not be dragged upward into Viṣṇu; the cosmology already seats the ocean as Viṣṇu’s resting-place, so he of the sea and he who sleeps upon the sea stand one short step apart, and the step runs downhill. It supplies the missing *why, Viṣṇu, and not some other god,* for the Devundara absorption: a shore-god whose whole being is the sea has, in the Tamil imagination, a door that opens onto exactly one member of the great pantheon.

Now the counterweights, stated plainly, because a hypothesis is only as good as the objections it survives. First,color cuts two ways: within the tiṇai theology itself, the dark-blue-bodied god is Māyōṉ of the pastoral*mullai*landscape, the figure who becomes Viṣṇu, so the blue body could be read instead as a Māyōṉ-side affinity, in which case the fifteenth-century Viṣṇu merger is simply the color-logic completing itself. Second, *attestation order*: the chronicles give Uppalavaṇṇa no marine function at all; he is an island-guardian in a Pāli text, and the securely littoral cult only appears from the seventh century onward, so the seashore features could be later accretions at a shore-side shrine rather than origins. The attested Tamil titles call him *Teṉavarai-nāyaṉār* and *Teṉavarai-āḷvār*, place-based honorifics rather than sea-theonyms. The Tamil evidence withholds the name.

But notice what it supplies instead. The Galle inscription’s own words frame the god as the remover of obstacles for those who cross the sea to reach him, a protector of crossings rather than a lord of the deep. That is a distinction worth building on: the whole dossier, from the Mahāvaṃsa’s water-and-thread checkpoint to the Ming emperor’s grateful crossing, presents Upulvan not as a sea god but as a sea-guardian, the *ārakkha* of the shoreline, the neytal frontier post rather than the ocean throne. The Tamil sources deny him the sea god’s name and hand him the sea-guardian’s job.

Where does that leave the hypothesis? Calibrated, not proclaimed. That the Devundara god, whatever the chronicle-deity’s ultimate origin, functioned within and absorbed a Tamil-littoral Varuṇaṉ-type sea-god complex or the Vedic version, as Paranavitana suggested. The strict identity “Upulvan = Kaṭalōṉ” is not currently assessable as a genetic claim; it is held here as a hypothesis.

## The guardian outlasts the god



Step back, at the end, and look at what actually survived at Dondra Head because it is not what usually survives.

The image was burned in 1588. The festival’s primacy passed to Kandy. The divine family, the consort, the bow-god son vanished with the Viṣṇu merger. The name itself dropped out of living speech, surviving only on copper plates that modern editors must annotate to make legible. By any ordinary accounting, the god is gone.

But run the other ledger. The office, *ārakkha*, the guardianship of the island, survives verbally intact from a fourth-century chronicle to a fourteenth-century royal grant to the living cult of the Buddhist Viṣṇu today. The boundary survives: the shrine still stands at the land’s last point, and the god of Devinuvara is still the protector to whom sea-crossers give thanks. The warrant survives: the *satara varan* gods still hold Laṅkā on the Buddha’s commission. The edge-rite survives, down to the protective thread on the wrist, an image any visitor to a South Indian festival will recognize at once. Even the martial coloring survives, tucked into an old commentary. What died was only the name.

That is the real lesson of Upulvan, and it extends well beyond a single Sri Lankan shrine. We tend to treat a god’s name as the god, as though when the name goes, the deity goes with it. The Devundara file, with its unmatched run of dated documents, shows the opposite: names are the *most* perishable part of a god. What persists is the structure underneath the bounded territory, the delegated authority, the watchman at the line, the rite performed on those who cross it. Buddhism inherited that structure and gave it a warrant; Viṣṇu inherited it and gave it a new face; and if the argument of this essay is right, both were late tenants in a house built by the sea god of the Tamil shore, who had himself already traded his own name for Varuṇa’s centuries before.

The Portuguese thought they destroyed a temple of Viṣṇu. The Sinhala court believed it was worshipping Viṣṇu. Paranavitana thought he had found Varuṇa. Perhaps all of them were looking at the same thing and reading only the newest label on it: the guardian at the water’s edge, older than every name he has worn, still standing where the land runs out.
