Every *Ciṅṅam (ചിങ്ങം)*, the first month of the Malayalam calendar, Kerala tells itself this story. Māvēli ruled; the world was one; a dwarf came asking for three steps of ground, and the good king went down so the god could ascend, with one promise kept ever since.

***Once a year, the demon king returns.***

It is a beautiful story. It is also, on the evidence, a young one, but the festival is ancient.

This is the evolution of a festival, told in layers: what the sources actually say, what scholars reasonably infer, and what tradition has lovingly added.

So, where does this story begin?

## The oldest Ōṇam has no Mahābali.



Let us begin where the evidence begins: not in present-day Kerala, but in Madurai, the ancient capital of the Pāṇḍyas.

The Maturaikkāñci (மதுரைக்காஞ்சி) of Māṅkuṭi Marutaṉār (Māṅkuṭi Kiḻār), one of the ten Sangam idylls of the Pattuppāṭṭu (பத்துப்பாட்டு), describes Madurai in festival mode on the day of Ōṇam, the birthday of Māyōṉ, the dark god of the mullai landscape whom the tradition identifies with Viṣṇu and Kṛṣṇa. Here is the passage itself, lines 590 to 599:

> மாறாது உற்ற வடுப் படு நெற்றி,   
> சுரும்பு ஆர் கண்ணிப் பெரும் புகல் மறவர்,   
> கடுங் களிறு ஓட்டலின் காணுநர் இட்ட   
> நெடுங் கரைக் காழகம் நிலம் பரல் உறுப்ப,   
> கடுங் கள் தேறல் மகிழ் சிறந்து திரிதர

In plain rendering: on the good day of Ōṇam, dear to Māyōṉ (மாயோன்) of the golden garland, he who overcame the massed avuṇar (asuras), the fight-loving maṟavar, faces deep-scarred by the goad, great whetstone-hardened hands built to bear the shock of arms, bee-swarmed garlands in their hair, battle quarter against quarter without yielding, foreheads gashed; they drive fierce elephants at one another while onlookers, behind long barriers draped in dark cloth over pebbled ground, roam about in high delight on strong clear toddy.

Now fast-forward to Kerala on an Ōṇam morning. New clothes elsewhere in the passage: the Ōṇakkōṭi (ഓണക്കോടി). Feasting and offerings sent to the temples: the sadya’s ancestors. Ritual combat: the Ōṇattallu (ഓണത്തല്ലു) that survives in pockets of central Kerala to this day. The continuities are real, and they run deep.

The poem holds one more surprise, and it cuts both ways. Māyōṉ is praised in the very first line as the god who conquered the massed avuṇar, the asuras. So the seed of the Vāmana story, a god victorious over asuras, is already attested at the festival’s root in its first appearance on record. But that is all that is there. What is absent is everything Kerala later grew from that seed. No Bali by name, no dwarf god, no three steps, and above all, no beloved king coming home.

The oldest Ōṇam is a day of victory, not a return of the demon king. That gap, roughly a millennium wide, between the festival’s first attestation and the first attestation of its now-central myth, is the single most important fact in Onam’s history.

_(The Maturaikkāñci is conventionally placed in the second or third century CE, following Kamil Zvelebil’s judgment that the most acceptable range for Sangam literature is roughly 100 BCE to 250 CE. Herman Tieken has argued for a much later date. David Shulman’s verdict that the corpus cannot be dated definitively on present evidence is the honest one. So: early first millennium, held loosely.)

By the early medieval centuries, the festival is everywhere in the Tamil record, and everywhere it is the god’s day, not the king’s. And the coupling of the great city feasts is itself Sangam-old: a Naṟṟiṇai (நற்றிணை) poet already pairs Uṟantai of the Paṅkuṉi festival with Vañci (வஞ்சி) of the Uḷḷi (உள்ளி) festival, in a single hyperbolic breath, cities so famous for their festivals that a lover’s beauty could be measured against them; the commentary on Iṟaiyaṉār’s Akapporuḷ then carries the trio forward, treating Uṟaiyūr’s Paṅkuṉi as a festival that took the whole city and counting Madurai’s Āvaṇi feast alongside it and Karuvūr’s Uḷḷi viḻā among the storied feasts of the great capitals.

> *பங்குனி விழவின் உறந்தையொடு உள்ளி விழவின் வஞ்சியும்*
>
> *paṅkuṉi viḻaviṉ uṟantaiyoṭu uḷḷi viḻaviṉ vañciyum*

Periyāḻvār, in the early ninth century, vows to keep twelve Tiruvōṇams a year with offerings of red rice, split gram, sweetened ghee and milk (பன்னிரண்டு திருவோணம் ஆட்டேன், *paṉṉiraṇṭu tiruvōṇam āṭṭēṉ*); tells Kaṇṇaṉ, seven days out, to keep the cattle home and stay adorned and dressed for his birthday, Tiruvōṇam; and pictures devotees wrapped in the Lord’s cast-off yellow garments, decked in his used tulasī, greeting him on the serpent couch on the Tiruvōṇam (திருவோணம்) festival day. And in the Tiruppallāṇṭu (திருப்பல்லாண்டு), he goes further and hands us a genealogy.

> *எந்தை தந்தை தந்தைதம் மூத்தப்பன் ஏழ்படிகால் தொடங்கி வந்து வழிவழி ஆட்செய்கின்றோம்
> திருவோணத் திருவிழவில்*
>
> *entai tantai tantaitam mūttappaṉ ēḻpaṭikāl toṭaṅki vantu vaḻivaḻi
> āṭceykiṉṟōm tiruvōṇat tiruviḻavil*

Meaning, beginning seven generations back, from my father, his father, his father’s father, and beyond, we have come serving in unbroken line at the Tiruvōṇam festival. Weigh that claim. If the poet of the early ninth century could assert seven generations of hereditary family service at the *tiruvōṇat tiruviḻā*, then the festival he knew was already an institution in his grandfather’s grandfather’s day, deep in the seventh century by his own calculation.

Nammāḻvār (நம்மாழ்வார்) sings the shrine at Tirukkāṭkarai (திருக்காட்கரை), the future Tṛkkākara (തൃക്കാക്കര) near Kochi, and the one who measured the three worlds. And in the fullest scholarly survey of this material, K.V. Sarma showed that Ōṇam was kept with special worship in Viṣṇu temples across South India, nowhere more elaborately than at Tirupati, whose deity he identifies as Viṣṇu in his Trivikrama-Vāmana form; yet the early festival, on his reading, honors the descent of the striding god without connecting it specifically with the Bali-Vāmana episode.

The star, the shrine, and the striding god were assembled by medieval times, but not yet the return of the king.

Before diving deeper into the festival’s evolution as the Malayalis know it, we should ask where the word Ōṇam itself comes from.

## Ōṇam, the name: a Linguistic Perspective



Where does the word Ōṇam come from?

The name is a star; the month is a season the whole ancient subcontinent kept.

The festival falls on the asterism sacred to Viṣṇu, called in Sanskrit both Śravaṇa and Śroṇā, and the Tamil names split that inheritance neatly in two. The month is Āvaṇi (ஆவணி), from the month-name *śrāvaṇa*: *śrāvaṇa \> sāvaṇa \> āvaṇa or Āvaṇi*, withthe initial cluster shed as Tamil phonology demands. The day is Ōṇam (ஓணம்), not from śrāvaṇa at all but directly from śroṇā, the star’s own name, worn smooth by the same Dravidian mouths: *śroṇā \> \*soṇā \> ōṇa*, Ōṇam, honored with tiru into Tiruvōṇam. Month from month and star from star, the two derivations run side by side, and the Bhāgavata’s own wording keeps both words in play when it fixes the star to the story: the Lord takes birth as Vāmana on *Śravaṇa-dvādaśī*, under *Śroṇā*, at the moment Abhijit (*śroṇāyāṁ śravaṇa-dvādaśyām*, 8.18.5–6). Star, month, god, and name lock together long before any king enters the frame.

Nor did Ōṇam sit alone on this star; the whole ancient subcontinent knew this stretch of the year as a season for festivity. Lunar Śrāvaṇa spilling into Bhādrapada, solar Āvaṇi and Ciṅṅam, carried a whole family of observances: the Śrāvaṇī or upākarma, when the Vedic schools renewed the sacred thread and recommenced study, kept in the Tamil country to this day as *Āvaṇi Aviṭṭam* (ஆவணி அவிட்டம்), the very name the Iṟaiyaṉār commentary gives Madurai’s great feast (and whether the commentator meant the upākarma or the adjacent Ōṇam season hardly matters, Ōṇam and Aviṭṭam being neighbouring asterisms in the same festive week); *Rakṣābandhan* (रक्षाबन्धन) on the Śrāvaṇa full moon in the north; *Nāraḷī Pūrṇimā* (नारळी पौर्णिमा) on the Konkan coast, when sailors offered coconuts to Lord Varuṇa and reopened the sailing season after the monsoon, a coincidence worth remembering when we come to the trade-winds question; and *Vāmana Jayantī* (वामन जयन्ती) itself, the dwarf’s birthday on *Śravaṇa-dvādaśī* (श्रवण द्वादशी) under his own star (P.V. Kane’s History of Dharmaśāstra, volume V, surveys this cluster of vratas and utsavas).

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kiB0!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F88c0ef29-4b12-46b0-9c17-ab292c119a12_1152x1728.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kiB0!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F88c0ef29-4b12-46b0-9c17-ab292c119a12_1152x1728.jpeg)





Ōṇam, in other words, is the Dravidian member of a pan-Indic Śravaṇa season, manifested in ancient Tamiḻakam (தமிழகம்), the region comprising present-day Tamil Nadu and Kerala. What is distinctively Tamil is the shape the day took, Māyōṉ’s festival of combats and feasts; what is distinctively Malayali is everything that came after.

Now let us dive into the mythology behind it.

## The Myth behind Mahābali



Kerala did not invent a sympathetic Bali. It inherited one, then did something remarkable with him.

The seed of the three-stride story is Vedic and startlingly spare. The Ṛgveda praises Viṣṇu, who measured out the worlds in three wide strides (1.22.17; 1.154), the wide-goer, the wide-strider, urugāya, urukrama; the name Trivikrama is the later crystallization of the hymn’s own verb, vi cakrame. Here is the oldest and tersest formulation, Ṛgveda 1.22.17 (Medhātithi Kāṇva):

> *idaṁ viṣṇur vi cakrame* *tredhā ni dadhe padam* *samūḷham asya
> pāṁsure*

“Through this world Viṣṇu strode wide; three times he planted his step; the whole lies gathered in the dust of it.” And the great Viṣṇu hymn, 1.154, sings the one “who alone measured out this long, far-extended dwelling-place with just three steps” (eko vimame tribhir it padebhiḥ), “in whose three wide strides all beings dwell” (yasyoruṣu triṣu vikramaṇeṣv adhikṣiyanti bhuvanāni viśvā), whose steps are “full of honey.” Notice the operative verb everywhere: vi-mā, to measure out. The strides create an ordered space. There is no dwarf god asking land from an asura king, no antagonist at all. It is pure cosmogony: a god making room for life.

And tuck away one detail for later, because it will pay off in Tamil. The most recited line of this whole complex, 1.22.20, has the wise forever beholding tad viṣṇoḥ paramaṁ padam, “that highest step of Viṣṇu, like an eye stretched across heaven,” and 1.154.5 places “the spring of honey” viṣṇoḥ pade parame, in Viṣṇu’s highest step. That phrase, paramaṁ padam, is precisely the word the Āḻvārs (ஆழ்வார்) and the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition inherit as *paramapatam* (பரமபதம்), their name for Viṣṇu’s heaven. When the Tamil Vaiṣṇava sings of paramapadam, he is naming the third stride of the Ṛgveda. The heaven of the tradition that built Tṛkkākara is, literally, the top step.

The next piece falls into place in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (1.2.5), where the gods, cheated of the earth by the asuras who are portioning it out among themselves, ask for only as much ground as Viṣṇu, “who was a dwarf,” can lie upon; the asuras, seeing no risk, agree; and through the dwarf the gods win back the whole world. Look at what is already assembled there: the contest with the asuras, the dwarf god, the fatally underestimated grant of land. And look at what is still missing: no sign of Bali, no disguise of the dwarf god, no three strides in that telling. The story we know, the disguised god, the generous asura king, the fatal donation, is a post-Vedic weld of the Ṛgvedic strides onto the Brāhmaṇa’s Vāmana, forged in the epics and perfected in the Purāṇas.

And we can put a startlingly early floor under that weld, in the unlikeliest of places: a grammar lesson. Pāṇini’s rule Aṣṭādhyāyī 3.1.26 is two dry words, hetumati ca, prescribing the causative affix when a prompter makes another act. Around the middle of the second century BCE, the grammarian Patañjali, working through this rule, took up verbs derived from the titles of narrative pieces: from Kaṁsavadham ācaṣṭe, “he performs the Slaying-of-Kaṁsa,” comes kaṁsaṁ ghātayati; from Balibandham ācaṣṭe, “he performs the Binding-of-Bali,” comes baliṁ bandhayati. Balibandha is cited as the proper name of a standard repertoire piece. Then Patañjali poses his puzzle: how can these be present tense, when cirahate kaṁse, cirabaddhe balau, “Kaṁsa was slain long ago, Bali was bound long ago”? His answer surveys the entertainment industry of his day, the *śaubhika* performers who *pratyakṣam*, before the very eyes of the spectators, make Kaṁsa be slain, and Bali be bound; the painted panels in which raised and fallen blows are seen; and the granthika reciters who narrate the deeds from origin to destruction. Performance makes the past present, so the present tense holds; the reciters, in his lovely phrase, sato buddhiviṣayān prakāśayanti, make manifest things that exist as objects of thought.

Patañjali says Balibandha, the Binding of Bali. He does not say Vāmana. Strictly, a binding is a separable motif, and in the Bhāgavata it is Varuṇa’s noose that binds the king, not the third stride. That the piece runs in harness with Kaṁsavadha makes a Vaiṣṇava story-cycle as near to certain as such things get, and the dwarf’s episode is far and away the likeliest referent; but the dwarf is not named, and we should say so.

Which raises the question this essay has been circling. When does Mahābali first stand beneath Vāmana’s foot? Not in the Ṛgveda, which has the strides and no antagonist at all. Not in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, whose asuras are a nameless crowd around an unnamed dwarf. Mahābali’s family, in fact, reaches Vedic literature by an entirely different road: the Atharvaveda’s great Virāj hymn (8.10) has the asuras take their milking with Virocana Prāhrādi, son of Prahrāda, standing as their calf, and the Chāndogya Upaniṣad (8.7–8) sends that same Virocana to Prajāpati alongside Indra, where he mistakes the body for the self and carries the error home to his people. Prahlāda and Virocana are thus Vedic and Upaniṣadic figures, but their heir is not yet in the dwarf’s story. The join, Mahābali, named as the king who gives the three paces and loses the worlds, becomes securely visible only in the epics, in the Rāmāyaṇa’s Bālakāṇḍa (sarga 29) and the Mahābhārata, which also preserves the Bali-Vāsava dialogues of the Śānti Parvan, where a fallen Bali argues with Indra, serenely and at length, about time, fortune, and the vanity of victors. Set beside them, the Mahābhāṣya remains the earliest datable notice that a story about Bali’s binding existed under a title and drew paying crowds.

By the time the people of the Maturaikkāñci’s Madurai gathered for Ōṇam, the Mahābali story already existed and traveled. Its absence from the poem is therefore not because the tale was unborn; it is because the tale was not yet what the day was about. And it lays an early cornerstone under Balipratipadā in the north, the day of Mahābali’s return, which we will meet properly in a moment.

A crucial point I would like to highlight, established in the classic studies by Deborah Soifer and Clifford Hospital: as the myth matures, Mahābali gets better. Across the Purāṇic corpus, he evolves from enemy of cosmic order into a model of truthfulness, generosity, and devotion, a righteous demon whose defeat reads less like punishment than like grace.

## Bhāgavata and Mahābali



The telling that matters most for Ōṇam is the Bhāgavata’s nine chapters of the Eighth Skandha (8.15 to 8.23), because it is there that Bali becomes unambiguously beloved.

Killed in an earlier war and revived by Śukrācārya, he performs the Viśvajit sacrifice and takes Amarāvatī; Bṛhaspati counsels Indra not to fight, since a strength drawn from brāhmaṇa favor cannot be broken by force. Bali, in other words, holds heaven legitimately. Aditi then keeps the payo-vrata, and the Lord is born to her under our own star: *śroṇāyāṁ śravaṇa-dvādaśyāṁ muhūrte 'bhijiti*, under Śroṇā, on the Śravaṇa dvādaśī, in the Abhijit hour, a day the text names Vijayā (8.18.5–6). He contracts himself into a dwarf and walks to Bali's sacrifice at Bhṛgukaccha on the Narmadā.

He asks three paces measured by his own feet. Bali offers islands, villages, gold; the dwarf refuses with the line that gives the episode its ethical spine, that one not content with three paces will not be content with a continent. Śukrācārya recognizes the god and orders his pupil to renege, arguing that untruth is permitted in extremity, and Bali’s answer is the reason Kerala could one day love him: no sin is greater than falsehood, the earth bears everything except a liar, and to be robbed by the Lord is the highest fortune. Cursed by his guru with the loss of all wealth, he pours the water anyway. Then Trivikrama: one stride takes the earth, the second the heavens, and from the washing of that upraised foot the Gaṅgā descends, which is why she is Viṣṇupadī. Bali’s followers rush to arms, and he stops them, saying it is time, not the dwarf, that has turned against them. Garuḍa binds him with Varuṇa’s noose, the very detail behind Patañjali’s Balibandha, and asks for the third step; he offers his own head.

What follows is not punishment. Prahlāda intercedes, Vindhyāvalī prays, and Viṣṇu grants Bali Sutala, a realm finer than Indra’s own, promises him the office of Indra in the coming manvantara, and then takes up his mace to stand as gatekeeper at Bali’s door; the same passage explains that the Lord strips of wealth those he favors, because riches breed pride. Elsewhere, Bali is numbered among the twelve mahājanas, the authorities on dharma, beside Prahlāda and Bhīṣma. But mark what the Bhāgavata does not say. There is no Kerala in it, no annual leave of absence, no homecoming. Its Bali is enthroned, guarded by God himself, and deferred to a future Indrahood. That silence is the exact space Kerala filled, and when it is argued today that the homecoming has no purāṇic warrant, this is what the claim rests on: half true and beside the point, since Sutala now and Indra later are already a promise of return, and a festival was never a footnote to scripture.

## Who was Mahābali or Māvēli?



Put the genealogy on the table because it explains the king’s prestige. In the Purāṇic reckoning, Mahābali is daitya royalty of the most devout line in the asura world: Kaśyapa and Diti beget Hiraṇyakaśipu; his son is Prahlāda, the boy-saint of Viṣṇu bhakti; Prahlāda’s son is Virocana; Virocana’s son is Bali, “Mahābali” being simply Bali the Great. His queen is Vindhyāvalī; his son is Bāṇāsura. He is, in other words, the grandson of the tradition’s greatest devotee, and the texts never let us forget it: at the catastrophe of the third stride it is Prahlāda who steps forward to intercede, and Viṣṇu who ends the scene not with a killing but with a coronation in reverse, Sutala granted as a realm finer than heaven, the Lord himself standing guard at Bali’s door, the office of Indra promised in a manvantara to come. Bali is even counted among the cirañjīvis, the deathless. In the Sanskrit tradition, he is not destroyed. He is deferred, a king in waiting.

The name traveled south and softened as it went: Mahābali, Prakritic Māvali, Malayalam Māvēli. Already in the Āḻvārs’ poems, Māvali is an established character, the king from whom the Lord requested mūvaṭi maṇ, three paces of earth. Hear Tirumaṅkai Āḻvār (திருமங்கை ஆழ்வார்) sing it, in his decad on Cīrāmaviṇṇakaram at Kāḻi (Sirkazhi), from the Periya Tirumoḻi (பெரிய திருமொழி):

> ஒரு குறள் ஆய் இரு நிலம் மூவடி மண் வேண்டி  
> உலகு அனைத்தும் ஈர் அடியால் ஒடுக்கி ஒன்றும்  
> தருக எனா மாவலியைச் சிறையில் வைத்த   
> தாடாளன் தாள் அணைவீர் தக்க கீர்த்தி   
> அரு மறையின் திரள் நான்கும் வேள்வி   
> ஐந்தும் அங்கங்கள்-அவை ஆறும் இசைகள்   
> ஏழும் தெருவில் மலி விழா வளமும் சிறக்கும்   
> காழிச் சீராமவிண்ணகரே சேர்மின் நீரே

> *oru kuṟaḷ āy iru nilam mūvaṭi maṇ vēṇṭi*   
> *ulaku aṉaittum īr aṭiyāl oṭukki oṉṟum*   
> *taruka eṉā māvaliyaic ciṟaiyil vaitta*   
> *tāṭāḷaṉ tāḷ aṇaivīr takka kīrtti*   
> *aru maṟaiyiṉ tiraḷ nāṉkum vēḷvi   
> aintum* *aṅkaṅkaḷ-avai āṟum icaikaḷ   
> ēḻum* *teruvil mali viḻā vaḷamum ciṟakkum

Meaning: becoming a little dwarf, he begged three paces of the broad earth, compressed all the worlds in two strides, and saying “now give the one that remains,” bound Māvali; you who would reach the feet of that mighty one, join Kāḻi’s Cīrāmaviṇṇakaram of fitting fame, where the four rare Vedas, the five sacrifices, the six ancillaries, the seven musics, and streets brimming with the wealth of festivals all flourish. Notice, in passing, what the Āḻvār counts among a holy town’s glories, right beside the Vedas themselves: teruvil mali viḻā vaḷam, streets overflowing with festival. The tradition itself measured sanctity in celebrations.

And the affection was never Kerala’s alone. In Maharashtra and across the north, the day after Dīwāli is Balipratipadā, Mahābali’s own day, when he is welcomed back to the earth with a blessing whose spirit any Malayali will recognize at once:

> *इडा पीडा टळॊ, बळीचॆ राज्य यॆवॊ  
> iḍā pīḍā ṭaḷo, Baḷīce rājya yevo*
>
> *may troubles and sorrows flee, may Bali’s kingdom come.*

Tulunadu keeps his memory too. And in the Andhra-Tamil borderlands, a real medieval dynasty, the Bāṇas, styled themselves Mahāvali Vāṇarāyar in their inscriptions and claimed descent from Mahābali through Bāṇāsura: kings proud, in stone, to be Bali’s heirs.

### Balipratipadā: the Lesser-Known Return of Mahābali



That Marathi blessing deserves more than a passing nod, because Balipratipadā (बलिप्रतिपदा) is not a footnote to Ōṇam. It is a full parallel homecoming festival with its own deep pedigree. The day is *Kārttika śukla pratipadā*, the first of the bright fortnight after Dīvāli’s new moon, falling in October or November; the digests count it among the three and a half wholly auspicious muhūrtas of the year, and in Gujarat and Rajasthan it is New Year’s Day itself, the Vikram Samvat turning over as Mahābali returns. The textual trail is old. The festivities are noticed in the Mahābhārata’s Vanaparvan (28.2) and across the Purāṇas, Brahma (chapter 73), Kūrma (chapter 1), Matsya (chapters 245 and 246); and the medieval digests, the Dharmasindhu and the Bhaviṣyottara among them, codify the rites, beginning with an oil bath at dawn; the image of Mahābali, sometimes with his queen Vindhyāvalī, drawn on the floor with five-colored powders and flowers; food and fruit set before the king; lamps at every sill; new clothes; the cattle decorated and honored; artisans thanking their tools; dramas staged; and community sports, the kaumudī-mahotsava, down to the tug-of-war (P.V. Kane assembles the whole dossier in History of Dharmaśāstra, volume V, pages 201 to 206).

Now read that list of activities codified in the north beside Ōṇam and let the hair rise a little. A royal image drawn on the ground in colors and flowers: the *pūkkaḷam* (പൂക്കളം) with its clay Tṛkkākarayappaṉ at the center. A feast set before the returning king: the sadya. New clothes: the *Ōṇakkōṭi*. Lamps, games, and contests for the whole community: the *Ōṇakkaḷikaḷ*, the *tallu*, the boat races. Two festivals at two ends of the subcontinent, one ritual grammar: draw the king, feed the king, play before the king. And both stand at new-year thresholds after the rains, Kerala’s at the solar Ciṅṅam new year as the southwest monsoon ebbs, the north’s at the Kārttika new year as the season turns. In both, the returning Bali rides the turning of the agricultural year.

It doesn’t stop here; the spread runs far beyond Maharashtra. In Karnataka, the day is*Bali Pāḍyami*(ಬಲಿ ಪಾಡ್ಯಮಿ), with a Bali shaped of cow dung set on a kolam-decked plank amid marigolds, the cowshed ceremonially cleaned, and the cows worshipped. In Himachal Pradesh, it survives as Barlaj, a worn-down version of*Bali Rāj;*when folk songs of Vāmana are sung, the plow rests, and artisans honor their tools. In Jammu, women shape Raja Bali from wheat dough, worship him, and immerse him in water. In Benares, Tracy Pintchman found the women’s Kārttik traditions binding Bali into the Samudra-manthana story and the birth of Lakṣmī, so that the goddess of Dīwāli and the demon king of its fourth day belong to a single tale (Pintchman, Guests at God’s Wedding). And one more name for the day is quietly telling: *Dyūtapratipadā* (द्यूतप्रतिपदा), the pratipadā of dice, when gambling is licensed, and even Śiva and Pārvatī are said to have played. A day when the ordinary rules pause, kept in honor of a king under whom, the songs insist, no one ever cheated: the joke writes itself, and it is an old one.

Yet no two stories about the return of Mahābali are twins, and the differences teach as much as the likeness. The northern digests prescribe worship of Mahābali, a bali-*pūjā* with icon and offerings; Kerala does not install Māvēli on an altar but receives him as a guest and a kinsman, the *pūjā* going to the Tṛkkākarayappaṉ while the king gets the welcome, and only the *Ōṇappoṭṭaṉ* (ഓണപ്പൊട്ടൻ) of Malabar embodies him outright. Balipratipadā is one day inside a five-day festival whose center of gravity is Lakṣmī, an appendix to the goddess’s season; Ōṇam is the sovereign festival of its land, ten days with the king himself at the center. In some regions, Balipratipadā even includes śrāddha offerings for the recently deceased, the same ancestral undertow we will encounter again in North Malabar. And the calendars part ways in one respect that matters enormously: the north gives the god and the king separate days, Vāmana Jayantī on Śravaṇa-dvādaśī in Bhādrapada and Bali’s pratipadā two months later in Kārttika, so triumph and homecoming never compete for the same stage. Kerala’s solar reckoning grants no such courtesy: Vāmana Jayantī falls each year inside the Ōṇam season itself, god and king pressed onto a single day.

Kerala’s move, then, was not to rehabilitate a demon king. It was to localize an already honored king by seating his golden age in Kerala itself, binding his memory to Tṛkkākara, and converting the Purāṇic deferral, Sutala now and Indrahood later, into an annual homecoming on Tiruvōṇam, shifting the festival’s emotional center of gravity from the god who wins to the king who leaves and returns. When that last step happened, no dated source yet tells us; it lies somewhere in the silence between the medieval inscriptions and the early modern folk songs.

## What the stones actually say



Now to the layer where history stands firmest: the inscriptions of the Cēra Perumāḷs of Makōtai.

The popular claim is that an 861 CE inscription at the Tṛkkākara temple contains the earliest reference to Ōṇam.

But the earliest epigraphic reference to Ōṇam is not at Tṛkkākara at all. It belongs to the temple of Tiru Āṟṟuvāy at Tiruvalla in central Kerala, the Śrīvallabha shrine the Āḻvārs sang as Tiruvallavāḻ, one of the divya deśams: a copper plate of the Cēramāṉ Sthāṇu Ravi Kulaśēkhara, regnal year seventeen, Kollam era 36, that is 861 CE, in Vaṭṭeḻuttu with Grantha, in Old Malayalam (”Tiruvalla Plate of Sthanu Ravi, No. III,” in T.A. Gopinatha Rao’s edition, *Travancore Archaeological Series*, vol. II). Better still, we can read the plate’s own opening words:

> *śrī kō-t-tāṇu iravikku cellāniṉṟa yāṇṭu patiṉēḻu, ivvāṇṭu
> tiruvāṟṟuvāy sabhaiyum aṭikaḷmārum avirōdhattāl kūṭi ceyta kaccam:
> puṉceyppaṭakārattu cēntañ caṅkaraṉ āvaṇi-y-ōṇam aṭuvāṉ koṭutta
> pūmi...*

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!fzXb!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fac29e3c9-230f-4eaa-a335-da5144f127a6_2544x768.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!fzXb!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fac29e3c9-230f-4eaa-a335-da5144f127a6_2544x768.jpeg)





Translation: “In the seventeenth year current of Śrī Kō Sthāṇu Ravi, the Tiruvāṟṟuvāy *sabhā* and the *aṭikaḷmār*, assembling without dissent, made this compact: the land given by Cēntaṉ Caṅkaraṉ of Puṉceyppaṭakāram for cooking the ***Āvaṇi Ōṇam***...” The grant then counts it out: so many kalam of Cēntaṉār rice, five hundred nāḻi from the newly cleared land, “and with these the village shall conduct the Ōṇam” (*ivai koṇṭu ūr maṟaiyāl ōṇam aṭakkaṭavar*).

A temple assembly and its dignitaries, meeting *avirōdhattāl* (അവിരോധത്താൽ), in unanimity, to endow an annadāna, a feeding of all comers at Ōṇam, at a Viṣṇu shrine already famous in Āḻvār song. The instrument they draw up is a *kaccam*, the same word of temple law that names the Mūḻikkuḷam *kaccam* a century later: Ōṇam enters Kerala’s written record not as a myth but as a legal act, a menu, and a budget. One Cēntaṉ Caṅkaraṉ, otherwise unknown to history, bought himself eleven and a half centuries of remembrance with a gift of rice land. And the feast’s name, Āvaṇi Ōṇam, ties Kerala by month and by star straight back to the Madurai of the Sangam poem.

It doesn’t stop there; the thread continues. The great Huzur Treasury plates of the same Tiruvalla temple, the longest of Kerala’s early records, carry it on: among their hundreds of lines of temple accounts, properties stand booked for the expenses falling within the Āvaṇi Ōṇam of Tiruvallavāḻ (അവണിയോണത്തിനുൾ ചെല്ലും ചെലവു), the god of the divya deśam now named in his own ledgers as host of the feast, with donors such as Kēcavaṉ of *Iṭaicceri* entering land at Veḷiyaṉūrkāṭu against the Tiruvōṇam day. The Tṛkkākara inscriptions themselves begin in the tenth century and run to the thirteenth: gifts of lamps, land, and gold for daily offerings and feasts, including a grant from the reign of Bhāskara Ravi that lays out the festival’s rhythm in measures of rice and ghee, offerings running “beginning from Pūrāṭam through to Ōṇam” (പൂരാടൻ തുടങ്കി ഓണത്തളവും; Sarma prints tiṭaṅki), three days of tiruvamṛtu set before the deity, after which the Brahmins and Śrīvaiṣṇavas are fed and the remainder measured out to the temple’s emperumakkaḷ.

How grand did this imperial Ōṇam become? Down to about 500 CE, Malaināḍu was simply the western face of Tamiḻakam, one country in custom and speech; the change comes with the Kulaśēkhara revival from about 800 CE, ruling from Mahōdayapuram, the two centuries historians have called Kerala’s golden age, when Ōṇam acquired what Sarma describes as high public and political significance. Elamkulam Kunjan Pillai, reading the epigraphy together with local memory, reconstructed a twenty-eight-day festival of worship, feasts, fairs, and friendly feats of arms culminating on Ōṇam day, at which the Kulaśēkhara emperor played host to his vassals, each chieftain with his own remembered camping ground at Tṛkkākara, capital of Kālkarai-nāṭu; Sarma follows him, and reads the Attaccamayam procession, performed on the day of Attam, ten days before Tiruvōṇam, by the Mahārāja of Cochin and the Zamorin of Calicut, as the last living remnant of that assemblage of chieftains. Call this what it is: scholarly reconstruction, plausible and grounded in the records, but reconstruction. What remains pure later tradition is the arithmetic of legend, the fifty-six kings and sixty-four chieftains, the Cēramāṉ Perumāḷ decreeing Ōṇam in every home, and the two-thousand-year-old temple, which belongs to piety, not epigraphy.

And through all of it, one silence holds. The stones and copper plates speak of endowments, measures, lamps, and feasts for the god of Tṛkkākara, the Vāmana of the temple-state reconstructed by M.G.S. Narayanan, Kesavan Veluthat, and Rajan Gurukkal. They never once narrate Mahābali’s return. A.M. Kurup, surveying this same literary and epigraphic record for the anthropologists half a century ago, drew the sober conclusion that the historical Ōṇam was a temple-based community festival, celebrated over a period (”The Sociology of Onam,” 1977).

When, then, did the king enter the festival? Narayanan’s own suggestion, relayed through M.G. Sasibhooshan, is worth weighing: that as the temple order hardened, epitomized by the Mūḻikkuḷam *kaccam*, a popular counter-current among cultivators gathered around the figure of the dispossessed Mahābali, until, over centuries, Ōṇam became his celebration. This is a hypothesis, not a conclusive proof. But it fits the shape of the evidence: a temple festival first, the people’s king after.

By the fourteenth century, the *maṇipravāḷam* *sandēśakāvya* Uṇṇunīlisandēśam (ഉണ്ണുനീലിസന്ദേശം), a messenger-poem in the line of the *Meghadūta*, shows how thoroughly Ōṇam was woven into Kerala life, to the point that it had become the measure of other things. Guiding his messenger up the road, the poet points out a beauty waiting by the wayside:

> വീണാവാണീം വടിവിനൊടിടത്തങ്ങടുത്തുണ്ണിയച്ചീ- മേണാങ്കന്നും ചെറുതു കുറവാക്കിന്റ
> വക്ത്രാരവിന്ദാം ഓണം ചോലേ വിരവിലെഴുന്നള്ളിന്റ നിന്മേൽത്തദാനീം കാണാം തൂവെൺ മുറുവൽ
> മലരാൽത്തൂകി മേവിന്റവാറ്

Her voice a vīṇā, her lotus face putting the moon to shame a little, Uṇṇiyacci stands, scattering flowers of pure white smiles upon you as you arrive in haste, *ōṇam pōlē*, “like Ōṇam.” The Uṇṇunīlītilakam commentary spells the simile out: she receives Ādityavarma’s coming exactly as one receives Ōṇam, with festivity. Later in the same poem, the heroine herself becomes the festival, the commentary glossing that she is Onam to Kāma, and to Lakṣmī the very house of her rising. And when the route passes Ōṭanāṭu, the commentary pauses to note its living name, Ōṇāṭṭukara, the Onam-country of Kāyaṁkuḷam.

A poet could measure a woman’s beauty and a lover’s welcome against Ōṇam and expect every reader to feel it without a word of explanation. The festival is everywhere. The return of the king, still, is nowhere stated.

## The song that sounds ancient and mostly isn’t



Nothing in the whole of Ōṇam feels older than this song:

> *മാവേലി നാടു വാണീടും കാലം, മാനുഷരെല്ലാരും ഒന്നുപോലെ  
> Māvēli nāḍu vāṇīṭum kālam, mānuṣar ellārum onnu pōle*
>
> *When Māvēli ruled the land, all people were as one. No lies, no
> theft, no false measures, no caste.*

Yet the song, in the sharply egalitarian form we all learned, carries the fingerprints of the twentieth century. Its explicit anti-caste utopia belongs to the world of Kerala’s reform movements, and it is closely associated with Sahodaran Ayyappan, the Ezhava rationalist in the circle of Nārāyaṇa Guru, who is widely credited with composing or recasting an Ōṇappāṭṭu.

Older folk verses praising Māvēli’s just reign very likely existed; the reformers did not conjure the beloved king out of thin air. But the pre-caste paradise, stated as such, is probably modern. Which means the most quoted ancient evidence for an egalitarian ancient Kerala is, in fact, a modern hope sung backward into the past.

The egalitarian Bali was not born in Kerala either. Half a century before Sahodaran Ayyappan, in the 1870s in Maharashtra, Jotirao Phule built an entire political movement on Baliraja. In Gulamgiri (1873) and across his writing, Phule read Bali as the ideal indigenous peasant king and Vāmana as the Brahman usurper, the myth itself as a memory of conquest and caste; and he seized on precisely the proverbial blessing quoted above, *iḍā pīḍā ṭaḷo, baḷīce rājya yevo*, as the peasantry’s own suppressed longing for the king’s return (Rosalind O’Hanlon’s Caste, Conflict and Ideology remains the standard study). So the asura king Bali, like the beloved Bali, is pan-Indian: Phule’s Marathi Baliraja is the elder cousin of Ayyappan’s Malayali Māvēli, each reformer finding in his region’s festival the same king. Whether Ayyappan drew directly on Phule or arrived independently, the sources do not yet tell us; the parallel alone is the point.

The anthropologists Filippo and Caroline Osella mapped what the myth has since become: a single story claimed by rival readers. For the orthodox Vaiṣṇava, Vāmana is the hero and Bali the humbled devotee. For the Dravidianist and the anti-caste reformist, Mahābali is the just indigenous king undone by a scheming Brahman god.

But the festival, generously, hosts them all today.

## The soil under the story



Strip away every myth and a festival remains. But where, exactly, does the harvest festival come from? The label is now so automatic, in textbooks, in tourism copy, in every greeting card, that it hides a real question.

The material coincidence is genuine and needs no myth to explain it. Ciṅṅam opens as the southwest monsoon slackens and the *virippu*, the first paddy crop of the year, comes in from fields sown with the early rains. It follows Karkiṭakam, the *paññamāsam*, the lean month of scarcity, medicine and unbroken rain, when the granaries of an older Kerala ran lowest, and the almanac itself counseled endurance. A feast laid at that threshold is the most natural thing in the world. And the Kollam era begins on the first of Ciṅṅam, so the season is a new year as well as a first harvest, exactly as Balipratipadā is a new year in Gujarat. Relief, plenty, renewal: the returning king is the harvest, spoken in the language of kingship.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!IyVn!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1d01a80f-fdb7-4400-8e27-af8f94555eac_1152x1728.jpeg)





Kerala’s own explicit first-fruits rituals are in fact adjacent observances that sit beside Ōṇam rather than inside it: the Illam Nira, when the first sheaf is cut and carried home in procession, and the ears of new grain are fixed above the door and on the vessels of the house, and the puttari, the first meal of the new rice. In the Ōṇāṭṭukara country, the Irupattiyettām Ōṇam, the twenty-eighth Ōṇam kept well after the great day, retains a frankly agrarian character, with cattle honored and the harvest thanked.

The harvest reading is not wrong, and the twentieth century did not invent the rice on the leaf. But it did quietly promote a coincidence into a definition. The Osellas have read the sadya itself, the feast on the plantain leaf, as Kerala performing its self-image as an agricultural homeland, even as remittances, not paddy, now pay for the pappaḍam.

And the practice is gloriously plural. The *pūkkaḷam* lay petal by petal from Atham to Tiruvōṇam, with the conical clay Tṛkkākarayappaṉ at its heart, a form that is somehow Vāmana and Mahābali at once. The vaḷḷaṁkaḷi is thundering down the Pamba. Pulikkaḷi’s painted tigers in Thrissur, Kaikoṭṭikkaḷi in the courtyards, the ceremonial Ōṇavillu. In North Malabar, oldest-feeling of all, the Ōṇappoṭṭaṉ: a Theyyam-descended figure, silent, bell in hand, performed by Malayan and Vaṇṇāṉ households, who walk door to door as Mahābali himself. If a pre-temple stratum of Ōṇam survives anywhere, it walks there, and it does not speak.

## The Modern Revision of an Ancient Day



The Kochi Mahārājas processed on *Attam* day during the Attaccamayam, as the Zamorin once did; the pageant lapsed with the princely state and was revived in 1961 as a public spectacle. That same year, the government of Pattom A. Thanu Pillai made Ōṇam the state festival of Kerala, and a temple-and-household observance was reframed as the secular birthright of every Malayali. The reframing largely took the form of: Hindu and Christian Kerala keep the feast together, and public Ōṇam is genuinely communal, though the Osellas record its limits, with Muslim participation thinner and contested in reformist circles. (Delicious archival footnote: RTI queries have reportedly failed to locate any surviving order actually declaring Ōṇam the state festival.) Tourism Week followed, then the Gulf, where diaspora Māvēlis in rented crowns now bless hypermarket atriums from Dubai to Doha, and a consumption season that retailers estimate in the tens of thousands of crores.

## Coda, with the Promise of a New Year



So who comes home at *Tiruvōṇam*?

What began as a festival of Māyōṉ, remembered across the old Tamil country and surviving as a mass celebration chiefly in Kerala. Anchored in the Sangam poems of the Maturaikkāñci and the Naṟṟiṇai, in the deep mythological past of the Veda and the Purāṇas, in the Āḻvār corpus, and in the stone inscriptions and copper plates of the Cēra Perumāḷs, the evidence lets us say with some confidence that Ōṇam traces its origin to a Vaiṣṇava festival once kept across the south, across ancient Tamiḻakam. It is also plausible that beneath the Vaiṣṇava layer lies an older harvest and ancestor-visitation festival, later absorbed into Ōṇam, its trace surviving in the silent Ōṇappoṭṭaṉ and in the homecoming motif itself. As for the trade winds, the coincidence of Nāraḷī Pūrṇimā on the far coast is suggestive, but the sailing season alone will not carry the weight of an origin.

And one last thought, before the *pūkkaḷam* is swept away.

The Maṟavar of Madurai no longer drive fierce elephants at one another on Ōṇa nāḷ, roaring on clear toddy behind blue-draped barriers. The Kulaśēkhara emperor no longer camps his vassals on the grounds of Tṛkkākara. The śaubhikas no longer bind Bali nightly before painted crowds. Nothing in this essay survives unchanged, because surviving unchanged is not what living cultures do. Cultures evolve. Myths evolve. Stories walk out of one century’s mouths into another’s and put on new clothes, like everyone else at Ōṇam. A cultural festival belongs to whoever carries it, and those who embrace it with a full heart become the torchbearers who move it forward. The question of ownership answered itself long ago. Ōṇam today is the heart and soul of the people of Kerala, kept wholeheartedly in every home and across every sea the Malayali has crossed, and at the feast it makes no difference whether the leaf is laid for the returning asura king Māvēli or for the dwarf god rooted at Tṛkkākara. Both are welcomed at the same door, with the same flowers. Both, in the end, are the same welcome.

The story of the righteous king Māvēli returning to his people is young, while the longing is ancient. The festival belongs, as it always has, to those who keep it. That, on the evidence, is the true antiquity of Ōṇam.

***Wish everyone a happy Onam, Ashamsakal! :)***
