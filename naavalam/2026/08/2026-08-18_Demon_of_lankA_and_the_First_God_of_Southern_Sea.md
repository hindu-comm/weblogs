# Demon of Laṅkā and the First God of Southern Sea

*In the Galle district of southern Sri Lanka, anthropologists watched an exorcist spend an entire night joking with the most feared demon on the island, gambling with him, sharing meat with him, until the patient rose well at dawn. Across the water, in the Tamil far south, a god with the same name was being fed the same feast. Nobody called him a demon there. They called him the son of Śiva.*

Stand at the edge of a village in Tirunelveli district in Tamil Nadu on a koṭai (கொடை) night. The shrine is a trident and a truncated obelisk under a neem tree. The graveyard is a stone’s throw away, and that is the point, because the god of this shrine is the graveyard’s king or the chief. The Kaṇiyāṉ drummers have been playing since dark. A man in the crowd begins to shake. The god starts to descend into his body, as he always has. Before dawn, they will carry blood-mixed rice into the burning ground and set it down among the ashes.

The god is *Cuṭalai Māṭaṉ* (சுடலை மாடன்), *Māṭacāmi* to those who love him. His Sanskritic title is *Mācāṉam* (மாசானம்).

Now cross the water. South of Galle, on the same night, a family has hired the hereditary drummers of the Berava community for a *Mahasona samayama,* a *tovil* (තොවිල්), one of the night-long ceremonies by which the Sinhala low country treats the illnesses sent by the yakku. Toward the deepest hour, a dancer enters wearing a bear-snouted mask, and the torchlight catches the face every Sinhala child has been warned about: the shoulder-crusher, the haunter of three-way junctions, the mightiest of the demons.

His name is *Maha Sohona* (මහසෝනා), the Great One of the *sohona* (සොහොන), the graveyard.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ol52!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F09fe35ba-630a-45f2-92fe-02347d58a8c3_860x860.jpeg)

One shore worships him as a god. The other exorcises him as a demon. Both are the same deity, remembered in two ways.

## What’s in a name



Take the Tamil title first. *Mācāṉam* is not a mysterious word. It is the ordinary Tamil form of Sanskrit *śmaśāna*, “cremation ground,” borrowed directly (Tamil Lexicon, s.v. *macāṉam*). His Tamil name says the same thing twice: *cuṭalai (சுடலை)*, the burning ground; *māṭaṉ (மாடன்)*, the chief. And *māṭaṉ* itself may carry a picture. Eveline Masilamani-Meyer derives it from *māṭu*, “bull.” The Māṭaṉ of the far south are bovine-headed gods (Masilamani-Meyer 2004: 28, 226).

Now, the Sinhala. *Sohona*, “grave,” is not mysterious either. Geiger’s etymological glossary traces it through Pali *susāna*, which descends from thesame Sanskrit *śmaśāna*.

You might wonder what’s in a name, but it is the hinge of everything that follows. The two gods have the same name, not similar names. The same Sanskrit word, reaching the two shores by two independent roads: a direct loan into Tamil, an inherited Prakrit descent into Sinhala. And, on both shores, on the supreme being of the burial ground, dignified with an honorific of mastery. Chief on one coast, Great Demon on the other.

Stories about a god change with every century, and we will watch both islands rewrite his story. But a name of this kind is a different sort of evidence, because names travel with worship, not with storytellers. When two cultures joined by two thousand years of sea lanes both call their graveyard sovereign the Śmaśāna lord, coincidence is still possible.

But a name is only the first exhibit. Let us meet the two claimants properly, then dig for what lies beneath them both.

## The same feast on both shores



Set the two nights side by side and watch what the rituals actually do.

On the Tamil shore, the koṭai. Hereditary specialists, the Kaṇiyāṉ drummer-shamans, summon the god by singing his story. The villuppāṭṭu (வில்லுப்பாட்டு), the bow-song of exactly this Kanyakumari and Tirunelveli belt, is the instrument by which this region sings its dead into divinity, as Stuart Blackburn’s fieldwork showed. The god arrives into a person: the possessed oracle shakes, dances, and speaks the year’s fortunes. He is fed what he actually eats: roosters, goats, arrack, blood-mixed rice, a portion carried into the graveyard itself. No Brahmin officiates. At dawn the village disperses, protected for another year.

\[\]

On the island shore, the samayama. Hereditary specialists, the Berava drummers, summon the yakā by singing his story. The *yak kavi*, verses of his birth, death, and transformation, are recited to call him into presence. The Sinhala theory of illness is a theory of sight: the yakā’s *diṣṭiya*, his gaze, has fallen on someone caught in *tanikama*, the aloneness of dusk, of crossroads, of the graveyard’s edge. And the cure is not a battle. Nobody fights Maha Sohona. The cure is a structured feast. The *dola pideni* offerings are presented through the watches of the night, the yakā is danced into visibility by a masked and entranced performer, and then, in Kapferer’s famous observation, he is drawn into comedy, teased back down to his proper size in front of the whole village, and sent away satisfied. At dawn the patient rises, healed.

Same drums, held by birth. Same sung ballads of birth and death as the summons. Same arrival in an entranced human body. Same menu, and the menu is ancient law: “meat and intoxicating drinks (sura) are the food of Yakṣas,” rules Manu himself (XI.96). Same graveyard. Same dawn.

None of this machinery is a late folk invention, either. The cult’s own earliest records already contain it. In the Jain Antagaḍa Dasāo, the garland-maker Ajjuṇae worships every morning at the shrine of the yakkha Moggara-pāṇi, a shrine that had belonged to his grandfather, his great-grandfather, and his great-great-grandfather. When his faith cracks in front of the wooden statue (”’tis plain this is but a log”), the yakkha answers by entering and possessing his body (Coomaraswamy 1928: 21–22). A hereditary shrine, a doubted image, a god arriving in a human frame. The koṭai and the samayama are performing a liturgy older than either island’s theology.

There is really only one structural difference between the two nights, and it is the label. The Sinhala rite runs on *varam*: the yakku may afflict and accept offerings only under a warrant descending from the Buddha through Vessavaṇa, their king, and every tovil reminds the yakā of his limits. In other words, the feast survived on the island by agreeing to call itself medicine. Coomaraswamy saw through this arrangement nearly a century ago. Writing about the demoness *Hārītī*, whose conversion earned her a portion in every monastery, he said the legend reads more like an explanation or justification of a cult than a true account of its origin, an edifying sanction for an ancient cult too strong to be subverted.

The offerings could continue if they were called medicine. The gods could keep their nights if they were called demons. Hold that thought, and let us look at the demon’s face.

## The demon with a seam down his back



Every Sinhala source tells a small masterpiece of the macabre. In the days of king Duṭugämuṇu, the second century BCE, lived the giant warrior Riṭigala Jayasēna, a man, the tradition is careful to say, of the yakṣa clan. He picked a quarrel with Goṭaimbara, one of the king’s ten champions, and they met, of all places, in a graveyard. Goṭaimbara kicked his head from his shoulders. Then the planetary god Senasurā, moved to intervene, seized the head of a bear and pressed it onto the corpse before it cooled. Backward, in some tellings, which is why the great yakā’s aim is mercifully poor. What rose from that graveyard was Maha Sohona: bear-faced, boar-mounted, crusher of shoulders, bringer of cholera.

He is met with the *tovil* (තොවිල්), the all-night masked exorcism whose drummers hold the office by birth, exactly as the Kaṇiyāṉ do across the water, and his samayama is the greatest healing ceremony on the island.

It is a wonderful story, with three seams running down its back. The folk songs disagree and offer entirely different origins, and they cannot decide whether he is a demon or a deity. The story gives away its own ground: by making Jayasēna a yakṣa clansman, it admits that this being belongs to the island’s pre-Buddhist spirit population, and merely supplies a respectable Buddhist-era account of how he got there. And the name is not a personal name at all. Great One of the Graveyard is a role. Jayasēna is a man the tradition placed in it.

The Tamil god wears the same kind of placed biography. His temple myth makes him Śiva’s son, born of the god’s burning lock, cursed from birth with a craving for corpse flesh. In one ballad line he runs straight from birth to the burning ground and feasts on the dead there; in another he is born of gathered sparks, *cuṭar*, “flame”; and among his forms the tradition counts Piṇamālaicūṭimāṭaṉ, “Māṭaṉ garlanded with corpses” (Reiniche 1979: 202; Arunachalam 1976: 191; Masilamani-Meyer 2004: 226). Two late origin stories, then, one Buddhist and one Śaiva, each explaining how a much older graveyard sovereign came to hold his post. Parallel stories are suggestive. What comes next is more than suggestive.

Go back to the Tamil shore, to Tirunelveli district itself, and listen to how that country tells the story of Dakṣa’s sacrifice. Vīrabhadra beheads Dakṣa. The head burns in the sacrificial fire. To revive him, a bull’s head (*kalaiyiṉ talai*) is fitted onto the corpse, and the revived hybrid is then commissioned as a divine military commander and sent down to earth, where he stands today as the district’s own guardian: Taḷavāy Māṭaṉ (Masilamani-Meyer 2004: 28–29).

A beheading. A lost head. An animal’s head pressed on. Revival. A commission as a spirit-commander. That is the Jayasēna story, move for move, told on the Tamil shore, about a Māṭaṉ.

And the bull’s head is not an accident of one telling. Eveline Masilamani-Meyer derives *māṭaṉ* itself from *māṭu*, “bull”: the Māṭaṉ gods of the far south are bull-headed (2004: 28, 226). Set them side by side, the bull-headed lord of the Tamil graveyards and the bear-headed lord of the Sinhala ones, and try saying coincidence again.

Still, myth parallels, however dense, are still myth. The next piece of evidence is a state archive.

## The king who built shrines for the demons



The Mahāvaṃsa, Laṅkā’s Buddhist chronicle, had every reason to bury the memory of what the island worshipped before Buddhism. Instead, it wrote the memory down. Chapter ten describes the reign of Paṇḍukābhaya of Anurādhapura, fourth century BCE, two generations before the first Buddhist missionary landed, and S. Paranavitana built the founding study of pre-Buddhist Sinhala religion on that one chapter in 1929. Here is what the king did.

He settled the yakkha Kāḷavela on the east side of his city, and the yakkha Cittarāja by the Abhaya tank. The chronicle has already told us who these two were. Citta the herdsman and Kāḷavela the slave were the prince’s own attendants, executed for saving his life when he was an infant, reborn as Yakkhas, who kept guard over him from the womb onward.

He raised an abode at the southern gate for Dāsi, the slave woman who had nursed him, now dead, reborn as a yakkhinī, thanked with a shrine.

Within the royal precincts he housed the mare-faced Cetiyā of Dhūmarakkha mountain, the wild, white-bodied, red-footed mare-yakkhinī he had captured and bridled, who became his adviser, and whom he rode in battle. Notice her name. *Cetiyā* is the caitya word, the word for the shrine itself, worn as a personal name. Remember that, because a Tamil goddess is about to do the same thing.

Vessavaṇa received a shrine in his banyan. The palmyrah deity received another. The Queen of the West took the west. And all of them were given sacrificial offerings, annually, at state expense. On festival days, the chronicle adds, the king sat with Cittarāja beside him on a seat of equal height, and Coomaraswamy pointed out that the chronicle’s remark that Cittarāja and Kāḷavela were visible means they were present as statues. A king of Laṅkā and the image of his deified dead herdsman, enthroned as equals at the state festival of the realm.

Now read the list again and see what it actually is. His herdsman. His slave. His nurse. His war-mare. Every named being in the state religion of pre-Buddhist Anurādhapura is a dead member of the king’s own story, installed at a shrine and fed by the kingdom. The chronicle even has a word for what such beings are: *yakkha-bhūta*, “those that had become Yakkhas.” On this island, becoming a yakkha is what the honored dead do.

The island never entirely forgot it. Its foundation myth calls the aboriginal inhabitants simply Yakkhas, and marries the settler-prince Vijaya to one of them, the yakkhinī Kuveṇī, whose children became the ancestors of the Pulinda. “Perhaps the Veddas,” Coomaraswamy wrote, “who are still worshippers of Yakkhas; perhaps as ancestors?” And twenty-three centuries after Paṇḍukābhaya, the ethnographer Parker recorded a villager’s dream in which a dead man announces, in four words, the entire religion this essay has been digging for:

**“I am now a Yakā.”**

That is the role Maha Sohona holds: chief of the honored dead, in a land where the dead become yakku. So who holds it on the other shore?

## The woman named Yakshi



The Tamil far south answers with a pair, and the second member of the pair carries the key in her own name.

In Cuṭalai Māṭaṉ’s districts, often inside his very shrine compounds, stands Icakki Ammaṉ (இசக்கி அம்மன்). Her legend is the same god-making machine running in a woman’s register: Nīli, wronged and murdered beside a cactus, dead, dangerous, avenging, and then ritually installed, pacified into a guardian mother, worshipped now with a child on her hip. The ethnography records her exactly as the old class of beings demands, fierce, fanged, “portrayed with a child in her hands and/or mouth,” the eater of children become their giver (Masilamani-Meyer 2004: 225, 208). Readers of Buddhist history will recognize that career instantly. It is Hārītī’s, to the letter.

Now her name. Say it backward through time: Tamil *Icakki/Iyakki*, from Prakrit *yakkhī*, from Sanskrit *yakṣī*.

Her name is the word *yakṣī*. The goddess of Kaṉṉiyākumari and Tirunelvēli is simply called the Yakshi, a Prakrit word left in Tamil soil during the Jain centuries (her old correspondence with the Jain yakṣī Ambikā, the mango-tree mother, marks the route) and wrapped around a goddess older than any of them. The island kept the same femininity. Coomaraswamy’s table of the word’s forms gives the Sinhala as *Yakā* for the male and Yakī for the female. Icakki and Yakī. Two shores hold the woman of this class by her class name, just as Cetiyā carries the shrine word and Mācāṉam carries the graveyard word. This religion names its beings after what they are, and the names match across the water, one for one.

Even her shrine says what she is. Icakki’s immemorial address is the milk-oozing pālkallu cactus, and the yakṣī, in Coomaraswamy’s reading, is above all the guardian of *rasa*, the sap of life that the texts call soma and amṛta. Her shrine bleeds milk. And when the Sanskrit story tradition wanted to meet a yakṣī, it knew where to look: the Kathāsaritsāgara’s rites for attracting yakṣīs are performed in cemeteries. The śmaśāna and the yakṣī belong together in the classics too.

So, on both shores: the same graveyard king under the same śmaśāna name, the same class-sister beside him under the same yakṣī name, the same feast around them, sung by the same hereditary drums. One question is left, and it is the deepest one. Where does a religion of the divine dead come from? Stop reading texts. Start digging.

## The earth underneath



From roughly 1200 to 300 BCE, the Tamil far south and Sri Lanka were a single archaeological province, and its signature is how it treated the dead: the urn burials of Ātichanallūr on the Tāmiraparaṇi, which is Cuṭalai’s own river, the cists and stone circles of Tamiḻakam, and their close cousins on the island at Pomparippu and Ibbankaṭuva (Seneviratne 1984; Rajan 2000). Iron weapons, pottery, and ornaments went into the urns. Equipment for people who were understood to continue. To need things. To watch.

The Tamil country even lets us watch what that mortuary religion became, because the oldest Tamil grammar caught it alive. The Tolkāppiyam lays out the hero-stone rite, the *naṭukal*, in six ordered stages: beholding the stone, taking it, bathing it, planting it, laying great offerings before it, praising it. That is not a funeral. That is an installation. It is the consecration of a dead man into a god, and the Caṅkam anthologies show the finished product receiving worship: stones garlanded, offered toddy and blood, spoken to directly, as Auvaiyār spoke to the stone of her dead patron Atiyamāṉ (Puṟanāṉūṟu 232). George Hart reconstructed the worldview around the rite, a world of *aṇaṅku*, dangerous immanent power managed by drum, possession, and offering. Stuart Blackburn named the engine that has run from that day to this: in the Tamil country, the dead, above all the violently dead, are made into guardian gods.

Now ask the question this whole essay has been walking toward. In a religion whose gods are the processed dead, where the megalith, the urn, and the planted stone are the machinery that makes divinity, what is the burial ground?

Not one sacred site among many. The capital. The place where gods are made. A capital needs a king, and both shores agree on his title because both inherited it from the same religion: the lord of the śmaśāna.

## Discussion



Before a conclusive summary, two objections deserve their day. Every culture fears its graveyard, so both shores could in principle have coined “the śmaśāna one” independently; and a real resemblance could still be recent, carried across the strait by medieval or colonial migration. But independent invention explains one correspondence comfortably and struggles with eight: name, role, class, sister goddess, hereditary drummers, sung ballads, possession, menu, and a beheading myth with an animal’s head on each shore. Late borrowing runs aground on the independent antiquity of each side, for Paṇḍukābhaya’s shrines predate any such contact by centuries, and the Tamil god-making tradition runs unbroken from the Iron Age urns to this year’s koṭai. Even Coomaraswamy’s famous reservation, read closely in his first volume, shrinks to the monuments alone; on the question that matters he testifies for our side, writing that in many cases yakṣas “have been human beings attached to the service of a community or individual, and, reborn as a spirit or geni, continue to watch over those whom they had formerly served” (1928: 14–15). So I hold the strict genetic claim as a working hypothesis rather than a settled fact, while noting what even the objections concede: on both shores, an ancient graveyard sovereign of the yakṣa class, served by the same old ritual technology, was taken over late by a classical religion and given a new life story.

The Galle district shows what sits beneath those stories. There the Berava exorcist does not revile Maha Sohona; he jokes with him, gambles with him, shares meat with him through the watches of the night until the patient rises well at dawn, dealing with the demon exactly as the Tamil coast deals with its kāval teyvam. The theology says demon, the practice says god, and the practice is older. It is older, in fact, than the long fall of the whole yakṣa class, which these two far shores alone escaped: the same beings once addressed as gods in the early Pali records, where the Buddha himself is called a yakkha in canonical poetry (Majjhima I.383), were ranked into the passionate middle of the Gītā’s hierarchy of worship (17.4), dragged into the underworld of the post-Vedic imagination as Sutherland showed, and finally carved into subjection, a dwarf under the brimming vase at Polonnaruva, the apasmāra crushed beneath Naṭarāja’s dancing foot. Across the subcontinent the first god of the place was renamed and danced upon. Here, he was not. Buddhism demoted the burning-ground king and Śaivism adopted him, but both arrived late to a court already ancient, and beneath the two stories the same night feast goes on: the hereditary drums, the sung ballads of birth and death, the possessed body, the meat and the arrack, the graveyard, the dawn. The Mahāvaṃsa keeps the older arrangement in the one image I would save if I could save only one: a king of Laṅkā at his state festival, seated beside the statue of his deified dead herdsman, on a seat of equal height. The living and the mighty dead at one table, with the lord of the burning ground presiding.

Step back, at the end, and look at what actually survived on the two shores because, as with every story this series tells, it is not what usually survives.

On the island, the old god’s divinity was taken from him. Buddhism filed him among the demons, wrote him a death, gave him a bear’s face and a warrant-holder for a warden. By any ordinary accounting he was destroyed. But run the other side, the graveyard is still his; the drums are still hereditary; the night-long feast still ends at dawn with the patient healed; and in Galle they never stopped treating him as what he was, a fierce friend. The classification changed, but the relationship survived.

On the Tamil coast, the direction ran the other way. Śaivism filed him among the gods, wrote him a divine birth, made the graveyard a paternal appointment. And underneath the promotion, nothing changed there either: the night-long feast, the meat, the arrack, the blood carried to the ashes, the possessed oracle, the potters’ and drummers’ hereditary role, the whole apparatus stands outside the Āgamas to this day, exactly as old as it looks.

Hinduism is layered like an onion. The outermost skin is the religion of the printed calendar and the granite gopuram: Āgamic liturgy, Purāṇic biography, the tidy pantheon of the textbooks. Peel it, and beneath lies the bhakti synthesis that adopted the village gods into divine families and gave the graveyard king a father. Peel again, and you reach the Śramaṇic centuries, Jain and Buddhist, which classified the old beings and left their words behind, a Prakrit yakkhī fossilized in a goddess’s name. Peel again, and you are holding the yakṣa religion itself, the worship of the mighty dead at tree, stone, and burning ground. And at the core there is no text at all. It is society itself in its oldest form: the living family and its honored dead, refusing to let go of one another.

He presides still. The theologies disagree about what he is. The drums, on both shores, have never changed their answer.
