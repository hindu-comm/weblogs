In the section on the [myth of Mahābali](https://naavalam.substack.com/i/211942994/who-was-mahabali-or-maveli), we began with the Purāṇic genealogy, then moved on to its attestation in Āḻvārs’ bhakti poems.

## Māvali Marumāṉ



In the Maṇimēkalai (மணிமேகலை), the Tamil Buddhist epic of Cīttalaic Cāttaṉār, canto 19 describes the Cōḻa prison guards reporting a miracle to the Cōḻa king, who is sauntering in a grove with his queen.

The poet wrote the scene like this:

> நெடியோன் குறள் உரு ஆகி நிமிர்ந்து தன்  
> அடியில் படியை அடக்கிய அந் நாள்  
> நீரின் பெய்த மூரி வார் சிலை  
> மாவலி மருமான் சீர் கெழு திரு மகள்  
> சீர்த்தி என்னும் திருத் தகு தேவியொடு  
> போது அவிழ் பூம்பொழில் புகுந்தனன் புக்குக்  
> கொம்பர்த் தும்பி குழல் இசை காட்டக்  
> பொங்கர் வண்டு இனம் நல் யாழ்செய்ய  
> வரிக் குயில் பாட மா மயில் ஆடும்  
> விரைப் பூம் பந்தர் கண்டு உளம் சிறந்தும்
>
> *neṭiyōṉ kuṟaḷ uru āki nimirntu taṉ  
> aṭiyil paṭiyai aṭakkiya an nāḷ  
> nīriṉ peyta mūri vār cilai  
> māvali marumāṉ cīr keḻu tiru makaḷ  
> cīrtti eṉṉum tirut taku tēviyoṭu  
> pōtu aviḻ pūmpoḻil pukuntaṉaṉ pukkuk  
> kompart tumpi kuḻal icai kāṭṭak  
> poṅkar vaṇṭu iṉam nal yāḻceyya  
> varik kuyil pāṭa mā mayil āṭum  
> viraip pūm pantar kaṇṭu uḷam ciṟantum*

Roughly translates to: On the day Neṭiyōṉ (Tamil name of Viṣṇu) took dwarf form and, rising, pressed the world beneath his foot, the day the earth was given with water poured. The Cōḻa king, Māvaṇkiḷḷi (மாவண்கிள்ளி), with his worthy queen, Cīrtti (சீர்த்தி), the glorious daughter of the descendant of Māvali, the one with the mighty long bow, entered the grove where bees on the branches made the music of the flute for him, swarms in the blossoms played the good yāḻ, the striped cuckoo sang and the great peacock danced, and seeing that fragrant bower his heart lifted.

Why is this significant? It is the first time the full Vāmana-Bali story, with the dwarf, the rising, the earth underfoot, the water of the gift, and Māvali named, is attested in Tamil literature.

U.V. Swaminatha Iyer's commentary precisely describes Cīrtti, properly Kīrtti, as the daughter of a king descended from Mahābali, whose name is unrecorded, who married into the Cōḻa house. And the epic's own catalog of dynasties runs to exactly three: the line of Indra, Sūryavanśha, and Māvali-kula. A Buddhist epic, probably written somewhere in the middle of the first millennium, before Āḻvārs made Ōṇam one of their festivities, documented the story of Vāmana-Mahābali, kept Mahābali's dynasty real and significant, and recounted how Cōḻa would enter into a marital relationship with the Māvali-kula princess. The prince Utayakumaraṉ (உதயகுமரன்), who pursues the epic's heroine through the whole story, is Bali's descendant on his mother's side.

A Cōḻa prince, literally a descendant of Māvali according to Maṇimēkalai. Let that sit for a moment. While we were discussing the myth all this time, one Cōḻa king proved it was as real as it gets.

Iyer further explains that the historical dynasty claiming descent from Mahābali is the Bāṇas, as noted by the epigraphist V. Venkayya. Maṇimēkalai keeps the names scrupulously apart when it praises Buddha, calling him Vāmaṉ, one who overcame Kāma, while the dwarf god is always Neṭiyōṉ.

Who were the Bāṇas who claimed descent from Mahābali?

## Bāṇas



Venkayya's footnote opens the longest missing chapter of all, because the Mahābali genealogy was not a poet's flourish.

The Bāṇas were a long-lived and attested dynasty from the borderlands of Tamil Nadu and Andhra from the early fourth century. They ruled in the hill-and-scrub county between Palar and Kolar, the Chithoor region. In Tamil records, their region is attested as Perumbāṇappāḍi (பெரும்பாணப்பாடி). The famous Paraśurāmeśvara temple at Gudimallam is located here. Over the centuries, they survived among the dominant powers of this region, starting with the Pallavas, the Gangas, and the Rāṣṭrakūṭas. They reached prominence in the ninth century, but Parāntaka I ended it, as the Udayendiram plates record.

They identified themselves as Mahābali kulōdbhava, born of Mahābali’s lineage, and titled themselves Bāṇādhirāja; in Tamil, they are known as Māvali Vāṇarāyar. Their lineage is traced to Bāṇāsura, the son of Mahābali. Post Cōḻa’s dominance, Cōḻa conferred the title Bāṇādhirāja and the Bāṇa lordship on his Ganga ally, Pṛthivīpati II Hastimalla. The name became a transferable dignity, detachable from blood, which the Cōḻas carried forward. A thirteenth-century record mentions a chief called Māvali Vāṇātirāyaṉ and Vāṇakōvaraiyar of the Magadai. The use of Vāṇātirāyaṉ as chief’s title persisted through the Vijayanagara and Nāyaka periods, but whether the later bearer descended from the original Bāṇas is a question.

The practice of claiming descent from Mahābali continued long after the original dynasty of Perumbāṇappāḍi had vanished; whether they were truly descendants of Bali or simply following the common royal custom of tracing lineage to Solar, Lunar, or Ikshvakhu lines, we cannot be sure. But the practice extends into modern times; a record from Rājarāja’s period mentions a Maṟavaṉ Narasiṅga-paṉmaṉ known as Rājarāja Vāṇakōvaraiyar. Another record praises him as Balikula Tilakam; a stone inscription from 992 CE names a Mummuṭiccōḻa Vāṇakōvaraiyaṉ (Toṅkala Maṟavaṉ). An eighth-century hero-stone from Dharmapuri documents a Māvali Vāṇarāyar. As recently as a 1911 land deed from the Sankarankovil country, the parties registered themselves as Māveli Vāṇā kulattu Maṟavar jāti, the Maṟavar caste of Māvēli’s Vāṇa line. We can’t be certain whether the claims about Mahābali’s lineage through Bāṇa are true or a myth propagated by the progenitors of the Bāṇa dynasty.

—

*The Maṇimēkalai's opening canto has the Indra festival at Pukār running nāl ēḻ nāḷ, four sevens of days, twenty-eight days. Kunjan Pillai reconstructed a twenty-eight-day imperial Ōṇam at Tṛkkākara. And Ōṇāṭṭukara still keeps Irupattiyettām Ōṇam, the twenty-eighth Ōṇam. Three twenty-eights is probably a shared convention for long royal festivals rather than a genetic link, but it deserves a sentence.*
