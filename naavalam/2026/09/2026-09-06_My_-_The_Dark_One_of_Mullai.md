Tonight, somewhere after midnight, a conch will sound in a temple in Mathurā, and a small, dark infant will be bathed in milk, because this is the night Kṛṣṇa was born in a prison cell on the Yamunā. Janmāṣṭamī is a thoroughly northern festival, rooted in the geography of Mathurā, Vṛndāvana, and Gokula, the cowherd country of Braj. Instinctively, everyone would treat the South as a late recipient of Kṛṣṇa’s birth story and regard the spread of Kṛṣṇa’s tradition to the Indian peninsula, among Sanskrit-speaking communities, as a mature cult.

Philology tells a different story. The earliest substantial devotional literature about Kṛṣṇa was not a Sanskrit text or an inscription, but Tamil poetry that loves and adores him. Before that poetry, the earliest Tamil literary tradition had already given him a place in its grammatical tradition.

Kṛṣṇa, known as Māyōṉ to the people of Tamiḻakam, some two thousand years ago.

## Where it all started in North Mathurā



To understand what distinguishes the Tamil attestations, we must first examine the northern chronology of Kṛṣṇa.

The earliest mention of Kṛṣṇa appears in a verse from the Chāndogya Upaniṣad, which names*Kṛṣṇa Devakīputra,*Kṛṣṇa*, the son of*Devakī. Here,*Kṛṣṇa*is described as a pupil of Ghora Āṅgirasa.

> *tad dṛtaitāgāra ghora āṅgirasaḥ kṛṣṇāya devakīputrāya uktvā vāca
> apipāsa eva sa babhūva \| so ‘ntavelāyām etat trayaṃ pratipadyeta
> akṣitam asy acyutam asi prāṇasaṃśitam asīti \|\|*
>
> *When Ghora Āṅgirasa, after having communicated this to Kṛṣṇa, the son
> of Devakī, he also said, as he had become free from desire, in the
> final hour one should take refuge in these three thoughts: thou art
> the indestructible, thou art the unshaken, thou art the very essence
> of life.*

Whether this Kṛṣṇa is the later Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa remains genuinely uncertain; scholars have argued for both possibilities.

The earliest clear evidence of Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa worship comes from the grammarian Pāṇini, conventionally dated to the fourth century BCE. The Aṣṭādhyāyī includes rule 4.3.98, which gives the affix used to form the word for a devotee of Vāsudeva, along with Arjuna (*vāsudevārjunabhyāṃ vun**vāsudevakaḥ, arjunakaḥ*). This probably means that, in Pāṇini’s time, Vāsudeva was regarded as a god, and his followers were called Vāsudevakaḥ. The very next sūtra, 4.3.99 (*gotrakṣatriyākhyebhyo bahulaṃ vuñ*), already provides an affix for names of gotras and kṣatriyas. Patañjali, commenting on Pāṇini two hundred years later, explicitly states that Vāsudeva is not merely a kṣatriya name but a designation of the worshipful one (*Bhagavat*). The theistic reading offered by Patañjali is secure only in the Mahābhāṣya era. Bhandarkar's (1913) argument that Vāsudeva-bhakti dates to roughly the fourth century BCE should be taken with a grain of salt. In another sūtra (4.1.114), Pāṇini mentions Vṛṣṇi (*ṛṣyandhakavṛṣṇikurubhyaś ca*) and refers to them as kṣatriyas, along with the Andhakas (*andhaka-vṛṣṇiṣu).*In the Arthaśāstra 1.6.10, Kauṭilya identifies the clan as a*saṅgha*, *vṛṣṇisaṃgha,*marked by the near-ungovernable factionalism of the Vṛṣṇi-Andhaka saṅgha (*rājaśabdopajīvin*).

> *harṣād vātāpir agastyam atyāsādayan, vṛṣṇisaṃghaś ca dvaipāyanam iti*
>
> *Through harṣa, Vātāpi assailing Agastya perished and the saṅgha of
> the Vṛṣṇis, assailing Dvaipāyana.*

Megasthenes, the Seleucid ambassador to the Mauryan court around 300 BCE, reports that the Śūrasenas (Sourasenoi) of the Mathurā region held Herakles in special honor and named their city Methora. Another city, recorded by the Greeks as Kleisobora and read by scholars as Kṛṣṇapura, may preserve the god’s own name in the fragment if that reading is correct. Identifying Herakles of Mathurā as Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa is an inference, but an honest alternative reading could also refer to the club-bearer and plow-wielder, Baladeva-Saṃkarṣaṇa, one among the Vṛṣṇi pantheon (many scholars favor Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa, while others prefer Baladeva-Saṃkarṣaṇa). For our article, the rivalry hardly matters, whether the Greeks were looking at the dark one or the white one. They were looking in the right place, at the right time: the Vṛṣṇi cult.

> ...  
> ya\[s\] toṣāyāḥ śailaṁ śrimadgr̥ham atulam udadha samadhāra ...  
> ārcādeśāṁ śailām paṁca jvalata iva paramavapuṣā ...*
>
> *– Mora Well Inscription, 1st century CE*

The earlier depiction of the Vrsnis as a triad, found in a rock shelter at Tikla, south of Gwalior on the old Mathurā–Ujjain route, was read by Neumayer and Gupta. It shows Balarāma-Saṃkarṣaṇa holding *halāyudha* in his right hand and a barbed, harpoon-like object in his left; Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa in the middle holding a barbed, harpoon-like object with a hooded snake in his right hand and a cakra in his left; and the female figure Ekānaṃśā holding the umbrella.

A Greek ambassador named Heliodorus erected a Garuḍa pillar in honor of Vāsudeva and called himself a*bhāgavata*, a devotee. These Greek witnesses belong to the same early horizon as the first clear traces of Vṛṣṇi worship in India. Two Greek witnesses, a king’s mint and a diplomat’s pillar, remain among the strongest deflationary facts against any tidy story of Hinduism as ethnically bounded. Around the same time, inscriptions from Ghosundi and Hathibada record a stone enclosure for the worship of the Vṛṣṇi duo, named in the order of Saṃkarṣaṇa and Vāsudeva

> *(This) enclosing wall round the stone (object) of worship, called
> Narayana-vatika (Compound) for the divinities Saṃkarṣaṇa-Vāsudeva who

In the first century BCE, the cave inscription of the Sātavāhana queen Nāgaṇikā at Nānāghāt in the western Deccan begins with an invocation that mentions Saṃkarṣaṇa and Vāsudeva, the duo’s earliest datable appearance on the peninsula. The trajectory does not end with the Sātavāhanas; by the late third or early fourth century CE, the Kondamottu panel in coastal Andhra depicts the five Vṛṣṇi heroes in genealogical order.

A striking asymmetry in the journey of Saṃkarṣaṇa-Vāsudeva to the south is that the only temples in India where the Vṛṣṇi group still stands enshrined and is worshipped today are in the Tamil country, at Tiruvallikkeni, Tirunaraiyur, and Tiruttangal. The paper trail that begins at a Greek mint ends, alive, in Tamil sanctums.

The North gave birth to Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa of the Vṛṣṇi**saṅgha and to the evolution of Pañca-Vīras into the peninsula, yet for centuries, devotional bhakti literature and the poems in which humans address Kṛṣṇa with viraha, a sense of longing, did not appear.The Harivaṃśa and the Viṣṇu Purāṇa narrate what happened around him, while the Gītā philosophizes about him. The emotion of viraha, expressed in poems addressed to the cowherd—the mode we now regard as the very essence of Kṛṣṇa bhakti, appeared elsewhere for the first time.

## Where He Rests in South Mathurā



The Tamiḻ country described its lifestyle in five types based on topography. Along with the totemistic and animistic religious character reflected in Old Tamiḻ Sangam literature, some of the earliest references to the principal Hindu deities, such asMāyōṉ*,* Murukaṉ, andKoṟṟavai*, are*found in Sangam literature, usually dated from 300 BCE to 300 CE. The Tolkāppiyam, the foundational treatise on Tamil grammar and poetics, presents in Poruḷatikāram the concept of the five*tiṇai*, the five landscapes that organize all Tamil love poetry.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!zOMu!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd3923180-8df6-49b0-b40c-2c6f79dd9925_1792x1008.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!zOMu!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd3923180-8df6-49b0-b40c-2c6f79dd9925_1792x1008.png)





Each tiṇai mentioned is an ecosystem, a mood, a phase of love, and each has a presiding deity. Among the five tiṇais, the one that matters most to us is Mullai, the forested pastoral land of cattle-herders, the landscape of patient longing, which belongs to Māyōṉ, whose literal meaning is “the dark one.” The verse goes as this:

> மாயோன் மேய காடுறை உலகமும்  
> சேயோன் மேய மைவரை உலகமும்  
> வேந்தன் மேய தீம்புனல் உலகமும்  
> வருணன் மேய பெருமணல் உலகமும்  
> முல்லை குறிஞ்சி மருதம் நெய்தல் எனச்  
> சொல்லிய முறையான் சொல்லவும் படுமே
>
> *māyōṉ mēya kāṭuṟai ulakamum  
> cēyōṉ mēya maivarai ulakamum  
> vēntaṉ mēya tīmpuṉal ulakamum  
> varuṇaṉ mēya perumaṇal ulakamum  
> mullai kuṟiñci marutam neytal eṉac  
> colliya muṟaiyāṉ collavum paṭumē  
>   
> The world of forest dwellings where Māyōṉ abides, the world of dark
> mountains where Cēyōṉ abides, the world of sweet waters where Vēntaṉ
> abides, the world of wide sands where Varuṇaṉ abides, in the order
> stated, these are called mullai, kuṟiñci, marutam, neytal.  
>   
> — Tolkāppiyam, Poruḷatikāram, Akattiṇai iyal*

Before any Tamil hymn was dedicated to Māyōṉ, the Old Tamil grammatical tradition portrayed him as the presiding deity of the pastoral lands and cattle-herders, much like Kṛṣṇa Gopāla in the northern imagination. The name itself means “the black-colored one”; Māyōṉ, like its cognates Māl and Tirumāl, belongs to a Dravidian semantic field (DEDR 4781) of darkness:*mā “darkness,” māy-*“to grow dark, vanish,” and*māl*“darkness, cloud.”Just as Sanskrit *kṛṣṇa* is simply the adjective “black, dark.”

The dating of the Tolkāppiyam remains a notorious quagmire; its layers likely span several centuries, and the Poruḷatikāram’s deity verse cannot be dated with confidence. But that is beside the point of this article; the framework's independence is more crucial to our argument than whether Tolkāppiyam predates Pāṇini. The Tamil tradition does not present Māyōṉ as an import requiring explanation, but rather keeps him within the ecosystem of Old Tamil and imagines him as part of Tamil society. He was part of the foundational shaping of how the language thought about Tamiḻakam.

Paripāṭal (பரிபாடல்), one of the eight anthologies in the Sangam corpus and usually dated to the later Sangam period, the third to fifth century CE, is where the narrative first shows Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa. Eight poems (1, 2, 3, 4, 13, 15) survive as full-dress hymns to Tirumāl (திருமால்) and his mythological stories, and they are remarkable and unmistakable. These are the earliest sustained devotional hymns in Tamil, and arguably in any language, to Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa/Viṣṇu. The most famous hymn addresses Tirumāl in a litany that calls him*nī*(நீ), the first-person singular that dissolves the god into everything and everything into god.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!LC4S!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F04d75d88-8c5b-4492-b09a-bd5ffc5191ac_1792x1008.png)





Compare Tamil here with the Besnagar pillar’s terse*devadevasa.*That contrast is the heart of this article: the inscription merely declares, while Tamil adores him. Let’s look at the grammar, because grammar is the theology here. Each line begins with the small Tamil word*uḷ**(உள்),*“within, inside”; the poet never says you are the fire, and without that, it would read like any other poem with a pantheistic undertone. Fire is something you can see and feel, but the burning within the burning, the inner core that makes fire fire, is invisible, and the poet says that is you. The whole hymn is this repeated movement; Tirumāl is not on the surface, but hidden, invisible, the essence behind everything. Note the pronoun the poet uses,*nī,*the intimate singular; it is not a distant honorific, but the “you” addressed to a child, a friend, or a lover lying close enough to caress.

Now unravel the world behind each line, burning within fire, the god who drank the forest fire at Bhāṇḍīra to save the herds, heat hidden in heat. Then immediately it’s *pūviṉuḷ nāṟṟam nī*, the fragrance within the flower. A Tamil ear hearing this hymn would immediately ask, “Which flower?” The poet stands in the mullai lands, where mullai jasmine grows abundantly and waits to be worn by the*āy (ஆய்)*kula girls, just as Hāla would call Radha Rāhiāi in his Sattasaī.At the end, the poet adds a philosophical line that might seem pantheistic: you are all things (*aṉaittum nī*), yet he adds a punch with you are the inner meanings, or anything and everything (*aṉaittiṉ uṭporuḷum nī)*.The word uṭporuḷ carries a lot of weight here, because this is one of the key grammatical doctrines of the Sangam corpus: uḷḷuṟai (உள்ளுறை), the hidden meanings the poet conceals within the traits of the landscape, the unstated love-truth implied by a heron, the paddy, the rain, etc. It is an akam poem; fire, jasmine, and moonlight are its imagery, and Tirumāl is the uḷḷuṟai, the beloved meaning the entire poem is secretly about. A pillar far away in Rajasthan can declare Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa as the god of gods to the world, but only a lover would think to look for him inside a jasmine flower, and the deeper you breathe, the more it smells of Kaṇṇaṉ, as the Tamil world lovingly calls him.

Another hymn from Paripāṭal worth mentioning is not about the god himself, but about where to find him in the southern country. The poet begins with the hills beloved by the gods, and the one dear to Tirumāl, where he took up residence, was Māliruṅkuṉṟam (மாலிருங்குன்றம்). People in Madurai would know it by heart, since this hill is none other than Tirumāliruñcōlai (திருமாலிருஞ்சோலை), the Aḻakar hill (அழகர் கோயில்) northeast of Madurai, the southern Mathurā. This is one of the Vaiṣṇava temples, still functioning and bustling with devotees, and processing him down to the Vaikai every Cittirai. From the Sangam poets' time to the Āḻvārs’ era, Māliruñcōlai remained a beloved place for morning worship.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!zOmj!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8472737c-7da5-4b28-9f9b-6d72bd22651d_1792x1008.png)





Moving on to who lives there, it was not just Tirumāl but also his elder brother Vāliyōṉ, like the word and its meaning (*colluṁ poruḷum*). Everything we traced from north Mathurā, through the Agathocles coins, to the inscription and epic of Ghosundi's paired brothers, and the Ādi Parva's white and black hairs, stands tall here on a named Tamil hill in south Mathurā, sharing one shrine and praised in a single breath. In the same passage, the poet plays with words:*puvvat tāmarai puraiyum kaṇṇaṉ,*which can be read as “the lotus-eyed one,” using the word*kaṇ*with the masculine suffix -*aṉ,*while writing it exactly as the Tamil name of Kṛṣṇa, Kaṇṇaṉ, as the Tamil world later calls him.

## When South Mathurā Loved and Longed Him



Before Āṇṭāḷ herself woke Nappiṉṉai (Rādhā) and asked her to rouse her lord, by the 8th-9th centuries CE, in Tiruppāvai, the Sangam corpus *talaivi* (the heroine) had begun to see herself through the lens of her *talaivaṉ* (the hero). The vocabulary of viraha-bhakti begins to take shape in Kalittokai poems through longing expressed in dairy and cattle, the beloved and the dark god sharing a single gaze, and women's bodies as the site of devotion-shaped love.

> விடிந்த பொழுதினும் இல் வயின் போகாது,  
> கொடுந் தொழுவினுள் பட்ட கன்றிற்குச் சூழும்  
> கடுஞ்சூல் ஆ நாகு போல், நின் கண்டு நாளும்  
> நடுங்கு அஞர் உற்றது என் நெஞ்சு.
>
> *viṭinta poḻutiṉum il vayiṉ pōkātu,  
> koṭun toḻuviṉuḷ paṭṭa kaṉṟiṟkuc cūḻum  
> kaṭuñcūl ā nāku pōl, niṉ kaṇṭu nāḷum  
> naṭuṅku añar uṟṟatu eṉ neñcu.*
>
> *On seeing you, my heart trembles in fear daily like a young cow who
> gave birth to her calf and does not leave the curved stable in the
> house to graze at daybreak.*

In Kalittokai (110), separation-anguish (*añar*), a trembling body (*naṭuṅku*) brought on by separation, is imagined through a cow parted from her calf. This is precisely the image complex that Kṛṣṇa tradition will later canonize: the cows at Gokula lowing after the absent flute-player, the Bhāgavata’s herds refusing grass, and the emotional physiology of the gopī here in a mature form, in a cowherd’s love-plaint, waiting only for the divine addressee.

> மத்தம் பிணித்த கயிறு போல் நின் நலம்  
> சுற்றிச் சுழலும் என் நெஞ்சு
>
> *mattam piṇitta kayiṟu pōl niṉ nalam  
> cuṟṟic cuḻalum eṉ neñcu*
>
> *Fearing, confused every day, and not staying with me, my heart whirls
> around your beauty like a rope moving around a churning rod.*

When talaivaṉ expresses his anguish, he is confused, and his heart swirls like a rope around the dasher. A few lines later, his distress deepens, and he compares his life to something pitiful and useless, like milk churned with no butter to show for it (*ney kaṭai pāliṉ payaṉ yātum iṉṟu)*.Viraha appears as the restless, swirling churn of the dairy metaphor around the churn-rope that binds Dāmodara. A few centuries later, when Āṇṭāḷ's gopīs come begging for the*paṟai*amid butter and churning, this is the idiom they inherit.

This is the interesting part: the scene is set; imagine a scene from two thousand years ago, *talaivi* and her friend (*tōḻi*) watching the bull-baiting and discussing the scenes exactly as the people of Madurai or any other town in Tamil Nadu where the annual bull-baiting (ஏரு தழுவுதல் ēru taḻuvutal) is happening during the Poṅkal (பொங்கல்) season. They discuss how young men move around the bulls, standing face-to-face with them, holding the intestines from its horns with pride. The friend shares details of how a son of buffalo herders leaps and holds on without letting go of a rough bull’s neck as it struggles with desire, extols how a son of cow herders sits on a spotted bull and rides it, appearing like a boat moving in shallow water, and is enthused by how a son of goat herders holds firmly to the side of a beautiful white bull with tiny spots that look like dark spots on the moon.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Szld!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F029300cc-8d6c-483d-9433-1c812f461b0a_1792x1008.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Szld!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F029300cc-8d6c-483d-9433-1c812f461b0a_1792x1008.jpeg)





Her heart trembles at the sight of a herder wearing a garland of kāyā flowers as he attacks a red bull that stampedes against him with unceasing force, clinging to its ears. Here the scene shifts; she says it seems like Māyōṉ is splitting the mouth of the maned horse, Keśī, sent by his enemies.

> …இன்னன் கொல் மாயோன் என்று உட்கிற்று என் நெஞ்சு
>
> *…iṉṉaṉ kol māyōṉ eṉṟu uṭkiṟṟu eṉ neñcu*
>
> *…appearing like Thirumāl who split the mouth of a maned horse sent by
> his enemies*

This is exactly the transfer mechanism captured as it happens: a woman’s erotic admiration and religious awe, the beloved seen through the god. Viraha-bhakti is the same, but imagined in reverse, where the devotee sees the god through the beloved.

It doesn’t stop with*tōḻi*or*talaivi:*the hero,*talaivaṉ*,**calls her woman*mayil eruttu vaṇṇattu māyōy (மயில் எருத்து வண்ணத்து மாயோய்)*“O dark one, colored like a peacock’s neck,” courting a girl with god’s own name, and to win her, he swears to touch the feet of the mountain-chested lord, Thirumāl.There is no difference between love speech and god speech; both are in the same dialect.

What is Rāsalīlā without reimagining a scene from the Kuravai Kūttu (குரவைக் கூத்து) in south Mathurā, two thousand years ago? Āycciyar women clasping hands in a circle, dancing to sung refrains with a distinctive ululating vocal trill, role-playing as Māyavaṉ and Piṉṉai centuries before the Bhāgavata Purāṇa canonized it. Before Kṛṣṇa and Rādhā danced among the gopīkas of Vraj in Vṛndāvana, Āycciyar women and men were performing Āycciyar Kuravai some 2,000 km south of Mathurā 2000 years ago.

> முயங்கிப் பொதிவேம், முயங்கிப் பொதிவேம்,  
> முலை வேதின் ஒற்றி முயங்கிப் பொதிவேம்  
> கொலை ஏறு சாடிய புண்ணை, எம் கேளே!
>
> *muyaṅkip potivēm, muyaṅkip potivēm,  
> mulai vētiṉ oṟṟi muyaṅkip potivēm  
> kolai ēṟu cāṭiya puṇṇai, em kēḷē!*
>
> *Let us embrace and cover their wounds! O friend! Let’s embrace with
> our warm breasts and cover the wounds of our lovers, caused by
> murderous bulls!*

Now, back to the Mullaikkali, the bull-baiting scene is nearing its climax, and the wounded men are falling one by one. In the Kuravai, the Āycciyar women collectively claim to take the hurt into their bodies as balm. Tenderness, eros, and communal performance came together. Centuries later, in the Nappiṉṉai verse of Āṇṭāḷ’s Tiruppāvai, the lord Māyavaṉ sleeps on her breast; this is the continuity of viraha-bhakti. The Āycciyar’s breast, once the hero’s place of healing, becomes the god’s resting place.

The Mullaikkali opens under the rubric of what the tradition itself calls patient waiting: the rains, the jasmine, bull-baiting, and the appointed return. Some 600 to 800 years later, everything the Āḻvārs do with their poetic words describing the evening laments, the messenger poems, and the mother-speaking-for-the-lovesick-daughter poems is the full display of Mullai tiṇai's repertoire, with Kaṇṇaṉ imagined as the absent talaivaṉ.

Simply put, the entire Mullaikkali (முல்லைக் கலி) is absolute cinema!

### When South Mathurā Danced Him



If the Mullaikkali is absolute cinema, the 17th canto of the Cilappatikāram is its tragic feature film, set in Madurai itself.

Kaṇṇaki traveled a long way with her husband Kōvalaṉ to Madurai, hoping to start a new life together; aren’t we all in need of a second chance in life? They stayed temporarily at Mātari’s house in the cowherd quarter of the city. Āycciyars, a herdswoman sheltering Kaṇṇaki. The omens have gone wrong in the most domestic way imaginable: the curds will not set, the butter will not come, and the bulls tremble in their stalls. A herdswoman reads disaster the way a herdswoman would, through the dairy, and her answer to gathering evil is not a priest or a sacrifice, but a dance.

Āycciyar Kuravai (ஆய்ச்சியர் குரவை)

The Āycciyar girls of Madurai form a circle, join hands, and begin role-playing. One girl dances as Māyavaṉ (மாயவன்), the Māyōṉ himself; another as his brother, Vāliyōṉ (வாலியோன்), Balarāma himself. Finally, the last one dresses as Piṉṉai (பின்னை), the beloved, favored of Māyōṉ. Round the circle they sing, dance, clasp hands, and refrain and refrain the deeds of Māyōṉ. Māyōṉ isn’t discussed, only his deeds: the pot dance, the butter, the seven bulls he had to tame to win Piṉṉai’s hand, and the serpent humbled in the river. He is being danced into the room by teenage Āycciyar girls, who have been summoned to deal with the emergency.

One poem deserves discussion because it contains an important theology of Kṛṣṇa bhakti in the future. The cosmic power caught and held by the love of Yaśodā in her kitchen, centuries before the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, the same wonder under the name Dāmodara:

> வடவரையை மத்தாக்கி வாசுகியை நாணாக்கி  
> கடல்வண்ணன் பண்டொருநாள் கடல்வயிறு கலக்கினையே  
> கலக்கிய கை அசோதையார் கடைகயிற்றால் கட்டுண்கை
>
> *vaṭavaraiyai mattākki vācukiyai nāṇākki*  
> *kaṭalvaṇṇaṉ paṇṭorunāḷ kaṭalvayiṟu kalakkiṉaiyē*  
> *kalakkiyakai acōtaiyār kaṭaikayiṟṟāl kaṭṭuṇkai*
>
> *Making the northern mountain his churning-stick and Vāsuki his rope,
> the sea-hued one once churned the very belly of the ocean and that
> churning hand was bound by Yaśodā’s dairy-rope.*

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!QamM!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff7d1ab94-860a-4d24-bff5-5af016ff7470_1792x1008.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!QamM!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff7d1ab94-860a-4d24-bff5-5af016ff7470_1792x1008.png)





In our Mullaikkali section, we discussed our talaivaṉ, the hero, whose heart spun as the rope lashed the churning staff. Here, one epic later, the same rope tied the god himself through the love of his mother. The poet is, in a way, chuckling at Kṛṣṇa, saying that the hand that churned the ocean of milk was once tied with a cowherd mother’s butter cord through the songs of the āycciyar women. Kṛṣṇa doesn’t get mad, because he loves it and smiles back at them with love. The framework, tone, and everything else in the inherited repertoire of the*tiṇai*tradition. Without ever raising its voice, the irony is in the dance, hoping to turn away an evil omen that won’t turn. This will be the day Kōvalaṉ meets his demise in the same city.

Piṉṉai, Nappiṉṉai (நப்பின்னை), is Kaṇṇaṉ’s beloved in Tamil literary sources. She was the one for whom Kaṇṇaṉ had to subdue the bulls to win her hand, and his partner in the kuravai dance. Was there a Nappiṉṉai in the northern tradition? The Harivaṃśa does not mention Nappiṉṉai or anyone like her, and the Viṣṇu Purāṇa does not name her either. Even the Bhāgavata, the text Hardy argued was composed within earshot of Tamil, never names her. Rādhā, the woman whom the whole world now seats beside Kṛṣṇa, appears in datable Sanskrit only centuriesafterPiṉṉai is dancing along Kṛṣṇa in Madurai.A beloved Kṛṣṇa, fondly remembered as Māyōṉ in the Tamil sphere, is completely absent from early Sanskrit sources. This is definitely not just a case of receiving a god from the north, but of nativizing him into the fabric of its own culture and writing his love story for him.

## When South Mathurā had Viraha-Bhakti for Him



The Tamil tradition never let Piṉṉai go; she became the ever-longing āyar woman, patiently waiting for her Māyavaṉ, once a core trait of the Mullai land. The Śrīvaiṣṇavas enthroned her as Nīḷā Devī, a third consort alongside Śrī Devī and Bhū Devī during the Bhakti period. When Āṇṭāḷ, one of the Āḻvārs, composed Tiruppāvai, the āyar girls knew exactly whose door they should knock on. Not the god’s but hers:

> குத்து விளக்கெரியக் கோட்டுக்கால் கட்டில்மேல்  
> மெத்தென்ற பஞ்ச சயனத்தின் மேலேறி  
> கொத்தலர் பூங்குழல் நப்பின்னை கொங்கைமேல்  
> வைத்துக் கிடந்த மலர் மார்பா! வாய்திறவாய்!  
> மைத் தடங்கண்ணினாய், நீ உன் மணாளனை  
> எத்தனை போதும் துயிலெழ வொட்டாய் காண்  
> எத்தனையேலும் பிரிவாற்ற கில்லாயால்  
> தத்துவமன்று தகவேலோ ரெம்பாவாய்.
>
> *kuttu viḷakkeriyak kōṭṭukkāl kaṭṭilmēl  
> metteṉṟa pañca cayaṉattiṉ mēlēṟi  
> kottalar pūṅkuḻal nappiṉṉai koṅkaimēl  
> vaittuk kiṭanta malar mārpā! vāytiṟavāy!  
> mait taṭaṅkaṇṇiṉāy, nī uṉ maṇāḷaṉai  
> ettaṉai pōtum tuyileḻa voṭṭāy kāṇ  
> ettaṉaiyēlum pirivāṟṟa killāyāl  
> tattuvamaṉṟu takavēlō rempāvāy*
>
> *While the standing lamp burns bright, upon the couch with its legs of
> ivory, mounted on the soft five-layered bed, resting on the breast of
> Nappiṉṉai, her flower-clustered hair in bloom — O flower-chested lord,
> open your lips and speak!*
>
> *And you, lady of the wide, kohl-darkened eyes at no hour, it seems,
> will you let your bridegroom rise; not for the briefest moment can you
> bear him from your side. This suits neither your nature nor your grace
> — ēl ōr empāvāy.*

The first half of the poem is a petition: Māyavaṉ lies asleep on the breasts of Nappiṉṉai, while the āyar girls ask him to speak. Then the poem shifts, and the āyar girls turn on her in a teasing tone, accusing the beloved āyar girl Nappiṉṉai of keeping the lord to herself. The last line carries the weight of the whole poem; they tell her to her face that he is not your*tattuvam, or takavu.* Śrīvaiṣṇavas will later canonize it as*puruṣakāra,*with the consort as mediatrix, through whom alone the devotee reaches the god himself.

Above, we discussed how, in Mullaikkali, the Āycciyar women vow to embrace the wounded heroes from bull-taming with their breasts, making the women’s bodies places of healing. In the Tiruppāvai, the lord of the universe sleeps on Nappiṉṉai’s breast, now the god’s resting place, and the girls must negotiate with her to reach him. The heroes of the Mullai landscape in the Sangam period were later replaced by Māyavaṉ. The framework has not changed, but the theology has, and this may be one important genealogy for viraha-bhakti toward Kṛṣṇa.

### When Both Mathurās Sing Him Home



So where was Kṛṣṇa bhakti born?

Kṛṣṇa was born in the North, a Vṛṣṇi hero of a fractious saṅgha, and emerged as Bhagavat sometime during Pāṇini’s time and Heliodorus’s pillar. His journey began with the Greeks and the painted ochre image at Tikla. Traders from Bharukaccha took them all the way to Egypt, probably grew with the Sātavāhanas and Ābhīras as their own, and eventually in the Tamil lands.

The one we all love was born, or at least raised, in the South, in a grammatical tradition that wove together landscape, flora, fauna, and, most importantly, the gods. He was set as Māyōṉ of the pastoral lands, a cattle-breeding herdsman by trade, where the āyar girl waited through the rains in the kuravai circle of Madurai’s cowherd quarter. The North built him a theology, and the South taught it to weep, tease, wait, express anguish, love, and dance. It made him feel native, a home, and a mood. The Akam repertoire gave him a cast in which every talaivi, aching through the jasmine season, was already a waiting gopī. The doctrine of uḷḷuṟai uvamam (உள்ளுறை உவமம்) gave bhakti its deepest hermeneutic. If a Sangam poem’s landscape secretly means the beloved, the Paripāṭal’s leap was to read the entire universe as one akam poem. When the gopīs later weep for the absent flute-player in the Bhāgavata, Hardy heard them weeping with a Tamil accent. This remains contested scholarship, not yet settled, but after a stroll through the land of Mullaikkali in southern Mathurā, you can understand why he might have heard it.

And Rādhā?

That’s for another day and another thread.

Tamil tradition says that anyone who reads the Tiruvācakam, the Śaiva bhakti hymns, will melt, even if no one has ever melted them before. It’s true, Māṇikkavācakar will have you in tears by the second verse. What I would leave readers with today is this: read the Sangam poems first, and then the poems Tamil poured out for Kaṇṇaṉ-Tirumāl, the young cow circling her penned calf at daybreak, the heart spinning like the rope on a churning-staff, the Āycciyar pressing their warmth to their heroes’ wounds, Āṇṭāḷ’s girls at dawn arguing theology with the woman the god is sleeping on, I guarantee that you come away with something more than the melting the Tiruvācakam would do.

This is where the whole country learned what love is and developed it, as it celebrated him every year on the day he was born. He is imagined as the child who stole the butter, as the youth who playfully stole the clothes from the *āyar* girls, as the young adult every girl would ache to win over, and as the*sārathi*everyone enjoys listening to as he spills pearls of life lessons.

Ever wonder why that is?
