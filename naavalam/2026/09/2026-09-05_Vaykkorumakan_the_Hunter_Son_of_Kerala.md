# Vēṭṭaykkorumakan, the Hunter Son of Kerala

Every child in Kerala who has sat through a night of kaḷampāṭṭu (കളമ്പാട്ടു) knows how the Mahābhārata’s Vanaparvan ends. The story goes like this: Arjuna ascends the Himālaya to win the Pāśupatāstra weapon from Śiva through austerity. Śiva comes down to test him, disguised as a Kirāta, a hunter, with Pārvatī beside him as a Kirātī. The demon Mūka charges them in the form of a boar. Two arrows strike it at once, two men claim the kill, and the dispute becomes a duel that ends only when Arjuna’s flowers, offered to a liṅga, appear at the stranger’s feet. He understands then and falls. Śiva gives him the weapon. In Sanskrit, this narrative, as elaborated in the*mahākāvya**Kirātārjunīya*(किरातार्जुनीय) by Bhāravi, ends here, and so does every version told outside Kerala.

The Kerala version of the same story did not end where Kirātārjunīya did. In Malayalam ballads, the Kirāta-Kirātī figures kept their hunter’s skins as they wandered. During this wandering, Śiva embraces the excited Kirātī, and, as the story goes, a son was born. S. A. S. Sarma quotes that when Paramaśiva embraced Pārvatī in her intoxicating Kirātī form, they were blessed with a divine son named Vēṭṭaykkorumakan (വേട്ടയ്ക്കൊരുമകൻ).

This is the origin story of Vēṭṭaykkorumakan, which no one outside Kerala narrates, and in Kerala it survives more in ballads than in scriptures. We discussed a very similar parallel story about Cuṭalai Māṭaṉ (சுடலை மாடன்) in one of the previous articles, where Māṭaṉ was born of Śiva and Pārvatī, and the narrative has been retold for generations as ballads in the form of villuppāṭṭu (வில்லுப்பாட்டு).

S. A. S. Sarma, writing in Cracow Indological Studies in 2018, describes him as a minor deity and notes something else worth holding onto: in the Malabar narratives, he is not only an incarnation but also a hero, a man who came from elsewhere and did something the songs remember. Kumbhodaran and Preeta Nayar, in their iconographic survey two years later, call him a deity of folk origin, unique to Kerala, later absorbed into the Hindu pantheon as a son of Śiva and Pārvatī.

## Who was he and what are his origins?



The name itself is unusual, and that oddness is the key. Most deities from Kerala either have Sanskrit-derived names or are smoothly rendered in Malayalam. Kumbhodaran and Nayar break his name into vēṭṭa (വേട്ട), meaning “hunt"; oru (ഒരു), meaning "one"; and makan (മകൻ), meaning "son”. Together, they give a semantic derivation of the son for hunting. Devakumar Thenchery offers a similar reading of the syllables, as the son of Śiva, who is lord of hunters. There is also a distinctive case in which Y. V. Kannan revived a name that goes back to Gundert and calls him Vēṭṭaykkarumakan (വേട്ടയ്ക്കരുമകൻ).

Regardless of how the name is derived, the root is a Dravidian word. Tamil has vēṭṭai (வேட்டை) and vēṭṭuvan (வேட்டுவன்) or vēṭan (வேடன்) for hunter, which DEDR traces to a Dravidian root without difficulty. Another name is also in use: Kirātasūnu, which Kannan bluntly calls a later calque of Vēṭṭaykkorumakan. He goes on to say that this is one way he was absorbed into the fold, by using the calqued name in literature. The balladeer elaborates his origin story with a few tales; one describes his iconography with a golden*cūrika (dagger),*and him as an uncontrollable hunter, until Viṣṇu intervenes to control him, disguised as an elderly hunter or a Brahmin boy, and gives him the dagger on the condition that he never set it down. By shifting his bow to his left hand, he keeps his*cūrika*in his right hand, leaving him permanently unable to use the bow. It’s possible that the story exists to explain the pose, since every image of him ever made, in wood, stone, bronze, plaster, or colored powder, holds those weapons in those hands.

Afterward, he was sent south to the land of Paraśurāma as a guardian deity. The tōṟṟam songs of Malabar take him north through Kaliṅga, Tirupati, Kāñcīpuram, Kumbakonam, Rāmeśvaram, Thiruvananthapuram, and Thrissur before he reaches Kozhikode, where he takes the form of a Nāyar, marries into the Kāṟakkūṟa Nāyar family, and fathers a son named Kaṇṇan. The family’s fort at Balussery (Balussery Kōṭṭa) had passed to the Kurumbranad chief, and Vēṭṭaykkorumakan was asked to recover it. As he entered the fort with his son to make the request, he found a heap of twenty-one thousand six hundred coconuts. In the blink of an eye, his son broke every coconut in the pile, and the fort was recovered. Sarma notes that the kaḷam drawn at the fort’s entrance was rubbed out by him.

Another strand of his origin story places him elsewhere. In this version, he goes to Nambumala, in the borderlands of Tamil Nadu, and raises an army of tribal fighters, the Elegirivilli Chevakar. Kumbhodaran and Nayar note that this could suggest a tribal origin. The Nilambur Kovilakam, which claims to have built his first temple, keeps the story in circulation.

## Was he a tribal God?



The immediate guess might suggest Vēṭṭaykkorumakan emerging from the forest with the Wayanad hunters. But the historical records link him to the martial households of Malabar, especially the Kolattiri of Chirakkal, the rajas of Nileśvaram, Kōṭṭakkal, and Kurumbranad, and the kōvilakams of Nilambur and Amarambalam. Under the martial household, the Nāyar tarawads and kaḷari appear as the main basis. The Portuguese observed in the 16th century that young Nāyars trained with arms, and that every kaḷari kept a deity. Devotional literature presents him as the chief deity of the Chera kings. But none of these claims has any inscriptional support.

Kumbhodaran and Nayar identify the murals from the Lokanārkāvu Bhagavati temple and the Peruvanam Mahādeva temple as containing the oldest images of Vēṭṭaykkorumakan, both dating to the fifteenth century. A wooden figure from Kalpathur in Kozhikode is again attributed to the 15th or 16th century. The sequence is reconstructed as the image moving from the ballads to the mural, then to sculpture, with a powder drawing underneath throughout as a shared source. The murals and sculptures largely ceased by the fifteenth century, whereas the dhūḷicitra technique is described in Someśvara III’s Abhilaṣitārthacintāmaṇi, dating to the 12th century. The archaeologists date the present form to no earlier than the seventeenth century, while the story in ballads goes back much earlier.

Across all forms of his iconography, he stands in a straight samabhaṅga posture, wide-eyed and bearded, with two arms. The bow is in his left hand, and the*cūrika*in his right. He also wears the karaṇḍa mukuṭa or tied-up jaṭa, round earrings, heavy necklaces, armlets, a belly band, the sacred thread, and a pleated lower cloth. The artists almost always give him a green body, from the murals to the kaḷam figures.

## Kirāta



Kirāta is an ancient word attested in the oldest layer of the Rgveda. It also appears in the Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā, the Atharvaveda, the Manusmṛti, and the Amarakośa, always with the same meaning. The hunting peoples of the hills and forests, mostly in the north and northeast and outside the Arya hierarchy, were classified as mleccha or described as fallen kṣatriyas. So when the Mahābhārata depicts Śiva as a Kirāta, it presents him as a tribal avatar. Amarakośa duly lists Kirāta among Śiva’s names (Kirātamūrti/Kirāteśvara), the mountaineer who fought Arjuna, and the āgamas describe that form as having a black body, a bow in the left hand, a feather in the topknot, and a tiger skin. Kerala’s story of Kirātasūnu says that his father was a hunter.

If Vēṭṭaykkorumakan was a Dravidian hunter-god later given a Sanskrit father, then the father he received was itself a Brahmanical portrait of a tribal hunter. What likely happened was less Sanskrit absorbing a folk religion than two idioms of the wild recognizing each other. That seems more interesting than the usual Aryan-Dravidian narrative, and it fits what the sources actually say.

## The Rituals of Drawing



South and central Kerala encounter Vēṭṭaykkorumakan through kaḷameḻuttuṃ pāṭṭuṃ, the drawing and the song. Sarma watched the rituals unfold and recorded them line by line. A canopy is raised, and on the smooth floor of the Kurup, or, in some places, a Karuvan Nāyar, the image of him is laid out, 8-10 feet long. Five different powders were used: white rice flour, black charred paddy husk, yellow turmeric, lime for red, and dried leaves for green. Deepa and Lalini note that these same five serve every kaḷam in Kerala, from the Brahmin’s aniconic padma to the most terrifying Bhadrakāḷi. The figure is built in stages of increasingly rapid handwork: the skeleton first in white, then the body, and finally the ornaments over it. The finished drawing is the mural of a green-bodied, black-bearded deity, with a bow on the left and a blade on the right: Vēṭṭaykkorumakan.

The afternoon begins with the Mullakkalpāṭṭu, songs that describe and praise him; the man who sings becomes the veḷiccappāṭ, the oracle, or the one possessed by the deity himself. He then leads a torchlit procession, with pleated cloth, red silk at the waist, and a garland of red ixora to the kaḷam, followed by drums and pipes. In front of the temple, he performs a sword dance called Īṭuṃ Kūṟuṃ. When he reaches the figure on the floor (kaḷam), he circles the deity nine times, then places the sword by the wooden stool and withdraws so the Brahmin priests can do pūjā. After that, with a brief reenty of the veḷiccappāṭ, a different group begins singing the kaḷampāṭṭu, which recounts the deity's entry into the world. One of the songs is played on the nantuṇi, a plain stringed instrument made of kūvaḷam or kumiḻ wood, while the other keeps time on cymbals.

After the kaḷampāṭṭu, the veḷiccappāṭu stands in the middle of the Vēṭṭaykkorumakan drawing and begins smearing it with his legs. He throws coconut leaves onto its face and offers incense to the eight directions, chanting mantras to Gaṇapati, Sarasvatī, and the seven Mothers. He waves nine lit wicks over the image, drops rice on its head, touches its heart, rubs its face with the inflorescence of the coconut palm, and touches its feet. He starts to dance across the drawing until nothing is left, starting at the feet, and the face is never trampled. The colored powder is usually shared as prasādam with the people.

The rituals continue with a coconut-breaking ceremony, which excites people the most. Depending on the temple and the vow, anywhere from three hundred to twelve thousand coconuts are broken on a stone, one after another, with alternating hands. Karippath explains that the number 12,000 represents the names of the gods plus the eight guardians of the directions. Every crack of a shell is Kaṇṇan at the fort gate.

Above Kozhikode, no one depicts him as part of the ritual. He is worn as part of Teyyam. Teyyam, as Rich Freeman explains, does not mean the performer dancing a god but the god himself dancing in the body lent to him for the night. Vēṭṭaykkorumakan’s teyyam belongs to the martial clans’ repertoire. The teyyam performer is greeted at the sanctum with rice thrown around and back three times, circles the temple, and dances the kalāśams. The one with the small sword is called kaṭuttila (കടുത്തില), and the one with the churika. He breaks a coconut on the teṅṅākkal stone at the south-east corner, and the numbers remain modest compared with the southern tradition. Finally, uriyāṭal (ഉരിയാടൽ), the speaking part in which the deity calls devotees up in order of rank and caste, blesses them, and places turmeric on a plantain leaf into each pair of hands.

## Where does it leave us?



If we were to date Vēṭṭaykkorumakan, he is evidently a late-medieval deity. The murals at Lokanārkāvu and Peruvanam date from the fifteenth century and probably form the basis of the tradition. Later Kerala literature, such as *Tantrasamuccaya,**Kēraḷōlpatti,*or the*Kēraḷamāhātmyam,*does not document him. Even the recorded Sanskrit verses and the dhyānaślokas do not substantiate his origins. A true deity who stands outside the canon.

The silence runs deep and cuts both ways. A late-medieval deity, absent from Brahmanical registers, is what a folklore deity should look like. His abode is the tōṯṯam ballards and the kaḷam pāṭṭu. His name comes from a Dravidian root meaning “hunt,” the same root found in Kerala’s Vēṭar and Vēṭṭuvar. His ritual sits comfortably beside the Vēlaṉ veṟiyāṭṭu documented in Sangam literature and the fierce ritual methods of kāval deivams we have been discussing in our series. An older substrate, a pre-āgamic deity, remains the best explanation for these facts.

So what can be asserted is a process rather than concrete evidence of his origin. Sometime before the fifteenth century, a hunter-god of the Malabar countryside, whatever his prior records or origins were, became firmly embedded in the genealogy of what we know today as Kirātārjunīya. Śiva had been a hunter in the Kirātārjunīya narrative for more than a millennium, carved at Pattadakal and Ellora. At Mamallassery, a child was placed on Kirātī’s hip, watching the boar fall. The addition was probably the whole history or origin of our deity, Vēṭṭaykkorumakan: a regional deity written into a pan-Indian narrative, at the edge, held by his mother.
