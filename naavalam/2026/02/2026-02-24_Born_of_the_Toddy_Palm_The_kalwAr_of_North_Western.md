The Kalwār (कलवार), also known as Kalāl (कलाल) or Kalar, and occasionally referred to as Lahāne in certain localities, is a traditional liquor-distilling caste found across North, Central, and Eastern India. Historically identified with the production and sale of toddy (fermented palm sap) and other alcoholic beverages, the Kalwārs have long been associated with the liquor trade as their hereditary occupation. Very few direct references to the community appear in ancient or medieval literature; the scattered mentions that do exist tend to surface in later Mughal-era documents or local Rajasthani records, where the Kalwārs are typically categorized under liquor-related professions.

Much of what is known about their occupational roles, social practices, and internal clan structures comes from 19th- and early 20th-century ethnographic studies, including Herbert Risley's *Tribes and Castes of Bengal*, E. A. Gait's census notes, and the Punjab Census Reports. Because distilling and selling liquor was widely regarded as a demeaning line of work, the Kalwārs historically occupied a low position within India's traditional caste hierarchy. This social standing has shaped much of the community's collective experience and identity over the centuries.

## Regional Distribution



### Eastern Uttar Pradesh and Western Bihar



Large pockets of the Kalwār population historically resided in eastern Uttar Pradesh and western Bihar, regions situated along significant inland trade routes where a steady flow of merchants, travelers, and laborers sustained a consistent demand for fermented drinks. The community’s concentration in these areas was not incidental; the fertile plains of the Gangetic belt provided ready access to raw materials such as sugarcane and mahua flowers, both of which were central to local distillation practices. Over time, the Kalwārs in these regions developed well-established networks for producing and distributing country liquor, becoming an integral part of the local economic fabric.

### Punjab: The Kalals and the Ahluwalia Transformation



Further to the northwest, historical records from Punjab refer to “Kalals” as a recognized distilling community with a distinct social identity. The Kalals of Punjab followed much the same hereditary occupation as their counterparts elsewhere, but their story took a notable turn in the 18th and 19th centuries. Certain Kalal families, upon embracing Sikhism and gradually rising in social and political influence, chose to distance themselves from the stigma associated with the liquor trade by rebranding themselves as “Ahluwalias,” a name derived from the town of Ahlu in Punjab. This transformation is one of the more striking examples of caste mobility in the region, illustrating how religious conversion and political ascent could enable a community to reshape its public identity and social standing.

## Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh



In Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh, the Kalwārs maintained occupations broadly similar to those of their North Indian counterparts, engaging in the production and sale of locally distilled spirits. However, the trajectory of the community in these regions shifted significantly following the introduction of more restrictive liquor laws under British colonial administration. As licensing regimes tightened and the state sought greater control over the production and distribution of alcohol, many Kalwār families found their traditional livelihood increasingly curtailed. In response, many turned to agricultural pursuits, gradually integrating into the broader agrarian economy while retaining their caste identity and social customs.

### Maharashtra and the Southern Frontier



Although the name “Kalwār” is less prevalent in Maharashtra, genealogical and anthropological evidence points to the existence of a parallel community engaged in toddy tapping and arrack distillation in the region. These groups, while known by different local designations, share occupational, cultural, and sometimes kinship ties with the Kalwārs of the northern and central plains, suggesting that the community’s geographic reach extended further south than its most commonly recognized heartland, even if regional naming conventions have obscured the connection over time.

## Etymological Analysis



The word *Kalāl* (कलाल) is believed to derive from the Sanskrit *kalyapālaka*, a compound of *kalya* (“fermented liquor”) + *pāla* (“guardian, keeper”). This is almost certainly a Sanskrit folk etymology (or learned reanalysis), because the compound*kalya-pāla*is a transparently Sanskrit formation that would regularly yield Middle Indo-Aryan forms like \**kaliāpāla*or \**kalyavāla*, not\**kallāla*or\* *kalāl*. The actual attested Prākṛt form *kallāla* cannot be derived from *kalyapāla* through regular Prākṛt sound changes.

So, what is the root of *Kalāl* (कलाल), and why is this analysis critical to understanding a possible substratum that existed in prehistoric north-western parts of India?

### An Alternative Theory and the Structural Parallel



The most striking observation is that *kallāla* (“liquor-maker, distiller”) appears to share an identical Dravidian derivational template. The Proto-Dravidian root \**kaḷ* means “toddy” or “fermented palm sap.”

**Proto-Dravidian Root: \*kaḷ**

This root is remarkably well-attested across the Dravidian language family: Tamil *kaḷ* (கள்), Malayalam *kaḷḷ* (കള്ള്), Kannada *kaḷḷu* (ಕಳ್ಳು), Telugu *kallu* (కల్లు), Gondi *kalī*, and Kurukh *xalxā* all preserve the meaning of “toddy” or “palm wine” with minimal semantic drift. The reconstruction of \**kaḷ* as a proto-Dravidian form suggests the antiquity of toddy production as a culturally significant practice among early Dravidian-speaking communities long before sustained contact with Indo-Aryan speakers. The monosyllabic CVC with an alveolar/retroflex lateral \*ḷ is characteristically Dravidian and has no Indo-European source.

The proto-Dravidian \**kaḷ* appears to have entered the Indo-Aryan linguistic sphere at an early stage, likely during the period of initial bilingual contact between Dravidian and Old Indo-Aryan (Vedic Sanskrit) speakers. The Prākṛt form *kallāla* (𐐓𐐮𐑍𐐮𐐰𐐮), attested in Middle Indo-Aryan texts, represents an intermediate stage in this borrowing process. The phonological shift from the retroflex lateral /ḷ/ to the dental or alveolar lateral /l/ is a well-documented and regular sound change observed across a wide range of Dravidian loanwords in Sanskrit and Prākṛt. The Indo-Aryan phonological system lacked a retroflex lateral, necessitating its adaptation to the nearest available phoneme, either /l/ or, in some cases, /ḍ/. Some well-known borrowings include PD \**nāḷ* \> Skt. *nāla/nāḍa* (“tubular stalk”) and PD \**pīḷ* \> Skt. *pīḍ* (“to press, squeeze”).

### Dravidian Compound Analysis: kaḷ + āḷa(ṉ)



A particularly significant approach to the morphology of Pkt. *kallāla* is to analyze it as a Dravidian compound: \**kaḷ* (“toddy”) + \**āḷa(ṉ)* (“one who has mastered a trade” or “one who rules over a domain”). The agent-noun suffix *-aḷ*, with the characteristic alveolar lateral, is one of the most productive caste-naming and occupational-naming suffixes in the Dravidian family. Its presence in a range of established caste names lends strong structural support to this analysis:

• ***Vēḷḷāḷaṉ*** (வேளாளன்): from *vēḷ* (“cultivation, agriculture”) + *āḷa(ṉ)*, denoting “one who has mastered agriculture,” a prominent Tamil agrarian caste.

• ***Veḷḷāḷaṉ*** (வெள்ளாளன்): from *veḷ* (“flood, stream”) + *āḷa(ṉ)*, denoting “one who has mastered water management.”

• ***Villāḷaṉ***: from *vil* (“bow”) + *āḷa(ṉ)*, denoting “one who has mastered archery.”

• ***Kōḷāḷaṉ*** (கோளாளன்): from *kōḷ* + *āḷa(ṉ)*, denoting “one who has mastered retentive memory or firm grasp on things.”

By direct analogy, \**kaḷ* + \**āḷa(ṉ)* would yield \**kaḷḷāḷaṉ*, meaning “one who has mastered the toddy tapping process or trade,” a formation entirely consistent with the Dravidian pattern of deriving caste names from the combination of an occupational or material noun with the agentive suffix *āḷa(ṉ)*. The gemination of /ḷ/ at the morpheme boundary (*kaḷ + āḷaṉ \> kaḷḷāḷaṉ*) is a regular sandhi process in Dravidian morphophonology. When the word was borrowed into Old Indo-Aryan, the de-retroflexion (\*ḷ → l) of Dravidian alveolar/retroflex lateral /ḷ/ usually mapped onto the nearest Sanskrit equivalent, the dental-alveolar /l/.

The derivation would follow: PD \**kaḷ-āḷaṉ* → \**kaḷḷāḷaṉ* → Prākṛt *kallāla* → Sanskrit *kalāla / kalyapāla*. The Prākṛt geminate *-ll-* in *kallāla* was simplified to *-l-* in certain registers and later reflexes, yielding *kalāl*, the modern caste name.

### Sanskritization: kalyapāla and kalpāla



The Sanskrit word *kalyapāla* is most probably a folk-etymological reanalysis, a common phenomenon in which the lexeme is reinterpreted through the morphological or semantic structure of the receiving language, akin to calquing. Hemacandra in the *Abhidhānacintāmaṇi* and other medieval lexicons (*kośa* literature) record the forms *kalyapāla* (कल्यपाल) and its contracted variant *kalpāla* (कल्पाल), glossed as “a distiller” or “a liquor-maker.” This Sanskrit compound is essentially a semantic parallel to the proposed Dravidian etymology (*kaḷ* “toddy” + *āḷaṉ* “master of a trade”), with the words reimagined within Indo-Aryan morphological conventions. The element *kalya* itself is noteworthy: while it is attested in classical Sanskrit with the meaning of “health, well-being” and, by extension, “an intoxicating drink taken in the morning,” some scholars have suggested that *kalya* in the sense of “fermented liquor” may itself be an early Dravidian borrowing that was subsequently naturalized into Sanskrit, a possibility that, if confirmed, would make *kalyapāla* a doubly Dravidian-influenced formation.

### From kallāla to Kalāl and Kalwār



The progression from the Prākṛt *kallāla* to the modern Hindi-Urdu form Kalāl (कलाल) follows well-established patterns of phonological simplification in the transition from Middle Indo-Aryan to New Indo-Aryan (NIA) languages. The variant Kalwār (कलवार) appears to incorporate the suffix *-vār* or *-wār*, which in several NIA languages functions as an agentive or occupational marker. This suffix may represent a secondary derivation within the NIA period, appended to the base *kal-*, a reduced form of the original *kallāla / kalāl*.

## Discussion: A Possible Cognate - The Tamil Kaḷḷar



An intriguing, but a speculative extension of this etymological analysis that I would like add here is, the Tamil caste name Kaḷḷar (கள்ளர்). In contemporary Tamil usage, Kaḷḷar is conventionally understood to mean “thief” or “warrior clan,” derived from the Tamil semantic evolution kaḷ (கள், “to steal”) with the agentive plural suffix -ar. If the first element of Kaḷḷar were traced not to kaḷ (”to steal”) but to the homophonous kaḷ (”toddy”), the name could originally have denoted a community associated with toddy production, a derivation structurally and semantically parallel to the northern Kalāl/Kalwār. The phonological form kaḷ + ar the standard Tamil agentive plural suffix \> Kaḷḷar would be entirely regular and Kaḷvar (கள்வர்) denoting the people who belong to a toddy tapping community.

Though this hypothesis merely connects phonological coincidence but we should explore the literary association of Kaḷḷar with the Pālai (பாலை) landscape of Tamil Caṅkam literature. *Tolkāppiyam* (தொல்காப்பியம்) classifies the Tamil-speaking world into five tiṇai (திணை) or landscape-mood zones, each associated with a characteristic terrain, flora, fauna, deity, mode of life, and human community. The fifth of these, Pālai, denotes the arid, sun-scorched wasteland, a landscape of drought, harsh terrain, and separation. The *Tolkāppiyam*‘s *Poruḷatikāram* (பொருளதிகாரம்) and subsequent Caṅkam commentarial tradition identify the inhabitants of the Pālai tract as communities engaged in highway ambush and plunder, and it is in this context that the Kaḷḷar (along with the closely allied Maravar and Eyiṉar) are situated.

The conventional reading of this association is straightforward: the Kaḷḷar inhabited the barren Pālai country, where settled agriculture was impracticable; consequently, they turned to raiding and robbery, and the name Kaḷḷar (”thieves”) reflects this mode of subsistence. But the alternative etymology proposed demands a reappraisal of the ecological logic underlying the association. And the article where I discussed about the potential migration of Yādava people from the heartland of Indus, should be analogically compared for the spread of caste community of Kalāl or Kalwār from the north western region of India. If the connection holds, it would imply that the occupational identity linking toddy production to a specific endogamous community stretches not only across the breadth of the subcontinent, from the Gangetic plains to the dry tracts of Tamil Nadu, but deep into the earliest strata of Tamil literary civilization.

## Conclusion



In summary, the caste name Kalāl (कलाल) / Kalwār (कलवार) is best understood as deriving ultimately from the proto-Dravidian root \*kaḷ ("toddy"), entering the Indo-Aryan linguistic sphere at an early period, possibly as far back as the Old Indo-Aryan stage and subsequently undergoing both phonological adaptation (retroflex /ḷ/ \> dental /l/) and morphological reanalysis (from a Dravidian compound kaḷ + āḷaṉ to a Sanskritized compound kalya + pāla). The name thus encapsulates, in a single word, the deep intertwining of Dravidian and Indo-Aryan linguistic and cultural histories, and it underscores the degree to which the identity of the Kalwār community has been, from its very etymological roots, inseparable from the production of toddy and fermented liquor.
