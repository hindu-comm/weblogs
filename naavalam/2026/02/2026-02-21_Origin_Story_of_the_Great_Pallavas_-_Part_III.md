## Discussion 2: Rise of the Brahma-Kṣatriyas



The epigraphic corpus of the Deccan reveals a complex process of socio-political legitimization wherein emergent regional powers actively negotiated orthodox Brāhmaṇical frameworks to validate their sovereignty. A foundational text in this discourse is the Nāsik Cave Inscription of the Sātavāhana queen-mother Gautamī Bālaśrī. Issued during the reign of her grandson Vāsiṣṭhīputra Pulumāvi, the *praśasti* (eulogy) extols the great king Gautamīputra Śātakarṇi with prominent titles such as *ekabamhaṇa* (Skt. *ekabrahmaṇa*, “the unique Brāhmaṇa”) and *khatīyadapamaṇamadaṇa* (Skt. *kṣatriyadarpamānamardana*, “destroyer of the pride and conceit of the Kṣatriyas”). Furthermore, Gautamīputra is credited with having achieved *viṇivattita-cātuvvaṇṇasaṃkara* (Skt. *vinivartitacāturvarṇasaṃkara*), signifying the restoration of *varṇa* purity by halting the intermixture of the four socio-religious classes.

Historians such as D. R. Bhaṇḍārkar have interpreted these epithets as early assertions of a “Brahma-Kṣatriya” status. By emphasizing Brāhmaṇical purity, the dynasty sought to legitimize its rule over indigenous populations while simultaneously projecting martial supremacy against foreign invaders (such as the Śakas). This process of Kṣatriyaization established a regional paradigm that was subsequently emulated by Sātavāhana successor states, including the Āndhra Ikṣvākus of Nāgārjunakoṇḍa, the Kadambas of Banavāsī, the Pallavas of Kāñcī, and the Viṣṇukuṇḍins of Āndhra. These dynasties uniformly engaged in the adoption of Brāhmaṇical *gotra* lineages or explicit Brahma-Kṣatriya conversions to validate their imperial authority.

However, the historical veracity of these claims of orthodox lineage warrants critical scrutiny. The Sātavāhanas serve as the historical prototype for the Brahma-Kṣatriya phenomenon in the Deccan; yet, while Gautamīputra Śātakarṇi is styled *ekabrahmaṇa*, orthodox North Indian texts such as the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* and *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* classify the dynasty as Āndhras, and occasionally disparage them as *vṛṣalas* (Śūdras or degraded classes). From a strict Vedic and Gaṅgetic perspective, the term “Āndhra” denoted non-Āryan, indigenous tribal populations residing south of the Vindhya range.

### Ritual Assimilation: Gotra Adoption and the Purohita-Pravara-Nyāya



The disparity between indigenous origins and Brāhmaṇical claims is further evidenced in later dynastic charters. The Pallavas, in the Kaśākūḍi copper plates, claim descent from Aśvatthāman of the Bhāradvāja *gotra*; the Koṇḍamūḍi plates attribute the Bṛhatphalāyana *gotra* to their issuers; and the Śālaṅkāyanas similarly invoke an eponymous *gotra* affiliation. In contrast, the Āndhra Ikṣvākus bypassed priestly lineages to claim Kṣatriya status directly within the mythical *Sūryavaṃśa* (Solar Dynasty).

According to the strict Vedic *Dharmaśāstra*, the *gotras* claimed by dynasties such as the Pallavas constitute priestly (Brāhmaṇa) lineages, which are theoretically prohibited to the Kṣatriya ruling class. The mechanism for this assimilation, however, is found within orthodox ritual mechanics, specifically the *Purohita-Pravara-Nyāya* rule. As articulated in the *Aitareya Brāhmaṇa* (34.5) and elaborated in later legal commentaries such as the *Mitākṣarā*, this rule permits a non-Brāhmaṇa chieftain to ritually adopt the *gotra* and *pravara* of his officiating Brāhmaṇa priest during royal consecration (*abhiṣeka*) or initiation (*upanayana*):

> *Yajamānasya ārṣeyān pravṛṇīte / Purohita-pravaro rājñām*

This liturgical loophole provided a mechanism for indigenous Deccan chieftains to formalize their transition into orthodox *mahārājādhirājas*.

### Mēnarikam vs. S*apiṇḍa* Exogamy (*Dharmaśāstra* vs. *Deśadharma)*



The tension between orthodox North Indian frameworks and indigenous Southern realities is most distinctly visible in dynastic marriage practices. In orthodox *Dharmaśāstra*, marriage to a consanguineous relative, such as a father’s sister’s daughter, constitutes incest and a severe violation of *sapiṇḍa* exogamy. Conversely, the Dravidian kinship system inherently prefers cross-cousin marriage, a practice culturally normative, socially prescribed, and fundamentally distinct from the Indo-Āryan model of the Gaṅgetic plains.

The epigraphic records of the Ikṣvāku dynasty furnish the earliest incontrovertible evidence of this Dravidian practice, locally termed *mēnarikam* (Telugu: మేనరికం), operating at the highest levels of statecraft. While the Ikṣvāku monarchs sponsored orthodox Vedic sacrifices (such as the *Aśvamedha*) and heavily patronized Buddhist institutions, their domestic and political alliances adhered strictly to Dravidian *deśadharma* (regional custom).

The Ikṣvāku founder, Vāsiṣṭhīputra Cāntamūla, arranged the marriages of his sisters, Cāntisirī and Hammasirī, to the *Mahātalavara* (great general) Vāsiṣṭhīputra Skandaśrī and another prominent *Mahātalavara* of the Pūkīya family, respectively. Subsequently, King Śrī-Vīrapuruṣadatta (son of Cāntamūla I) espoused his paternal aunts’ daughters: Mahādevī Bāpiśrī (daughter of Cāntisirī) and Mahādevī Chāthisirī (daughter of Hammasirī). By executing these cross-cousin marriages, Vīrapuruṣadatta ensured that the powerful Pūkīya generals transitioned from being merely his father’s brothers-in-law to becoming his own fathers-in-law. This double kinship bond forged unbreakable political loyalty between the central monarchy and its paramount military feudatories.

### Pallava Parallels



Analogous structural evidence of Dravidian kinship networks is prevalent in the later history of the Pallava dynasty, particularly within the lineage of Nandivarman II (c. 731–796 CE). This later Pallava branch repeatedly intermarried with the Rāṣṭrakūṭa imperial family, demonstrating a hallmark of Dravidian cross-cousin obligations, in which iterative unions with specific “wife-giver” lineages are sustained across generations.

Nandivarman II wed Queen Revā, a Rāṣṭrakūṭa princess. Their son, Dantivarman (c. 796–846 CE), was named in honor of his maternal uncle or grandfather (Dantidurga), illustrating a quintessential Dravidian convention that privileges the mother’s natal lineage. Subsequently, Nandivarman III (c. 846–869 CE) married Queen Śaṅkhā, the daughter of Rāṣṭrakūṭa Emperor Amoghavarṣa I. Structurally, this represents a classificatory cross-cousin marriage: Nandivarman III espoused a woman from his paternal grandmother Revā’s natal house. Amoghavarṣa I thus occupied the classificatory role of *mātula* (mother’s brother) to the Pallava line, rendering Śaṅkhā an extended “mother’s brother’s daughter.”

In conclusion, the Pallava assertion of Brahma-Kṣatriya status and Bhāradvāja descent should not be interpreted as literal genealogical history, but rather as a highly successful legitimizing construct facilitated by the *Purohita-Pravara-Nyāya* rule. The persistence of *mēnarikam* networks throughout early South Indian dynastic history provides compelling structural evidence of indigenous Dravidian origins operating beneath a veneer of Brāhmaṇical orthodoxy. Based on the strategic intermarriages of the Ikṣvāku era, it is highly probable that the Pallavas originated as local indigenous chieftains—potentially rooted in the powerful Pūkīya feudatory clan—who utilized orthodox Brāhmaṇa priests to ritually elevate their socio-political status upon assuming sovereign power.

## Discussion 3: Puṣyakandīya Mahāsēna to Pallava Subrahmaṇya



### The Epigraphic Foundations of the Mahāsena Cult at Vijayapurī



The Ikṣvāku dynasty represents a pivotal transitional phase in the religious history of South India. While the royal women of the court are renowned for their monumental patronage of Buddhism, the kings and their high-ranking generals (*Mahātalavaras*) championed a resurgent Vedic-Purāṇic identity. Among the Brahmanical deities patronized, Mahāsena, variously invoked in epigraphic and literary records as Skanda, Kārttikeya, Kumāra, Ṣaṇmukha, and later, Subrahmaṇya, emerged not merely as a god of war, but as the central state deity of the military aristocracy.

The Nāgārjunakoṇḍa āyaka-pillar inscription (C3), discovered at the Mahācaitya, is crucial for understanding the political theology of this era. It documents a royal epithet that forms part of a standard eulogy (*praśasti*) to the founder of the dynasty, Vāsiṣṭhīputra Cāntamūla I. The inscription reads:

> *Sidhaṃ namo Bhagavato Devarāja-sakatasa supabudha-bodhino savamñuno
> savasat-ānukampakasa*
>
> *jita-rāga-dosa-moha-vipamutasa mahāgani-vasabha-gaṃdha-hathisa
> samma-sambudhasa dhātuvara-parigahitasa Mahācetiye*
>
> *Mahārājasa **Virūpakhapati-Mahāsena-parigahitasa**
> Agihota-Agiṭhoma-Vājapey-Asamedha-yājisa*
>
> *aneka-hiraṇa-koṭi-go-satasahasa-hala-satasahasa-padāyisa savathesu
> apatihata-saṃkapasa*
>
> *Vāsiṭhīputasa Ikhākusa siri-Cātamūlasa sodarāya bhaginiya raño
> Māḍharīputasa*
>
> *siri-Vīrapurisadatasa pituchā Mahāsenāpatisa Mahātalavarasa
> Vāsiṭhīputasa Pūkiyānaṃ*
>
> *Kaṃdasirisa bhariyā... Mahātalavarī Cāntisiri...*
>
> *Raño siri-Vīrapurisadatasa saṃva vā pā diva...*

Though this is fundamentally a Buddhist dedicatory document consecrated by Mahātalavarī Cāntisiri (the paternal aunt of King Śrī Vīrapuruṣadatta and wife of the *Mahātalavara* Skandaśrī of the Pūkiya family), it proudly lists the militant, Vedic titles of the dynasty’s founder. Foremost among these is the epithet *Virūpakhapati-Mahāsena-parigahitasa* (Sanskrit: *Virūpākṣapati-Mahāsena-parigṛhīta*), meaning “the king who is accepted \[or favored\] by Mahāsena, the Lord of the Virūpas.” It is significant that this martial, Śaivite epithet was prominently featured in a donation sanctioned by a Buddhist patroness, reflecting the synthesized religious landscape of the period. Furthermore, it must be noted that prior to asserting sovereignty, Śrī Cāntamūla himself served as a *Mahātalavara* under the Sātavāhanas, suggesting that his devotion to Mahāsena was rooted in his origins as a military chieftain.

### Cultural Transmission: Sātavāhana Precedents and Martial Republics



The prominence of Mahāsena among the Ikṣvākus is frequently attributed to their matrimonial alliances with the Western Kṣatrapas of Ujjain. However, literary evidence suggests that the deity was already deeply integrated into the cultural fabric of the Deccan centuries earlier during the Sātavāhana period. The *Gāthāsaptaśatī* contains verses that reference the polycephalic nature of Kārttikeya, treating him not merely as a terrifying war god, but as a familiar figure in romantic metaphors:

> *so jayaï su-a-muhoppala-pabbhara-paricumvi-a-Gaurī-va-aṇo /
> ṇi-a-paa-ṇa-ha-ppahā-milï-a-Surāsurā-ṇamï-a-calo ṇo*
>
> *Saṇmuha-sariccha-vippiya-karaṇa-uha-tthā-iṇo bhaṇasu ūsa /
> Ekka-muhassa vi majjhaṃ pāe paḍi-ussa kisa ṇa lajjā*

In another instance, the text acts as a cultural mirror, seamlessly weaving the iconography of Kārttikeya and his peacock mount (*moro*) into the natural landscape:

> *Kālāguru-dhūva-sama-cchavīhiṃ a-a-meva dīsai gahaṇe /
> Kārttikeya-bhaṃdīra-komudī-kāṇaṇe moro*

Unlike later Ikṣvāku inscriptions, which invoke the terrifying *Virūpākṣapati*, the *gāthās* treat Ṣaṇmukha with a humanized familiarity. This indicates that Kārttikeya was well known among the populace of Dakṣiṇāpatha prior to the intense Western Kṣatrapa influence. It is highly probable that this earlier diffusion occurred through the southward spread of the *āyudhajīvī* (martial) republics of the Vahīka Saṃghas, such as the Mālavās, Yaudheyas, and Parvatīyas from the Indus-Punjab region, who venerated Kārttikeya as their primary tutelary deity alongside the Saptamātṛkās.

This northern martial connection is corroborated by archaeological findings at Nāgārjunakoṇḍa. The excavation of a square *garbhagṛha* installed by *Mahāsenāpati Mahātalavara* Khaṇḍaviśākhamnaka (of the Dhanaka family and son-in-law to Śrī Cāntamūla) yielded a mutilated stone sculpture of Kārttikeya. The iconographic program of this sculpture is nearly identical to the depiction of Kārttikeya on the copper coinage of the Yaudheya confederacy, bridging the martial cultures of the Punjab and the Deccan.

### The Mahātalavaras as Conduits of the Martial Deity



The epigraphic record clearly demonstrates that invoking Mahāsena in the early centuries CE was a practice deeply rooted in the military nobility rather than being a strictly royal prerogative. A Sanskrit inscription from the reign of King Ehavala Cāṃtamūla records the construction of a famous temple (*Sarvadevādhivāsa*) by his general, *Mahātalavara* Eliśrī:

> *...deve yasyātibhaktir hutavahatanaye caṇḍaśaktau kumārepautras
> senāpater yyas samaravijayinaẖ kkhyātakīrtter aṇikkeḥprāsādaṅ
> gāṇḍiputtras sa talavaravaraẖ kārttikeyapprasādāteliśrīś śrīviśālaṁ
> śubhamatir akarot sarvvadevādhivāsam...*

The text eulogizes Eliśrī for his intense devotion (*atibhakti*) to Kumāra, the son of Agni (*Hutavaha-tanaya*), who wields the terrible spear (*caṇḍaśakti*). Eliśrī credits the successful construction of the exceedingly grand temple to the grace of Kārttikeya (*Kārttikeyaprasādāt*). Further excavations at Nāgārjunakoṇḍa revealed another shrine installed by *Mahāsenāpati Mahātalavara* Khaṇḍaviśākhamṇaka of the Dhanaka family, a son-in-law of Śrī Cāntamūla. The archaeological retrieval of a mutilated Kārttikeya sculpture from the square *garbhagṛha* bearing near-identical iconographic traits to the deity depicted on ancient Yaudheya copper coins reinforces the theory that invoking this martial deity was fundamentally a practice rooted in the military valour of the *Mahātalavara* clans rather than an exclusively royal prerogative.

> *Siddham Mahārājasya aśvamedha-yājinaḥ
> aneka-hiraṇya-koṭi-pradāyinaḥśrī-Cāntamūlasya duhituḥ
> Aṭavi-Cāntiśrīnikāyāḥ bhartuḥ Mahāsenāpateḥ Mahātalavarasya Dhanakānāṃ
> Skandaviśākhamṇakasya Deva-melukaḥ ... kāritaḥ iti*

### The Cāḷuvaṉ Kuppam Excavations: The Missing Southern Link



The archaeological discoveries at Cāḷuvaṉ Kuppam, located north of Mahabalipuram, provide the crucial spatial and chronological link for the arrival of the Mahāsena cult into the Tamil heartland. Excavations by the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) revealed a brick temple complex dedicated to Murugan, substantially predating the Pallava monolithic structures. Stratigraphic analysis delineates three distinct phases:

- **Phase I (Early 4th century CE):** The oldest exposed level features
  a simple, rectangular brick *garbhagṛha* resting on a cushion of river
  alluvium. Crucially, the temple faces north, an architectural anomaly
  proving the structure predates the strict codification of Āgamic and
  Śilpa Śāstric mandates (formalized c. 6th–7th centuries CE), which
  required east or west orientations.

- **Phase II (6th–8th century CE):** A pre-Pallava phase characterized
  by an underlying brick basement.

- **Phase III (8th–10th century CE):** The Pallava-era construction
  phase, marked by the transition to granite walls and pillars.

### The Migration of the Martial Clans



The dissolution of Ikṣvāku sovereignty at Vijayapurī in the early 4th century CE coincided with the assertion of independence by successor lineages, notably the Bṛhatphalāyanas, Śālaṅkāyanas, and early Pallavas. This chronological horizon mirrors a profound political upheaval in Tamiḻakam, traditionally remembered as the Kaḷabhra interregnum, which displaced the established Caṅkam-era triumvirate (Cēras, Cōḻas, and Pāṇḍyas).

The origins of the Kaḷabhras remain a subject of fierce historiographical debate. While some scholars propose they were Sātavāhana feudatories pushed southward following the empire’s collapse, others identify them with the Kaḷavar tribe, a martial community from the Vēṅkaṭam region. Caṅkam literature corroborates the presence of valorous chieftains from this demographic, such as Pulli of the Kaḷavar clan:

> மழபுலம் வணக்கிய மாவண் புல்லி விழவுடை விழுச்சீர் வேங்கடம் பெறினும்

> *maḻapulam vaṇakkiya māvaṇ pulli viḻavuṭai viḻuccīr vēṅkaṭam peṟiṉum*

I would like to posit a synthetic hypothesis: the Puṣyakaṇḍīyas of the Palnāḍu region, devoted to Mahāsena and operating as powerful *Mahātalavaras,* constituted one of the elite feudatory lineages that reconstituted themselves as the early Pallavas at Kāñcīpuram. Their southward expansion likely occurred in tandem with the broader migration of other martial clans, including the Kaḷavars. If this historical identification holds, the Mahāsena worshipped by the Puṣyakaṇḍīyas, characterized by the iconographic triad of the spear (*vēl*), rooster (*kukkuṭa*), and peacock (*mayūra*) was the very presiding deity enshrined during the earliest Phase I of the Cāḷuvaṉ Kuppam temple, later officially recognized as Subrahmaṇya in subsequent Pallava epigraphs.

The geographical migration of this martial deity culminated in profound liturgical institutionalization. The earliest explicit literary reference to a public temple festival bearing the name*Taipūcam*dates to the Pallava domain in the 7th century CE. In the *Pūmpāvai Patikam*, the Tamil Śaiva saint Tirujñāṉacampantar enumerates the grand monthly festivals of the Tamil ritual calendar, specifically mentioning the*Taipūcam*festivalcelebrated at the Kapālīśvarar temple in Mayilāppūr. This solidifies the historical reality that by the middle of the Pallava epoch, the Northern/Deccani martial god of the *Mahātalavaras* had seamlessly synthesized with indigenous Tamil traditions, placing the worship of Subrahmaṇya firmly at the heart of South Indian public religion.

## Discussion 4: Family Feud



Rājan Vāsiṣṭhīputra Rudrapuruṣadatta represents the final attested sovereign of the Ikṣvāku dynasty of Vijayapurī. Succeeding his father, Ehavala Cāṃtamūla, his reign is generally dated to circa 297–309 CE. The eventual collapse of the Ikṣvāku polity is historically attributed to the ascendant early Pallavas; it is widely posited that Siṃhavarman I defeated Rudrapuruṣadatta to establish Pallava hegemony in the region. Crucial to understanding this dynastic transition is the role of the *Mahātalavaras* (high-ranking feudatory nobles), upon whom the Ikṣvāku administrative apparatus heavily relied. For instance, epigraphic evidence from a *chāyā-stambha* (memorial pillar) indicates that Rudrapuruṣadatta retained figures such as Śrīvarman of the Bṛhatphalāyana *gotra* in the capacity of *Mahātalavara*.

To contextualize the political instability that likely precipitated the dynasty’s fall, it is necessary to examine the court dynamics during the reign of Ehavala Cāṃtamūla. Epigraphic records identify three prominent queens, whose disparate backgrounds suggest the presence of distinct, competing political factions at court. These included Mahādevī Khaṇḍuvulā, notable for her patronage of a deeply rooted indigenous fertility cult; Mahādevī Vammabhaṭṭā, the mother of the eventual successor Rudrapuruṣadatta; and Mahādevī Kupanaśrī, the mother of Hāritīputra Vīrapuruṣadatta II.

Unlike many Ikṣvāku queens who originated from local Andhra feudatory families, such as the Pūgīyas or the Puṣyakaṇḍīyas, Vammabhaṭṭā was of foreign, royal extraction. Inscriptions explicitly title her *Mahākṣatrapa-dhītā* (”Daughter of the Great Satrap”). The Mahākṣatrapas were the sovereigns of the Western Kshatrapa (Śaka/Scythian) dynasty, governing the Malwa and Gujarat regions from their capital at Ujjayinī. While her father is not explicitly named, chronological correlations suggest she was the daughter of a prominent late 3rd-century Kshatrapa ruler, likely Rudrasena II or Bhartṛdāman. This Śaka lineage profoundly influenced the royal nomenclature of the Ikṣvāku. The prefix “Rudra” in her son’s name, Rudrapuruṣadatta, is a defining dynastic hallmark of the Western Kshatrapas, representing a clear departure from traditional Andhra naming conventions.

Conversely, Mahādevī Kupanaśrī represented the entrenched local nobility. She belonged to the powerful Puṣyakaṇḍīya family, a prominent noble house in the Palnādu region during the 3rd and 4th centuries CE. Her prestigious lineage is recorded in epigraphy as follows: *Puṣyakandiyānāṃ Mahātalavarasya Skandagopasya naptryāḥ*

> *Mahātalavarasya Khandahālasya duhitụḥ*
>
> *... Mahādevyāḥ Kupanaśriyāḥ*

Notably, while many Ikṣvāku royal women patronized Buddhism, inscriptions associate Kupanaśrī with the construction of a Śaiva temple dedicated to Nodagīśvarasvāmin. This suggests that the Khaṇḍahāla lineage may have been a staunch patron of Brahmanical Hinduism.

The presence of Hāritīputra Vīrapuruṣadatta II, the half-brother of Rudrapuruṣadatta, introduces significant ambiguity into the late Ikṣvāku succession. Appearing primarily as a *Mahārājakumāra* (crown prince) in the epigraphic corpus, it remains debated among historians whether he ever ascended the throne as a full sovereign. This dynamic strongly implies a severe succession crisis between two competing noble factions: the indigenous Andhra Puṣyakaṇḍīyas supporting Vīrapuruṣadatta II, and the foreign Śaka alliance supporting Rudrapuruṣadatta. The eventual ascension of Rudrapuruṣadatta indicates that the Śaka faction ultimately prevailed in this dynastic struggle.

Given the brevity of Rudrapuruṣadatta’s reign and the subsequent collapse of the state, a plausible historical hypothesis emerges: the disenfranchised Puṣyakaṇḍīya faction, potentially allied with other disgruntled *Mahātalavaras* (such as the Bṛhatphalāyanas), may have facilitated or actively participated in the overthrow of the Śaka-backed Ikṣvāku ruler, paving the way for the establishment of the early Pallavas of Kāñcīpuram.

This theory of a transition aided by local elites is supported by administrative continuity. Early Pallava Prakrit charters demonstrate that the Pallavas strategically retained the *Mahātalavaras* in their original posts to ensure local stability. The Mañcikallu Stone Inscription of Siṃhavarman I records a transaction by a high-ranking official named *Mahātalavara* Vāsiṣṭhīputra Skandacalikiremmanaka, suggesting this Ikṣvāku official either defected to or was reappointed by the Pallavas in the Palnādu region. Furthermore, the Hirahaḍagalli Copper Plates of Śivaskandavarman explicitly list *Mahātalavaras* in royal edicts alongside other state officials, such as *Mahādaṇḍanāyakas* (Great Generals) and *Mahāgrāmakas* (Village Administrators). The Mayidavōlu Copper Plates, issued by Śivaskandavarman while serving as *Yuvamahārāja* (Crown Prince) at the provincial capital of Dhānyakaṭaka, confirm that this rank remained active under provincial Pallava administration. However, this assimilation was temporary; the use of the title *Mahātalavara* by the Pallavas is chronologically restricted to their Early Phase (the era of the Prakrit charters, c. late 3rd to 4th century CE), eventually fading into obsolescence as the dynasty solidified its southern identity.

## Summary: **From Martial Republics to Sovereign Dynasties - The Indigenous Roots and Ascension of the Early Pallavas**



In this research, I have sought to challenge the traditional, heavily Sanskritized narratives surrounding the dynastic origins of early South Indian powers, proposing instead a framework deeply rooted in indigenous martial and kinship networks.

First, by discarding the mythological *Sūryavaṃśa* origins of the Āndhra Ikṣvākus in favor of their Purāṇic classification as *Śrīparvatīya Andhras*, I have traced a broader migratory continuity. I argue that the *arājatā* (stateless) warrior republics of the North-West—the *Vahīka Saṃghas* and *Parvatīyas* (such as the Yaudheyas and Mālavās)—migrated southward during the Iron Age under the pressure of Śaka and Kuṣāṇa incursions. Reconstituting themselves in the Dakṣiṇāpatha as the Bhojas and Raṭṭhikas under Sātavāhana suzerainty, these groups evolved functionally and linguistically into the *Mahātalavaras* and modern Reḍḍis of the Āndhra region. Crucially, this migratory hypothesis provides a historical anchor for the Tamil Caṅkam literary legends of the Vēḷir, suggesting a shared socio-linguistic memory of a massive Iron Age demographic shift.

Second, I have demonstrated that the Ikṣvāku founder, Vāsiṣṭhīputra Cāntamūla I, established the structural blueprint for the ascension of feudatories in the Deccan. Transitioning from a *Mahātalavara* under the disintegrating Sātavāhana empire to a sovereign monarch ritually legitimized through the *Aśvamedha* sacrifice, Cāntamūla I secured his borders through strategic matrimonial alliances. By marrying his sister Cāntisirī to Skandaśrī, a *Mahātalavara* of the formidable Pūkīya clan, he consolidated immediate power but inadvertently cultivated a parallel power center that would eventually eclipse his own lineage.

This brings me to my central proposition regarding the post-Ikṣvāku power vacuum. The synchronized emergence of the Bṛhatphalāyanas, Śālaṅkāyanas, and early Pallavas—all sharing structural homologies in administrative lexicon and utilizing Vedic *yajñas* for sovereign legitimacy—leaves a glaring historiographical gap regarding the fate of the powerful Pūkīyas. I propose a radical re-identification: the early Pallavas were, in fact, this ascendant Pūkīya warrior clan. I posit that Siṃhavarman I, originating from the ancient Pūkīya stronghold of Pūṅgavṛolu (modern Oṅgōle/Nellore), overthrew the Ikṣvākus with the likely support of Bṛhatphalāyana monarchs like Jayavarman, before strategically relocating the dynastic capital south to Kāñcīpuram.

Finally, to substantiate this Pūkīya-Pallava continuity, I have highlighted the enduring epigraphic and cultic devotion to the martial god Mahāsena. The Nāgārjunakoṇḍa *āyaka*-pillar inscriptions explicitly invoke the epithet *Virūpākṣapati-Mahāsena-parigṛhīta* (”He who is accepted/favoured by Mahāsena, the Lord of the Virūpas”) within the standard *praśasti* of Cāntamūla I. Tracing this specific religious patronage directly to the *Mahātalavarī* Cāntisiri, wife of the Pūkīya general, reveals that devotion to Mahāsena was the core cultic identity of this military aristocracy.

Ultimately, my findings indicate that the martial ideology, linguistic traditions, and religious identity of the Pūkīyas were seamlessly carried southward by these ascending chieftains, eventually manifesting as the early Pallava state religion at sites like Cāḷuvaṉ Kuppam. The Pallavas did not arrive from the north as ready-made Vedic kings; rather, they emerged from the indigenous, spear-wielding *Mahātalavaras* of the Deccan, successfully transitioning from generals of the Ikṣvākus to the sovereign masters of Tamiḻakam.

Final thoughts, Pāli text Mahāvaṃsa, in Chapter 29 (”The Beginning of the Great Thūpa”), describes the grand foundation-laying ceremony of the Great Stūpa (the Ruwanwelisaya in Anuradhapura) organized by the Sri Lankan King Duṭṭhagāmaṇī (Dutugemunu) in the 2nd century BCE. The text lists delegations of Buddhist monks arriving from various prominent regions across the Indian subcontinent and beyond. Among these delegations, a massive contingent of monks is recorded as having arrived from “**Pallavabhogga**,” led by a venerable monk named Mahādeva.

> *Cattāri satasahassāni sahassāni ca saṭṭhi ca \|* *Bhikkhū
> Pallavabhoggamhā Mahādevo mahāmati \|\|*

The mention of *Pallavabhogga* (meaning “the realm or fief of the Pallavas”) in this 5th-century CE chronicle is highly significant. It demonstrates that by the time the *Mahāvaṃsa* was compiled, the region associated with the early Pallavas (widely identified by scholars as the Palnādu) was internationally recognized as a major, distinct geographical and religious center capable of sending a massive Buddhist delegation to Sri Lanka.
