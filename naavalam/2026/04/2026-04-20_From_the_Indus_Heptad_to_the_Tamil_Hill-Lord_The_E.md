## Period II: The Megalithic Culture and Pre-Caṅkam Dravidian Stratum (c. 1500–300 BCE)



### Post-Indus Archaeological Cultures



Starting around 1900 BCE, the Mature Harappan urban system began to decline and entered what Rafique Mughal (1990) termed the*Localization Era,*culminating in a catastrophic collapse. In reality, the process was gradual, regionally varied, and above all, generative. The great cities contracted, long-distance trade networks fragmented, and the standardized administrative apparatus of seals, weights, and script disappeared, but the populations did not vanish. They dispersed, reorganized into smaller agrarian settlements, and carried their religious traditions into new cultural configurations across the subcontinent.

Understanding these post-Harappan archaeological cultures is essential for any account of how the Skanda-Mātṛkā-Murukaṉ complex moved from the steatite seals of Mohenjo-daro to the megalithic dolmens of Tamil Nadu. The sixteen centuries between the abandonment of Mohenjo-daro (c. 1900 BCE) and the composition of the earliest Caṅkam poems (c. 300 BCE) or the Vedic period (c. 700 BCE) are not an archaeological void. They are densely populated by a succession of ceramic traditions, burial practices, and metallurgical horizons, each of which can be located in space, dated by radiocarbon, and interrogated for evidence of religious continuity or rupture.

## Three Faces of Late Harappan



The Localization Era (c. 1900–1300 BCE) saw the former Harappan domain fragment into three major regional ceramic traditions, each representing a distinct pathway of cultural transformation.

### Cemetery H Culture (Punjab, Haryana, Western UP; c. 1900–1300 BCE)



Named after a cemetery found in “*Area H*” at Harappa itself, this culture represents the most direct continuation of Mature Harappan traditions in the northern plains. It is regarded as a regional form of the late phase of the Harappan civilization, but also as a phase of the Indo-Aryan migrations. Mughal argued that it developed in the northern part of the Indus Valley civilization around 1700 BCE, as part of the *Punjab Phase* of the Localization Era.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!uF8_!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F83944a37-4f2a-445e-88c8-ad250f08237e_800x600.jpeg)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!uF8_!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F83944a37-4f2a-445e-88c8-ad250f08237e_800x600.jpeg)





The Cemetery H culture is distinguished by several critical innovations. The earliest *systematic* cremation practice in the subcontinent is attested in the Cemetery H culture (some Mature Harappan burials at the R-37 cemetery at Harappa show possible evidence of partial cremation, but Cemetery H is the first to make it a dominant funerary mode). The practice is also described in the Vedas; the Ṛgveda contains a reference in RV 10.15.14, where the forefathers, both cremated (*agnidagdhá*) and uncremated (*ánagnidagdha*), are invoked. The funerary urns bear painted designs of extraordinary significance, peacocks with hollow bodies and a small human form inside, interpreted as souls of the dead being conducted to the afterlife, and a hound that can be seen as the hound of Yama, the god of death. The peacock-as-psychopomp motif is particularly important for the Skanda-Murukaṉ complex, since the peacock would become Skanda’s canonical *vāhana*, and the Cemetery H urns provide the earliest attested iconographic link between peacock imagery and funerary/afterlife symbolism in South Asia. However, the gap between these Cemetery H peacock depictions and Skanda’s peacock *vāhana* (firmly established in iconography only from the 6th century CE, with numismatic evidence from Yaudheya coins of c. 2nd century BCE) spans over a millennium, and no scholarship has directly connected them in a demonstrated chain.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FKJa!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F50ab03be-ddd8-4b7f-9a64-cad04ca0fba3_1024x559.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FKJa!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F50ab03be-ddd8-4b7f-9a64-cad04ca0fba3_1024x559.png)





According to Parpola, the Cemetery H culture represents a first wave of Indo-Aryan migration from as early as 1900 BCE, which was followed by a migration to the Punjab c. 1700–1400 BCE. The Sanauli necropolis (Baghpat District, UP), with its antenna swords, chariot burials, and ceramics showing both Cemetery H and OCP affinities, demonstrates that this culture maintained a warrior-elite burial tradition with strong links to the Copper Hoard corridor. The Sanauli vehicles have been described by the excavator S. K. Manjul as “chariots,” but this identification is heavily debated. Many archaeologists argue that the vehicles are ox-carts or funeral biers rather than spoke-wheeled war chariots, and the question remains unresolved.

### Jhukar Culture (Sindh; c. 1900–1300 BCE)



Named after the type site near Mohenjo-daro, the Jhukar phase represents a Late Harappan cultural tradition that emerged in the southern region of Sindh following the decline of the mature urban phase around 1900 BCE. Excavations at Chanhu-daro (Mackay 1934–36), Amri (Casal 1959–62), and renewed work at Jhukar itself (Mughal 1973–74) established the stratigraphic sequence. Kenoyer (1998) observed that while major technological features in pottery continued, there were critical differences: the absence of writing and Harappan-style animals on seals, the increased use of circular seals with geometric designs, and the discontinuation of the chert cubical weights and stylized female figurines.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!exM3!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F97accb3c-42ae-419f-acd0-81e944f8dac8_2816x1536.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!exM3!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F97accb3c-42ae-419f-acd0-81e944f8dac8_2816x1536.png)





This disappearance of the female figurine tradition is significant for the Mātṛkā question, while the Mature Harappan urban centers produced terracotta figurines prolifically (though Clark 2017 emphatically rejects their identification as *Mother Goddess* images), the Jhukar phase abandons them. This could indicate a rupture in goddess-worship tradition, its transformation into an aniconic mode that would later characterize village goddess shrines across South Asia, or simply the loss of the urban craft specialization that produced them. The Jhukar phase transitioned into the successor Jhangar phase and ultimately into the Pirak culture (c. 1800–800 BCE), which Jarrige and Meadow’s excavations showed thrived continuously from 1800 BCE to the time of Alexander’s invasion in 325 BCE, a remarkable instance of cultural continuity spanning 1,500 years on the Kachi plains.

#### Rangpur Culture (Gujarat, Saurashtra, Kutch; c. 1900–1400 BCE)



Stage VI at Dholavira, Period IB at Surkotada, Lothal B, and Rangpur IIA & IIC exhibit a decrease in typical Harappan ceramics and the emergence of new ceramic types, such as white Painted Black-and-Red Ware (BRW), which have parallels with Ahar ceramics. Gujarat’s Late Harappan phase is characterized by the distinctive lustrous Red Ware and an increase in the total number of settlements compared to the Mature Harappan phase, indicating not collapse but rural dispersal. The presence of Jhukar-style ceramics at Dholavira and Lothal shows continued interaction between the Sindh and Gujarat regions even as urban institutions dissolved.

Gujarat’s importance for the Skanda-Mātṛkā transmission is threefold. First, the Daimabad bronze buffalo hoard (Ahmednagar district, Maharashtra, c. 1800 BCE), the most monumental Late Harappan bronze, confirms the sacred status of the buffalo in western India at this time. Second, the crocodile-worship traditions documented by Fischer and Shah (1971) in fifty tribal villages of southern Gujarat, where wooden crocodile images are mounted on poles in precise analogy with the Amri potsherd, survive in precisely the Rangpur culture zone. The third one, which I think has the most significance to this study, is the Khoḍiyār Mātā tradition of Saurashtra, the crocodile-riding goddess who is one of seven Nāga-born sisters, representing a living descendant of the divine-couple theology discussed in Period I, also attested in the Binjor amulet (4MSR, c. 3000–2600 BCE), geographically continuous with the Rangpur Late Harappan horizon.

Gujarat’s potential importance for the Skanda-Mātṛkā transmission is threefold, though each link requires qualification. First, the Daimabad bronze buffalo hoard (Ahmednagar district, Maharashtra, c. 1800 BCE), the most monumental Late Harappan Bronze Age artifact, confirms the sacred status of the buffalo in western India during this period. Second, the crocodile-worship traditions documented by Fischer and Shah (1971) in fifty tribal villages of southern Gujarat survive in the Rangpur culture zone, but whether these traditions are directly continuous with the Mature Harappan period or represent later developments cannot be determined without intermediate evidence. Third, and the most significant one related to this study, is the Khoḍiyār Mātā tradition of Saurashtra, the crocodile-riding goddess who is one of seven Nāga-born sisters, is structurally parallel to the divine-couple theology discussed in Period I and attested in the Binjor amulet (4MSR, c. 3000–2600 BCE), but the historical connection remains inferential.

## Major Cultural Corridors of the Iron Age



The faces of the late-Harappan settlements consolidated into a couple of major cultural corridors that will emerge as the critical synthesizers of cultures, evolving as late as the medieval times in South Asia.

### Ochre Coloured Pottery (OCP) Culture and the Copper Hoard Corridor (c. 2000–1500 BCE)



The Ochre Coloured Pottery (OCP) culture of the Ganga-Yamuna Doab represents the earliest post-Harappan occupation of the Indo-Gangetic plains. Its ill-fired, crumbly red-orange pottery, often disintegrating on excavation, gives the culture its name. The OCP is dated to c. 2000–1500 BCE by most archaeologists, and the critical link to the Copper Hoard assemblage was established when B. B. Lal excavated an antenna sword from OCP-stratigraphic horizons at Saipai (Etawah District, UP) in 1971, thereby securely associating the two cultures.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!u5uL!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdc1b3cd2-74da-463f-896f-eeea5d444dd6_1043x1500.jpeg)

![undefined](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!u5uL!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdc1b3cd2-74da-463f-896f-eeea5d444dd6_1043x1500.jpeg "undefined")





The Copper Hoard/OCP culture produced over 1,500 copper artifacts distributed across four geographic groups: Haryana-Rajasthan (380+ objects); Ganga-Yamuna Doab (235); Chhota Nagpur (235); and Madhya Pradesh (120). Among these are the 130+ copper anthropomorphic axes objects whose identity remains disputed. Paul Yule (University of Kiel) has established that most show no signs of use, confirming a ritualistic rather than utilitarian function. But what deity or concept they represent is far from settled. Scholars proposed identifications with Indra’s *vajra* (Krishna Kumar), Viṣṇu in his arms-akimbo posture (Maharashtra tradition), or Śani cult images (Upinder Singh). The mainstream archaeological position, summarized by Dalal (2020), is that there are more questions than ever before and fewer answers. The Kheri Gujjar theriomorph (Sonipat, Haryana, 2005), with its crocodile/boar head and proto-Brahmi script, and the Sheorajpur anthropomorph (Kanpur, UP, 1951) with its incised fish motif, demonstrate that these objects carried explicit Harappan religious symbolism, script signs, divine animals, and astral markers deep into the Gangetic plains, centuries after the urban Harappan system had collapsed.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Wyh9!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F611f303c-38f0-46df-b1aa-79fa96faf885_1920x1636.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Wyh9!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F611f303c-38f0-46df-b1aa-79fa96faf885_1920x1636.png)





The four-group distribution of Copper Hoards presents a suggestive geographic correspondence with the later distribution of the Sapta Mātṛkā cult across South Asia. The first is the northwestern frontier, followed by the Gangetic Doab heartland, extended into the eastern tribal belt of Chota Nagpur-Odisha, and the final is central India, which bridges the Doab with the Deccan megalithic zone. What the Copper Hoard distribution establishes is that a network of copper-using, ritual-object-producing communities existed across the entire corridor from the Harappan frontier to the eastern tribal belt, through which cultural content could, in principle, have traveled.

### Gandhara Grave Culture (Swat Valley, NW Pakistan; c. 1600–500 BCE)



The Gandhara Grave Culture (also called the Swat Protohistoric Graveyards Complex) occupies the northwestern gateway through which Central Asian cultural influences and possibly Indo-Aryan speakers entered South Asia. First systematically excavated in the 1960s by Pakistani archaeologist Ahmad Hasan Dani at Timargarha, and subsequently by the Italian Archaeological Mission to Pakistan (MAIP) at Aligrama, Barikot, Butkara, and Udegram, the culture is distinguished by three phases, inhumation burials in stone cists with bronze objects (early phase, c. 1600–1400 BCE); cremation burials in urns, some bearing human-face designs, with bronze and gold objects (middle phase, c. 1400–1000 BCE); and multiple burials with fractional remains alongside iron objects (late phase, c. 1000–500 BCE), coeval with the beginning of urban centers at Taxila and Charsadda.

The pottery shows links to the BMAC of Central Asia, though Parpola has argued that the Gandhara Grave Culture is by no means identical with the Bronze Age Culture of Bactria and Margiana, and Antonini and Stacul have rejected connections to the Bishkent and Vakhsh cultures of Tajikistan. Horse remains were found in at least one burial. Simply made terracotta figurines, including female figurines, were buried with the pottery. The presence of both inhumation and cremation tracks the Ṛgvedic reference (RV 10.15.14) to forefathers both cremated and uncremated. The polished black-gray pottery has been associated with that of other BMAC sites, and Parpola notes that the burial of bodies, the metal pins used to fasten clothes, and the terracotta statuettes of females are similar to those found at BMAC sites.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0W78!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3ab5cb92-9ad5-45e0-a1c1-9d42c6daa9aa_2816x1536.png)





The Gandhara Grave Culture is relevant to the Skanda-Mātṛkā question for three reasons. First, according to Kochhar, the Indo-Aryan culture fused with indigenous elements of the remnants of the Indus Valley Civilization (OCP, Cemetery H) and gave rise to the Vedic Civilization. This fusion model implies that the Vedic religion within which Skanda-Kumāra would eventually be articulated was itself a hybrid of Indo-Aryan and Harappan elements. Second, Parpola’s two-wave hypothesis assigns the Gandhara Grave/Cemetery H people to his Atharvavedic Aryans (Dāsa-Aryans, c. 1900 BCE), whose principal gods were the Aśvins and Mitra-Varuṇa, the very Varuṇa whom Parpola identifies as the Vedic successor of the Harappan crocodile-god. Third, estimates based on ancient DNA analyses suggest that the ancestors of the middle Swat valley people mixed with a population from the Inner Asian Mountain Corridor, which carried Steppe ancestry, sometime between 1900 and 1500 BCE.

## The Central Indian Chalcolithic Bridge: Ahar-Banas, Malwa, and Jorwe (c. 2000–700 BCE)



Any account of how religious traditions traveled from the Indus basin to peninsular India must reckon with the Chalcolithic cultures of central and western India. These cultures occupy the geographic space between the Late Harappan world of Gujarat and Rajasthan and the megalithic cultures of the Deccan, and they share critical ceramic, subsistence, and ritual features with both.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xfOB!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe6ffeabb-d6b1-4b95-8ca7-24026becfad7_2816x1536.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xfOB!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe6ffeabb-d6b1-4b95-8ca7-24026becfad7_2816x1536.png)





The Ahar-Banas culture (southeastern Rajasthan, c. 3000–1500 BCE) at sites like Ahar (Udaipur), Balathal, and Gilund shows direct contact with the Harappan world. Harappan ceramics, steatite beads, and terracotta figurines appear alongside the distinctive white-painted Black-and-Red ware that would become the hallmark of all subsequent Chalcolithic and Iron Age cultures southward. The Malwa culture (c. 1700–1200 BCE), centered on the Narmada basin at sites like Navdatoli, Nagda, and Kayatha, represents the next stage, a prosperous agricultural society with elaborate painted pottery, copper tools, and critically, terracotta mother-goddess figurines that persist long after they disappear from the Jhukar phase in Sindh. The Jorwe culture (c. 1400–700 BCE) of Maharashtra, at sites like Inamgaon, Daimabad, and Nevasa, provides the most direct link to the Deccan megalithic world. Jorwe pottery overlaps chronologically with the earliest Iron Age BRW of Karnataka, and the Daimabad bronze hoard was found in a Jorwe-period context.

These Chalcolithic cultures are the actual stepping stones between Gujarat’s Late Harappan phase and the Deccan megalithic tradition. Their terracotta figurines, BRW pottery, and buffalo iconography make them the strongest candidates for intermediate links in the transmission of the goddess-buffalo complex from the Indus to the South. Post-Chalcolithic cultures, the two most important cultures that gave rise to the eminent cultural complexes were the pan-Indian Black-and-Red Ware cultures and Painted Grey Ware (PGW) of the Vedic heartlands.

### Black-and-Red Ware: Pan-Indian Ceramic Tradition (c. 1500–500 BCE)



Black-and-Red Ware (BRW) is found from the Late Harappan levels of Gujarat (Rangpur, Lothal, Dholavira) through the Chalcolithic cultures of Rajasthan (Ahar-Banas) and central India (Malwa, Jorwe) to the megalithic burial sites of peninsular India (Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Kerala). Its production technique, inverted firing, which produces a black interior and a red exterior, is remarkably consistent across these vast distances.

However, the significance of BRW as a transmission marker is debated. D. K. Chakrabarti and others have argued that BRW is a production *technique*, inverted firing, that could have been independently invented in multiple regions, rather than a cultural marker of migration or diffusion. If BRW were polygenetic (arising independently in Gujarat, central India, and the South), its geographic distribution would not indicate a continuous corridor of cultural transmission. If, on the other hand, it represents a single diffusing tradition, it constitutes the most widespread ceramic link between the post-Harappan northwest and the Iron Age South. The question remains unresolved, and the argument for cultural transmission along a BRW corridor depends on the single-origin hypothesis.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!w3MN!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F782ce915-887f-404b-aa4c-ee19772d2738_2816x1536.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!w3MN!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F782ce915-887f-404b-aa4c-ee19772d2738_2816x1536.png)





Every major megalithic burial complex in South India, Adichanallur, Brahmagiri, Hallur, Maski, and Chandravalli, yields BRW alongside iron implements. If the single-tradition reading is correct, BRW establishes the ceramic bridge between the Chalcolithic cultures of central-western India and the megalithic South. The polygenesis reading is highly unlikely and points to the diffusion of a single culture that brought the shared burial practices, figurine types, or metallurgical traditions southward from the northwestern region.

### Painted Grey Ware: The Vedic Heartland (c. 1200–600 BCE)



The Painted Grey Ware (PGW) culture of the upper Ganga-Yamuna Doab and the Ghaggar-Hakra basin is widely associated with the Middle and Late Vedic period. At Bhagwanpura in the Kurukshetra district of Haryana, excavations have revealed an overlap between the late Harappan and Painted Grey Ware cultures, large houses that may have been elite residences, and fired bricks that may have been used in Vedic altars. At Alamgirpur near Delhi, a 2013 excavation revealed contiguous occupation from Early Harappan through Mature Harappan, Late Harappan, and into the earliest PGW levels, with a radiocarbon date from the transition layer of 1647 BCE (OxA-21882). Multiple sites such as Bhagwanpura, Dadheri, Nagar, Manda, Katpalon, and Madina show Late Harappan/PGW overlap, demolishing the notion of a clean break between the two.

PGW sites are concentrated in the Kuru-Pañcāla region, specifically the territory where the earliest Saṃhitā and Brāhmaṇa texts were composed. Horse remains (*Equus caballus*) have been recovered from PGW contexts at Hastinapura, Atranjikhera, and Bhagwanpura. The introduction of iron attested from c. 1200 BCE onward marks the technological shift that would transform both warfare and agriculture.

For the Skanda-Mātṛkā complex, the PGW culture represents the archaeological horizon within which the Vedic textual tradition was being composed, including the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa*’s narrative of Kumāra’s birth (ŚB 6.1.3), the *Maitrāyaṇī Saṃhitā*’s *Deva Gāyatrīs* invoking Skanda (MS 2.9.1), and the *Chāndogya Upaniṣad*’s identification of Sanatkumāra with Skanda (ChU 7.26.2). These texts, composed in the PGW cultural milieu of the Kuru-Pañcāla region (c. 900–500 BCE), represent the earliest Sanskrit articulation of a deity who may have already been worshipped under different names and in different forms by earlier populations of the same geographic region.

The question of whether the PGW/Vedic community inherited the Skanda-Mātṛkā complex from the indigenous substrate or developed it independently cannot be resolved solely from the PGW archaeological record. This will be discussed in more detail in our Period III article, part of the series.

### Northern Black Polished Ware and the Second Urbanization (c. 700–200 BCE)



The Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) culture marks the beginning of the second urbanization of South Asia, the rise of the *Mahājanapadas*, the emergence of cities like Kāśī, Kośala, Magadha, and Taxila, and the composition of the great Epic and Sūtra literature.

The second urbanization created the institutional conditions, temple-building, royal patronage, military reorganization, professional priesthoods, medical codification, etc. The NBPW period closes the archaeological loop: the same Doab region that produced Copper Hoard anthropomorphic axes (c. 2000–1500 BCE) and PGW Vedic settlements (c. 1200–600 BCE) now produces the earliest coins, texts, and sculptures explicitly depicting Skanda-Kārttikeya. The archaeological succession from OCP to PGW to NBPW provides the physical infrastructure for the textual trajectory of the emergence of religious traditions. But the religious content of the OCP phase remains archaeologically opaque; what we can trace is geographic and demographic continuity, not demonstrated religious continuity.

### The South Indian Megalithic Cultures (c. 1200–300 BCE)



The megalithic cultures of peninsular India, characterized by large stone burial monuments (dolmens, stone circles, cists, menhirs), Black-and-Red Ware pottery, and iron technology, constitute the archaeological context within which the Dravidian religious substrate of the Tamil Murukaṉ was formed. The earliest South Indian megalithic sites are Hallur (Karnataka) and Adichanallur (Tamil Nadu), dated to approximately 1000 BCE, though some sites may push back to 1200 BCE.

The megalithic cultures are not a single unified tradition but a family of regional burial practices spanning Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and parts of Maharashtra. What unites them is a set of shared features: burial of the dead, both primary inhumation and secondary urn burial, deposition of iron weapons, tools, and horse trappings, Black-and-Red Ware pottery, and, at certain sites, terracotta figurines and monumental stone sculptures. At Malampuzha (Kerala), over 40 terracotta mother-goddess figurines were recovered from megalithic contexts (c. 800 BCE). At Mudu Konaje (Dakshina Kannada, Karnataka), Prof. T. Murugeshi discovered eight terracotta figurines inside megalithic dolmens dated to pre-800 BCE, including a mother goddess figure and a bull-headed figurine with a feminine human body, explicitly linking them to the later Bhūta cult (*Daivārādhane*) of coastal Karnataka. In the Vidarbha region of Maharashtra, horses were buried with the dead, possibly after sacrifice.

The monumental Anthropomorphic Axe sculptures at Mottur, Udayarnatham, and Sittannavasal (Tamil Nadu) and Kummatti (Karnataka), dated to 800–500 BCE, are the earliest monumental art from South India. Their form, splayed limbs, and broad shoulders are strikingly similar to the copper anthropomorphs of the Doab, but rendered in massive stone using iron-tool technology. The mechanism of cultural transmission from the North to Tamil Nadu is identified in the Caṅkam literary tradition, with the Vēḷir migration bringing settled agriculture, iron technology, horses, and, finally, the religious structure.

The most direct evidence for megalithic-period goddess worship comes from Malampuzha, Kerala, where urn burials yielded approximately 40 terracotta figurines, the largest collection from any megalithic site in India, including mother goddess themes, dated to approximately 800–700 BCE. In 2023, Prof. T. Murugeshi discovered eight terracotta figurines inside megalithic dolmens at Mudu Konaje (Dakshina Kannada, Karnataka), dated to the pre-800 BCE period, the first such find in dolmens anywhere in India. These included one mother goddess figure and a bull-headed figurine with a feminine human body. Murugeshi explicitly linked these to the later Bhūta cult (Daivārādhane) of coastal Karnataka, providing a direct archaeological bridge between megalithic material culture and living goddess-worship traditions.

A Neolithic stone celt from Sembian-Kandiyur (c. 2000–1500 BCE) bearing four Indus script signs, including the *muruku* sign, partially bridges the 900-year gap between the Indus civilization’s collapse and the earliest South Indian megaliths. Hero stones (nāṭukal/vīrakal) represent another continuity vector. The Tolkāppiyam provides a six-stage guide from selecting a stone to instituting formal worship, and the archaeological record shows hero stones evolving from commemorative slabs into deified village shrines, the very process by which human dead become grāmadevatā. Karnataka alone has over 2,650 recorded hero stones, the highest concentration in India.

The Tamil war goddess Koṟṟavai occupies the pivotal position linking the Seven Sisters complex, pre-Caṅkam-period religion, and the later Mahiṣāsuramardinī iconography. In the earliest Tamil texts, she presides over the *pālai tiṇai* (arid zone), receives ritual sacrifices, and is explicitly identified as Murukaṉ’s mother. A 10th-century relief at the Nayakar Palace Art Museum, Madurai, depicts Koṟṟavai atop the beheaded buffalo demon, making the Koṟṟavai-Mahiṣāsuramardinī identification visually explicit. The Mahiṣāsuramardinī Maṇḍapa at Mamallapuram (7th century, Pallava period) represents one of the earliest major South Indian sculptural renderings of the goddess slaying the buffalo, at a site where Koṟṟavai worship was deeply rooted.

## Three Major Cultural Corridors of Diffusion



Based on the post-IVC archeological cultures described above, we can hypothesize that three principal cultural corridors contributed to cultural diffusion; in turn, the Skanda-Mātṛkā-Murukaṉ complex may have traveled along them.

- Corridor 1: The Northern Route (Punjab → Kuru-Pañcāla → Magadha)

- Corridor 2: The Western Route (Sindh-Gujarat → Ahar-Banas →
  Malwa-Jorwe → Deccan)

- Corridor 3: The Eastern-Southern Route (Doab → Chhota Nagpur →
  Vidarbha → Deccan)

The supposed dark age between the fall of the Harappan cities (c. 1900 BCE) and the rise of the historical kingdoms (c. 600 BCE) is not a gap in the archaeological record but a gap in the urban record. The post-Harappan era was characterized by rural dispersal, regional diversification, and the slow formation of new cultural syntheses, conditions under which folk-religious traditions were transmitted through village practice rather than written text. The Tamil Murukaṉ of the Caṅkam poems and the Vedic Kumāra of the Brāhmaṇa texts may share a common ancestry in the Harappan substrate, but by the time they appear in their respective textual traditions, they look profoundly different, and that difference is itself evidence of transformation rather than simple transmission. But the most remarkable feature of the religious landscape that emerges during this period is the persistence and geographic expansion of a specific cultic structure, a group of seven female deities, fierce, maternal, boundary-guarding, disease-governing, accompanied by a single male warrior-guardian who serves as their brother, protector, and ritual intermediary. This structure, attested iconographically on the Mohenjo-daro seal M-1186 (c. 2600–1900 BCE) as discussed in Period I and echoed in living village worship from Sindh to Tamil Nadu, provides the most compelling evidence for the deep indigenous substrate out of which the Tamil Murukaṉ and his Mātṛkā-mothers would eventually emerge.

### Regional Variants of Sapta-Mātṛkā Complex



Despite enormous variation in goddess names, origin myths, and ritual details, by the end of the Iron Age or at the early stages of the second urbanization, the seven sister goddesses with a single male guardian emerged with striking consistency across at least ten distinct regions of South Asia.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-UVa!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5d49f3c5-72f2-4990-be65-09961afc118e_2816x1536.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-UVa!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5d49f3c5-72f2-4990-be65-09961afc118e_2816x1536.png)





In Tamil Nadu, *Ēḻu Kaṉṉimār* (ஏழு கன்னிமார், Seven Maidens, Tamil *ēḻu* = seven; *kaṉṉi* = virgin, maiden, young woman) are documented in detail by Brenda E.F. Beck (*Religions of South Asia* 13, 2021), who describes them as seven look-alike goddesses affiliated with the Pleiades in the Kongu region, a complex of village and regional goddesses attested in Caṅkam literature, *Cilappatikāram*, and as a living ritual practice. The specific names of the seven vary considerably by region, village, and caste community. There is no canonical pan-Tamil list comparable to the Sanskrit Sapta Mātṛkā roster. For instance, at Perunthalaiyur (Erode), they are Sumathi, Kowmaari, Maheshwari, Rajakanni, Vaishnavi, Pattakari, and Shubitra. The guardian figure is typically Karuppaswamy (the fierce black-skinned deity with a machete and a white horse) or Ayyanar.

The early *Caṅkam* corpus contains critical early evidence for plural goddess worship in Tamil culture. Though the term *Ēḻu Kaṉṉimār* as a fixed compound may not appear in the earliest stratum, *Koṟṟavai* was the dominant individual goddess figure in the *Caṅkam* corpus mentioned in the *Tolkāppiyam* as the deity of the *pālai* landscape. Later in *Cilappatikāram*, *Vañci Kāṇṭam* dealt with the cult of *Kaṇṇaki’s* apotheosis as a fierce goddess*, Āycciyar Kuravai* from Maturai Kāṇṭam explains the ritual choreography associated with Koṟṟavai-type goddess worship, and Kāṭukāṇ Kātai describes ecstatic ritual dancing associated with fierce goddess worship. The sevenfold schema of village goddesses becomes more prominent in medieval and early modern Tamil literature and inscriptions. The substantial differentiator within the Caṅkam corpus is that Koṟṟavai is identified as Murukaṉ's mother.

In Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, the Sapta-Mātṛkā are identified as even village Ammaṉs (mother goddesses), *Pōlēramma* (పోలేరమ్మ), *Aṅkamma* (అంకమ్మ), *Mutyālamma* (ముత్యాలమ్మ), *Pōcamma* (పోచమ్మ), *Baṅgāramma* (బంగారమ్మ), *Māremma* (మారెమ్మ), and *Ellamma*/*Rēṇuka* (ఎల్లమ్మ/రేణుక). They were often identified with a fierce male deity, named *Pōturāju* (పోతురాజు), who serves as the guardian-brother, and his name translates as “buffalo king” (pōtu = buffalo, rāju = king). W.T. Elmore’s fieldwork in the Nellore District (1915) and Henry Whitehead’s observations in Kuddapah and Kurnool (1921) provide independent confirmation of this grouping, though the specific goddess names differ between districts. *Pōturāju*is often stationed at the village boundary as the guardian deity (*sīmā rakṣaka*). In animal sacrifice rituals, he receives the first blood offering before the main goddess is propitiated, and he is conceptualized as carrying out the actual killing that the goddess demands but cannot perform directly. The four-day ritual for *Pōlēramma* is particularly significant: it culminates in a water buffalo sacrifice on the final day, with blood mixed with rice and scattered around the village, directly echoing the buffalo-combat scene of M-312 and the *Mahiṣāsuramardinī* narrative. Iconographically,*Poturāju*is typically depicted carrying a whip,*a coḷḷu,*or a similar object. During the annual Bonalu festival, men impersonating *Pōturāju* lead processions bare-bodied, wielding whips.

*Siri Pāḍdana* (ಸಿರಿ ಪಾಡ್ದನ) of Tulu Nadu, a 15,683-line oral epic enshrining the Ēḷver Sirikuḷu (ಏಳ್ವೆರ್ ಸಿರಿಕುಳು) or Seven Siris comprising Siri, Sonne, Ginde, Abbaga, Daraga, Daaru, and Kumara, six women across three matrilineal generations together with Siri’s son Kumara as the sole male among the seven, installed as a group at Adi Alade shrines like Periyadka, Hiriadka, and Nandalike. Presiding over this female-centered pantheon is Bermer (ಬೆರ್ಮೆರ್), the ancestral spirit-god and senior male guardian of Tulu Nadu, whose *guṇḍa* shrines stand at *nāga-bana*, *āḷade*, and *garoḍi* sites. The close parallel of seven-fold female core, one internal male brother/son guardian deity, equinox-window timing, possession as the operative ritual idiom, and matrilineal social embedding has led most South Indian folklorists (Peter Claus, Lauri Honko, Heidrun Brückner, B. A. Viveka Rai, Chinnappa Gowda, Yogitha Shetty) to treat Tulu *bhūtārādhane* and Tamil Amman worship as regional variants of a single pre-agamic Dravidian goddess substrate, itself plausibly continuous via Parpola’s reading of the Harappan seven-figure processional seal M-1186 with the Indus Valley iconography of the Sapta Mātṛkā lineage.

Maharashtra’s Sati Asara, or Sāt Asara (seven water-nymphs), includes *Campāvatī*, *Śītalā*, *Ekvīra*, *Padmāvatī*, *Kalalagi*, *Hiṃgalāja*, and *Caturaśṛṃgī* in the Konkan region, with each goddess assigned to a separate village. Their brother is *Mhasobā* (chief of buffaloes), worshipped as an aniconic red-lead-coated stone. The Mumbai Kolī community identifies their seven as *Muṃbādevī*, *Caṃḍikādevī*, *Mahālakṣmī*, Śītalāmātā, Kālabādevī, Jarī Mārī Mātā, and Chedadevī. D. D. Kosambi (*Myth and Reality*) famously argued that *Mhasobā* is the same entity as *Mahiṣāsura*, citing the spatial arrangement in Pune where a *Mhasobā* sits at the foot of the *Pārvatī* hill temple. The semantic equivalence between *Poturāju* and *Mhasobā*, both names meaning “lord/chief of buffaloes,” links the Telugu and Marathi traditions directly to the buffalo-sacrifice complex seen in M-312 and the Banawali seal.

Goa’s seven sisters, *Kel̤abāī*, *Mhāmāī*, *Laīrāī*, *Mīrābāī*, *Morajāī*, *Śītalāī*, and *Ādiyādīpā* arrived with brother *Kheṭobā* (from *Kṣetrapāla*, “field protector”) riding an elephant from the Western Ghats. Their dispersal across different villages after a quarrel explains why each goddess has her own temple. Remarkably, the Portuguese converted Mīrābāī’s temple into the Church of Our Lady of Miracles (*Milagres*), yet oil and jasmine flowers are still exchanged between the Laīrāī temple and the Milagres church during their respective festivals, one of the most striking examples of syncretic continuity in South Asia.

In Odisha, the Satvaheni are uniquely characterized as working women of low birth who achieved tantric prowess: Nitei Dhobani (laundress), Jnanadei Maluni (garland maker), Gangi Gauduni (jaggery maker), Sua Teluni (oil extractor), Luhukuti Luhurani (ironsmith), Sukuti Chamaruni (shoemaker), and Patrapindhi Saharuni. Their caste-professional surnames represent a strikingly egalitarian variant of the Seven Sisters motif. The Vaitala Deula in Bhubaneswar and the Saptamātṛkā temple at Jajpur preserve sculptural traditions linking them to the Tantric princess tradition. Odisha’s location at the eastern terminus of the Copper Hoard distribution (Group 3: Chhota Nagpur, 235 objects) suggests the seven-mother complex reached eastern India through the same cultural corridor that carried the copper anthropomorphs.

The equivalent of the Sapta Mātṛkā complex in Gujarat is distinctly serpentine. The seven sisters,*Āval*,*Jogala*,*Togal*,*Holabāī*,*Bījabāī*,*Sosāī*, and*Khoḍiyār*(birth name*Jānabāī*), with their brother*Meharaka,*are born as Nāga (cobras who transform into human children). According to *Khoḍiyār* folklore, when *Meharaka* was bitten by a snake, *Khoḍiyār* journeyed to the Nāga Lōka for amṛta, was injured on the return, and rode a crocodile home; hence her name (*khoḍi* = limping) and her crocodile mount. The Nāga origin distinguishes this variant from the buffalo-associated traditions further south.

In Rajasthan, the Satobahin (seven sisters) are a collective without fixed individual names, accompanied by brother Bhairu (from Bhairava). Helen Lambert (*JASO* 25(1), 1994) conducted the definitive ethnography, documenting that brides receive a silver amulet depicting the seven goddesses and their brother, mothers paint seven henna dots on children’s hands during Shravan, and the Satobahin are conceived as “homeless” roaming goddesses who circulate at dawn, dusk, and midday.

In Punjab and Haryana, the seven sister goddesses are Phul Mata, Sitala Mata, Badi Mata, Pansahi Mata, Gusulia Mata, Kankar Mata, and Malbal. Notably, Phul Mata is specifically invoked in cases of typhoid fever in children. Parents place an earthen pot filled with cool water and flowers near the affected child while burning incense, a ritual believed to “cool her fury.” This pediatric-disease-governance function directly parallels the *Skandagraha* / *Bālagraha* tradition documented in the *Suśrutasaṃhitā* and *Kāśyapasaṃhitā*, where Skanda and his Mātṛkā-mothers govern childhood diseases.

In the Chamba district of Himachal Pradesh, the seven sister goddesses, Lakshana Devi, Shakti Devi, Chamunda Devi, Ambika Devi, Jalpa Devi, Bhadal Devi, and Ava Devi are the subject of a local folklore documented by researcher S. L. Harnot: a childless couple blessed by Devi Hirma with seven daughters, who were consumed by a demoness near the Ravi River but re-emerged as deities, dispersing to preside over different villages, a narrative that precisely mirrors the dispersal-after-quarrel motif seen in the Goan tradition.

In the Sindh region, Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai’s 18th-century Sufi masterwork,*Shah Jo Risalo,*celebrates the Sat Surmiyon (Seven Heroic Queens) Marui, Momal, Sassui, Noori, Sohni, Sorath, and Lila as allegories of the soul seeking divine union. The seven-number motif persists, but the male figures become lovers rather than brother-guardians, and the protective/disease-governance function transforms into a Sufi mystical framework. The connection to the IVC is geographically intimate: Pakistani archaeologist Parveen Talpur has identified Satiyan jo Astaan (Shrine of Seven Women), a sacred site on an island in the Indus near Mohenjo-daro, as a possible living descendant of the cult depicted on M-1186.

### The Brother Guardian as Structural Homolog of Skanda-Murukaṉ



The male figure accompanying the seven sisters undergoes a fascinating regional transformation while retaining core structural features. *Pōturāju* (Telugu, buffalo king), *Mhasobā* (Marathi, buffalo chief), *Kheṭobā* (Konkani, from *Kṣetrapāla*), *Bhairu* (Rajasthani, from Bhairava), *Karuppusāmi* and/or Ayyanār (Tamil, the black lord/chief guardian), and *Meharaka* (Gujarati) all serve as protectors, boundary watchers, and ritual intermediaries. Whitehead observed in 1921 that *Poturāju* figures sometimes as the brother, sometimes as the husband, of the village goddess, and sometimes as merely an attendant, but “sacrifice is never offered to him alone.

The brother guardian figure is often represented as a sacrificial pillar or staff (*kantu* or *kampam*). Madeleine Biardeau (*Stories about Posts*, 2004) demonstrated that the sacrificial post representing the “little brother” connects to Vedic *yūpa* (sacrificial stake) symbolism, precisely paralleling the *sthāṇu* (ritual post) prescribed in the *Dhūrtakalpa* for the *Skandayāga*.

The semantic equivalence between Pōturāju and Mhasobā is particularly significant; both names mean lord/chief of buffaloes, linking the guardian figure directly to the buffalo-sacrifice complex. In ritual practice, Pōturāju is typically represented by nothing more than a wooden post of sami wood or a stick in the ground, while Mhasobā appears as an aniconic stone coated in red lead. Madeleine Biardeau (*Stories about Posts*, 2004) demonstrated that the sacrificial post representing the “little brother” connects to Vedic *yūpa* (sacrificial stake) symbolism, precisely paralleling the *sthāṇu* (ritual post) prescribed in the *Dhūrtakalpa* for the Skandayāga.

The connection between the village brother-guardian and the Sanskritic Skanda-Kārttikeya closes an important circle. In the Mahābhārata’s *Āraṇyakaparvan*, Indra sends the mothers of the world to kill the newborn Skanda, but they are overcome with maternal feeling and nurse him instead. Skanda then adopts them as his mothers and grants them power to torment children younger than sixteen and then act as their protectors (Mann 2012: 39). In Tamil Caṅkam literature, the relationship is inverted; Koṟṟavai is explicitly Murukaṉ’s mother, not vice versa. The *Tirumurukāṟṟuppaṭai* addresses Murukaṉ as “Thou son of Koṟṟavai!” (Zvelebil 1973: 129–130).

The structural homology is unmistakable: seven female deities + one male warrior deity, governing disease, fertility, and boundaries. Whether we call him Poturāju, Mhasoba, Khetoba, Bhairu, or Skanda-Murukaṉ, the brother-guardian occupies the same structural position. The Sanskritic transformation was to elevate this village guardian to the rank of *Senāpati,* which we will discuss in Period III. But the village substratum, where the brother stands at the boundary with his whip or his *vēl*, persists unchanged.

## The Buffalo Sacrifice Hypothesis



The buffalo occupies a paradoxical position in this religious complex. On the one hand, the brother-guardian is himself a buffalo deity, as seen in *Mhasobā*, meaning buffalo chief, *Poturāju*, meaning buffalo king, and the IVC Paśupati seal (M-304), which wears buffalo horns, not bull horns, as Hiltebeitel (*Anthropos*73:5-6, 1978) demonstrated. On the other hand, the goddess fights and kills the buffalo in the M-312 seal, in the Poleramma *jātara* ritual, and in the canonical *Devī Māhātmya* narrative of Durgā slaying Mahiṣāsura.

Kosambi, Biardeau, Hiltebeitel, and Parpola have proposed a three-stage transformation. First, an original stratum in which the buffalo was a positive, protective deity, evidenced by Mhasobā’s continued worship, the Daimabad bronze buffalo, and pastoral communities’ veneration. Second, a ritual elaboration in which the act of sacrificing a buffalo to the goddess became narrativized as the goddess slaying the buffalo-demon. Third, the demonization of the buffalo-guardian, in which Sanskritization transformed the revered buffalo deity into the demon Mahiṣāsura while assimilating the local goddess into Purāṇic Durgā. The continued worship of Mhasoba and Poturāju as benevolent brother-guardians preserves the pre-demonization layer.

## The Substrate Beneath the Silence



The sixteen centuries separating the abandonment of Mohenjo-daro (c. 1900 BCE) from the composition of the earliest Caṅkam poems (c. 300 BCE) have long been characterized as a dark age, a gap in urban civilization during which no continuity of religious thought could plausibly have survived. What the archaeological record shows is not absence but dispersal, not collapse but regional diversification under new ecological, technological, and demographic pressures. The Cemetery H peacock-urns of Harappa, the Jhukar sealings of Sindh, the Rangpur lustrous red ware of Saurashtra, the Copper Hoard anthropomorphs of the Gangetic Doab, the Sanauli necropolis, the Ahar-Banas, Malwa, and Jorwe Chalcolithic sites, the Gandhara Grave cremations of Swat, the pan-Indian spread of Black-and-Red Ware, the Painted Grey Ware villages of Kuru-Pañcāla, and finally the megalithic dolmens of Adichanallur, Mudu Konaje, and Malampuzha constitute a continuous lattice of material culture through which religious content could, and demonstrably did, travel.

Three broad arguments that I would propose and discuss as evidence for the dispersal, first, the post-Harappan fragmentation produced at least three major cultural corridors, the northern Punjab–Kuru-Pañcāla–Magadha route, the western Sindh-Gujarat–Ahar-Banas–Deccan route, and the eastern Doab–Chhota Nagpur–Vidarbha route, along which iconographic and ritual content flowed into the emerging Iron Age cultural landscape. The geographic correspondence between the distribution of the Copper Hoard and the later Sapta Mātṛkā cult zones is suggestive, even if not yet demonstrated. Second, the disappearance of the Harappan terracotta figurine tradition from Jhukar Sindh coexists with its persistence in Malwa, Jorwe, Malampuzha, and Mudu Konaje, indicating not a rupture in goddess-worship but a geographic shift of the iconographic center of gravity from the Indus basin to central India and the peninsular south. The bull-headed female figurine from Mudu Konaje (pre-800 BCE), explicitly linked by Murugeshi to the later Tulu bhūta cult, closes the thousand-year gap that earlier scholarship had treated as unbridgeable. The emergence of*Koṟṟavai*as one of the chief deities in the Tamil landscape, with her being referred to as the mother of Murukaṉ, is crucial. Third, and most consequentially, a single cultic structure, a group of seven fierce, maternal, boundary-guarding, disease-governing female deities accompanied by a single male warrior-guardian functioning as brother, protector, and ritual intermediary, emerges at the end of the Iron Age with startling consistency across at least ten distinct regions: Tamil Ēḻu Kaṉṉimār with Karuppusāmi-Ayyanār, Telugu Sapta-Mātṛkā village forms with Pōturāju, Tulu Ēḷver Sirikuḷu with Bermer, Marathi Sāt Asara with Mhasobā, Konkani-Goan seven sisters with Kheṭobā, Odisha’s Satvaheni with their tantric brother, Gujarati Nāga-born sisters with Meharaka, Rajasthani Satobahin with Bhairu, Chamba’s seven devis, and Punjab’s Phul Mata group. The structural invariance across linguistic, caste, and ecological diversity, coupled with the iconographic echo of the seven-figure procession on M-1186, constitutes the most compelling available evidence for a deep indigenous substrate out of which both the village *grāmadevatā* and the Sanskritic Sapta Mātṛkā emerged.
