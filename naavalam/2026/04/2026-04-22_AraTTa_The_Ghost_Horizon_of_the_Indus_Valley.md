# Āraṭṭa: The Ghost Horizon of the Indus Valley

### Tracing the Ancient City Across Mesopotamia and Vedic Traditions

Āraṭṭa (Sanskrit: आरट्ट) is one of the prehistoric Indian tribes mentioned in the Mahābhārata, the Mahāvaṃsa, and the Aṣṭādhyāyī of Pāṇini (Sanskrit: पाणिनि). V. S. Agrawala notes in his *India as Known to Pāṇini* that Ariṣṭapura (Pāli: Ariṭṭapura) was a city in the kingdom of Śivi within the Vāhīka region. The significance of exploring this ancient toponym opens up a rich field of inquiry into the polity and cultural landscape of north-western India, where Vedic (vaidika) and non-Vedic kingdoms (janapada) and republics (gaṇapada) coexisted and shaped the subcontinent’s early history.

The name “Aratta” also appears in Sumerian literary tradition dating to the late fourth and early third millennia BCE, raising the possibility of long-range cultural or commercial contact between Mesopotamia and the Indus region. This article seeks to trace the attestations of Āraṭṭa across both traditions, evaluate the archaeological evidence, and propose tentative linguistic connections that may illuminate the pre-history of the Indus Valley Civilization (IVC).

## Pāṇinian Geography and the Vāhīka Country



According to Pāṇini’s grammatical rules and their commentarial traditions, the Vāhīka country was divided into three principal subdivisions: Kekaya, Uśīnara, and Madra, with a fourth division, Śavasa, added at a later stage. Pāṇini enumerated sixteen mahājanapadas of his era, alongside numerous smaller janapadas. Patañjali (Sanskrit: पतञ्जलि; fl. c. 150 BCE), in his Mahābhāṣya (Sanskrit: महाभाष्य), identifies Śākala as a “Vāhīka-grāma.” In the Mahābhārata, Śākala, generally identified with modern Sialkot in Pañjāb, is mentioned as the chief city of the Madra kingdom, situated on the banks of the Āpagā river.

Neither Pāṇini nor Patañjali provides an etymology for Vāhīka, but Kātyāyana (Sanskrit: कात्यायन; fl. c. 3rd century BCE), who composed the Vārttika on Pāṇini’s Aṣṭādhyāyī, derives Vāhīka from the Sanskrit *bahis* (बहिस्), meaning ‘outside,’ with the suffix -īkak. This etymology aligns with the characterization in the Mahābhārata, where Vāhīka is depicted as the land of the five rivers lying outside the sphere of Āryan dharmic practice *(dharma-bāhya)*.

## The Karṇaparvan Testimony



The most extensive description of Vāhīka and Āraṭṭa occurs in the Karṇaparvan (Book 8) of the Mahābhārata. In Karṇaparva, 44.7.28-37 (Critical Edition: VIII.30.36–40), Karna delivers a scathing geographical and moral indictment of the Āraṭṭā and Vāhīka regions to psychologically undermine King Shalya by insulting his homeland's customs. He specifically identifies this territory as the land of the six rivers: theŚatadru(Sutlej),Vipāśā(Beas),Irāvatī(Ravi),Candrabhāgā(Chenab),Vitastā(Jhelum), and theSindhu(Indus), the sixth. Karna characterizes the inhabitants as outcasts (*vrātyas*) who have abandoned Vedic Dharma (*naṣṭadharmān*), claiming they consume "impure" foods such as barley meal (*śaktu*), fish, and the flesh of cattle (*gavyam*), and indulge in intoxicants (*guḍa-madhu*) and wild, "rākṣasa-like" revelry. Because they do not perform traditional sacrifices (*ayajvanām*), he asserts that their offerings are rejected by the devas, pitṛs, and brāhmaṇas, concluding that any righteous person should strictly avoid traveling to these spiritually "perished" lands.

## The Periplus Maris Erythraei



The Periplus Maris Erythraei (c. 40–70 CE), an anonymous Greco-Roman navigational guide, records in Section 47: “The country inland from **Barygaza** is inhabited by numerous tribes, such as the **Arattii**, the **Arachosii**, the **Gandaraei**, and the people of **Poclais**.” The **Arattii** (Greek: *Αραττιοι*) are thus placed in the hinterland of **Bharukaccha** (modern Bharuch, Gujarat), in proximity to the **Arachosians** (**Arachoṣī**) and **Gandhārāns** (**Gāndhāras**).

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!M_Mz!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fbucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcc039a2e-bdac-4f26-961c-b19a57eefa15_800x108.png)

Wilfred Schoff identified these **Arattii** with the **Āraṭṭas** of Sanskrit literature, a connection supported by the phonological correspondence and the geographical context of the **Pañjāb**-Sindh region. Lionel Casson’s 1989 critical edition corroborates this identification, placing the **Arattii** firmly within the northwestern tribal geography known from Indian sources.

## Aratta in Sumerian: The Matter of Aratta Cycle



The “Matter of Aratta” is a cycle of four Sumerian epic poems composed during the Ur III period (c. 2112–2004 BCE), preserved in early post-Sumerian copies from the Old Babylonian period. The cycle comprises: (1) *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta*; (2) *Enmerkar and En-suhgir-ana*; (3) *Lugalbanda in the Mountain Cave*; and (4) *Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird*. These poems represent some of the longest literary compositions in Sumerian, with *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta* alone extending to approximately 640 lines.

The epic describes the rivalry between Enmerkar, the second king of the First Dynasty of Uruk (Sumerian: Unug-Kulaba; cuneiform: 𒐕𒌶 unug^(ki)) per the Sumerian King List, and the unnamed ruler of Aratta, a fabulously wealthy city located beyond mountain ranges to the east. Enmerkar desires precious metals, gemstones, and especially lapis lazuli, materials not available in the alluvial plain of Sumer, for the embellishment of temples dedicated to the goddess Inanna at Uruk and to Enki at Eridu.

The narrative is notable for several features of literary-historical significance: the description of long-distance messenger diplomacy, the riddle-contest between rulers, the so-called “Incantation of Nudimmud” (which describes a primordial linguistic unity among the lands of Shubur, Ḭamazi, Sumer, Uri-ki, and the Martu land), and, most remarkably, the invention of writing on clay tablets when the messenger’s oral memory proves insufficient to convey increasingly complex messages.

The identification of Aratta with a specific archaeological site remains one of the great unresolved problems of ancient Near Eastern studies. The excavations at Tall-i-Malyan, led by William Sumner in the 1970s, confirmed the location of Anšan,and this, in turn, narrowed the search area for Aratta, which, according to Sumerian texts, lies east of Anšan, beyond multiple mountain ranges. Several locations have been proposed by scholars:

- **Southwestern Iran (Luristān):** Samuel N. Kramer proposed modern-day
  Luristān, largely based on the Sumerian narrative's general eastward
  orientation.

- **Shahr-i-Sokhta (Sīstān):** Georgina Herrmann identified Aratta with
  Shahr-i-Sokhta based on its role in the lapis lazuli trade network
  connecting Central Asia to Mesopotamia.

- **Hamadān-Nahavand-Kermānshāh-Sanandaj:** Sol Cohen suggested an
  integrated region in the central Zagros in his 1973 dissertation.

- **Kermān Province and Jīroft:** Yousef Majidzadeh argued against the
  Shahr-i-Sokhta identification and directed attention to the Kermān
  region, subsequently proposing the Jīroft site (*Konar Sandal)* as a
  candidate for Aratta. The Jīroft archaeological culture, dating to the
  early Bronze Age (late 3rd millennium BCE), features monumental
  architecture, elaborate chlorite vessels, and evidence of
  long-distance trade, all of which are consistent with the literary
  descriptions of Aratta’s wealth. However, this identification has met
  with scepticism from scholars such as Oscar Muscarella, and the
  question remains open.

An earlier proposal locating Aratta near Lake Urmīya in north-western Iran has been largely abandoned, as it was based on an incorrect translation of Mount Hurom in the Sumerian text.

## The Wood Catalogue: A Linguistic Excursus



In one remarkable passage of the epic, the emissary of King Enmerkar demonstrates the magnificence of the royal sceptre. The king of Aratta responds that the sceptre of Uruk is not fashioned from any single wood, and proceeds to enumerate a catalogue of timbers. Sol Cohen’s translation renders these as: **adāru**-wood, **kanaktu**-wood, **cedar**, **cypress**, **ḥašḫur-cypress**, **ušu**-wood, chariot-poplar, and **tuḥallu**-wood.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vFFJ!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fbucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc1f6e0e9-dcfc-4bdd-baef-5c35e9721d4b_1486x1338.png)

The relevant Sumerian and Akkadian correspondences for the principal woods are as follows. The Sumerian *eren* (Akkadian: erēnu) designates ‘cedar.’ The Sumerian *šurmen* (Akkadian: šurmīnu) denotes ‘cypress.’ The Sumerian *ḥašḥur* (Akkadian: ḥašḥūru) refers to ‘apple (tree).’ The Sumerian *asal* (Akkadian: ṣarbatu) signifies ‘poplar.’ The Sumerian *esi* (Akkadian: ušû) denotes a particular tree species.

### The Adāru and Kanaktu Problem



Of special interest are the first two woods in the catalogue. The Akkadian *adāru* corresponds to the Sumerian *ildag* (‘a poplar’), while the Akkadian *kanaktu* corresponds to the Sumerian *šimgig* (‘an aromatic tree’). The etymology of *adāru* was initially suspected to be a Sumerian loan into Akkadian, but subsequent analysis has not provided decisive evidence for this. Similarly, *kanaktu* was compared with a West Chadic term \*kunak (‘shea nut tree’), though this proposal too remained uncertain.

An alternative etymology for *kanaktu:*I would like to propose a possible loanword from the Dravidian language family: cf. Tulu *kaṇaků* (‘fuel, firewood’), Telugu *kaṇika* (‘a stick’), Kannada *kaṇike* / *kaṇuku* (‘stalk or stem’), Kurukh *kaṅk*, and Malto *kanku* (‘wood’). The Indus-Valley people indeed maintained trade relations with Mesopotamia, and loanwords from a Proto-Dravidian layer into the Akkadian commercial vocabulary would not be implausible.

## Aratta and Āraṭṭa - Convergence or Coincidence?



The central question that emerges from this article is whether the Sumerian Aratta and the Indian Āraṭṭa refer, at some deep historical level, to the same region or population, or whether the phonological similarity is coincidental.

The Sumerian literary tradition dates the rivalry with Aratta to the era of Enmerkar, conventionally placed c. 3400–3100 BCE. This pre-Bronze Age kingdom was described as wealthy, controlling precious metals, and dominating the lapis lazuli trade. Centuries later, the Late Iron Age Vedic literature describes a non-Vedic republic dwelling near modern Pañjāb, within the broader Vāhīka region, whose inhabitants were scorned as cultureless and impure by the Kuru-Pañcāla Āryans.

If the current archaeological consensus placing Sumerian Aratta in the Jīroft region of Kermān is correct, the question becomes: could there have been a migration of populations from southeastern Iran to the Indus Valley during or after the Early Bronze Age? Such a migration could explain both the transfer of the toponym and the cultural memory preserved in later Indian literature. The Jīroft culture, with its sophisticated chlorite-vessel industry, its connections to Mesopotamian trade, and its location intermediate between Elam and the Indus Valley, represents a plausible “missing link” in such a scenario.

The Sumerian epic describes Aratta as an epithet for ‘glory’ and ‘abundance’ while the Mahābhārata condemns Āraṭṭa as naṣṭa-dharmic and aśuci presents a striking arc of cultural decline, one that might be correlated with the well-documented deurbanization of the Indus Valley after c. 1900 BCE, compounded by the cultural pressures accompanying the Indo-Aryan expansion into the Kuru-Pañcāla region.

These remain, however, speculative hypotheses. But the study of Āraṭṭa, across Sumerian epic, Vedic polemic, Purāṇic genealogy, and Greco-Roman geography, reveals a toponym of remarkable longevity and cross-cultural resonance.
