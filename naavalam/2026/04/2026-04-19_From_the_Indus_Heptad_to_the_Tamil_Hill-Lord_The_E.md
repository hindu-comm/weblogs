# From the Indus Heptad to the Tamil Hill-Lord: The Evolution of Murukaṉ - Part I

### Tracing the transformation of a Bronze Age spirit into the sovereign deity of the Sangam landscape

The deity known today as *Murukaṉ* (Tamil: முருகன்) occupies a unique position in the history of South Asian religion. While mainstream Hindu theology identifies him with the pan-Indian *Skanda-Kārttikeya*, son of Śiva, it is argued that Murukaṉ's emergence as a Tamil god was an independent origin, with a pre-Sanskritic Tamil identity that predates his assimilation into the Brahmanical pantheon. This article examines the parallel evolution of Murukaṉ and Skanda-Kārttikeya through the lens of textual, archaeological, linguistic, and ethnographic evidence, as well as the mechanisms by which the two originally distinct traditions were synthesized.

## Introduction: The Problem of Identity



The question “Is Murukaṉ a Tamil god?” may appear straightforward to devotees across Tamil Nadu, Sri Lanka, and the global Tamil diaspora, for whom Murukaṉ is the pre-eminent deity of Tamil religious consciousness. Yet the question is deeply contested in academic scholarship, precisely because the figure known variously as Vēlaṉ (*வேலன்),* Murukaṉ *(முருகன்*), Skanda, Kārttikeya (*कार्त्तिकेय*), Kumāra (*कुमार*), Mahāsena (*महासेन*), Ṣaṇmukha (*षण्मुख*), and Subrahmaṇya (*सुब्रह्मण्य*) straddles two distinct cultural and linguistic worlds. One tradition is embedded in the Dravidian-language ecology of South India, attested in the oldest surviving Tamil literary corpus; the other belongs to the Sanskrit textual universe of the *Vedas*, the *Mahābhārata*, the *Purāṇas*, and the ritual literature of the *Atharvaveda*. The relationship between these two traditions, whether one is the original and the other is derivative, whether they represent parallel developments from a common substratum, or whether they were always the same deity under different names, is one of the central problems in South Indian religious history.

No other deity of the Hindu pantheon so clearly embodies the convergence of multiple, originally distinct cultic streams, Dravidian folk religion, Vedic ritual, tribal martial worship, Āyurvedic, Iranian imperial theology, and Brahmanical courtly ideology. Richard D. Mann, in his landmark study *The Rise of Mahāsena: The Transformation of Skanda-Kārttikeya in North India from the Kuṣāṇa to Gupta Empires* (2012), demonstrated that the deity’s earliest textual persona is that of a *graha*, a seizer or malignant spirit associated with the affliction of pregnant women and young children, and that the transformation from this *graha* figure into the divine *senāpati* (commander-in-chief) represents the defining theological shift in the deity’s history.

This article analyzes the historicity of the evolution and emergence of what we know as*Murukaṉ*in the Tamil country, drawing on converging evidence from multiple disciplines. The article analyzes the evolution of*Murukaṉ*,**or*Skanda-Kārttikeya,*across six periods, from the Bronze Age Indus Civilization to medieval prominence under the Gupta empire and the various Southern kingdoms.

  1500–300 BCE)

- Period III: The Vedic Period - Saṃhitā, Brāhmaṇa, and Upaniṣadic
  Evidence (c. 1200–300 BCE)

- Period IV: Post-Vedic, Caṅkam, and Purāṇic Formations (c. 4th cent.
  BCE – 4th cent. CE)

- Period V: The Historical Period - Numismatic, Epigraphic, and
  Archaeological Evidence

- Period VI: Later Developments - Gupta Consolidation, Śaiva Absorption,
  and Southern Survival

## Period I: Proto-Historic and Indus Valley Antecedents (c. 2600–1500 BCE)



### The M-1186 Seal and Proto-Saptamātṛkā



The question of whether Skanda-Kārttikeya’s origins can be traced back to the Indus Valley Civilization (IVC) remains among the most debated in Indian religious history. The most important IVC seal for the Mātṛkā-Skanda debate is a steatite seal, designated M-1186 from Mohenjo-daro, depicting a deity with a horned headdress standing inside a *pīpal* (*Ficus religiosa*) tree, a kneeling worshipper, a severed human head on a small stool, a giant ram, and seven figures in procession wearing single-plumed headdresses, long braids (*ēka-vēṇī*), bangles, and long skirts, the earliest iconographic evidence for a proto-Saptamātṛkā grouping. An eighth figure stands detached from the row, within the tree itself. Their female character is suggested by what Parpola describes as the one long plait of hair, which to the present day has remained characteristic of Indian women (*Visible Religion* 6, 1988). Additional seals and amulets from Mohenjo-daro also display rows of six or seven human figures.

J. N. Tiwari (*Goddess Cults in Ancient India*, *1985*) argued that the Mātṛkā cult was traceable to the IVC, citing these seals as evidence. Madhu Bazaz Wangu (*Images of Indian Goddesses*, 2003) concurred, believing Matrika's description in the Mahābhārata is rooted in the group of seven females depicted on Indus Valley seals. Mahadev Chakravarti (*The Concept of Rudra-Śiva Through the Ages*, Motilal Banarsidass, 1986) and Dilip Chakrabarti supported similar positions. Harper noted that the heptad recurs so frequently on IVC material that it implies heavy significance of the number seven in Indus culture (Harper, p. 6).

The chronological gap between IVC seals (c. 2600–1900 BCE) and the first clear sculptural representations of Mātṛkās in the Kuṣāṇa period (1st–3rd c. CE) spans over two millennia, and the earliest epigraphic reference to Mātṛkās is the Gangadhara inscription of Viśvavarman (c. 423–424 CE). Some scholars cite the Chebrolu inscription (Andhra Pradesh, c. 207 CE, Sātavāhana period) as earlier. But the earliest textual evidence of a seven-mother complex comes from Ṛgveda IX.102.4 and I.141.2. The Saptamātṛkā were not depicted as warrior goddesses (Brāhmī, Vaiṣṇavī, etc.) in later Hinduism, but were described as those who give birth to or nurse the divine Soma.

### Parpola’s Framework: The Dravidian Hypothesis



Asko Parpola in *The Roots of Hinduism: The Early Aryans and the Indus Civilization* (OUP, 2015) argued that Hinduism has two major roots: the religion brought by Indo-Iranian speakers in the second millennium BCE, and the Indus civilization, which used a pictographic script encoding a Proto-Dravidian language. This linguistic identification is supported, Parpola argues, by the many Dravidian loanwords in the Vedic language, including the oldest available source, the Ṛgveda, among which are religious terms important in both Vedic and Hindu religions, such as the sacred syllable *oṁ*. Furthermore, the present-day distribution of Dravidian languages in central and southern India favors Dravidian as the Harappan language, since the Chalcolithic cultures of Gujarat, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, and Karnataka are derived from the Early and Mature Harappan cultures of the Indus Valley.

Parpola postulated two waves of Aryan-speaking migrants entering South Asia through the BMAC (Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex), one being the *Atharvavedic Aryans* (Dāsa-Aryans) from c. 1900 BCE, whose principal gods were the Aśvins and Mitra-Varuṇa, and the second being the *Ṛgvedic Aryans* (Indra-worshipping) from c. 1400–1500 BCE. His most celebrated reading applied the rebus principle to the Indus*fish*pictogram, with Dravidian*mīn*(fish), which is homophonous with*mīn*(star in Old Tamil). The combination of six short strokes with the fish sign yields *āṟu-mīn* (literally six-star), the Dravidian name for the Pleiades (*Kṛttikā*) in relation to the six stars who nursed Skanda, giving him the epithet *Kārttikeya* and the six-faced form *Ṣaṇmukha*.

He identified the *pīpal*-tree deity seal’s anthropomorphic figure as a goddess of fertility and victory in war, the proto-form of Durgā, and linked the “dot + fish” sign to the star *Rohiṇī* (Aldebaran) via the carp (*rohita*, the red fish) through Dravidian *pottu mīn*. A Kalibangan cylindrical seal depicts what Parpola describes as a Durgā-like goddess of war associated with the tiger. He asserts that the Harappans, for instance, celebrated the beginning of the new year at the vernal equinox, associating it with the myth of *Murukaṉ*'s (*Skanda-Kārttikeya*) birth.

### The Goddess-Buffalo Complex



Parpola’s most consequential contribution to the Mātṛkā-Skanda debate lies in his reconstruction of what he calls as village Hinduism, a folk-religious substrate whose essential components appear to date back at least as early as the Early and Mature Harappan villages of the third millennium BCE. Its defining elements are:

> “A mother goddess as the guardian deity of the village, her husband or
> servant symbolized by the bull or buffalo, and their worship in the
> shape of clay or stone images, are basic elements of this age-old folk
> religion, likewise the cult of sacred trees and their divine
> inhabitants. Fig trees, especially the pipal and banyan, the bull, the
> water buffalo, the fish, and the peacock are some of the important
> motifs of Early and Mature Harappan painted pottery, which are also
> among the principal religious symbols of ‘village Hinduism.’ Its main
> concerns are the promotion of human and animal fertility and averting
> disease, drought, and other misfortunes.” (Parpola 2015: 306)

This description maps precisely onto the Seven Sisters complex documented across South Asia; seven mother-goddesses as village guardians, a male figure (husband/brother/servant) symbolized by the buffalo, aniconic clay or stone images, tree-centered worship, and the governance of fertility and disease. John Marshall (1931) had already suggested the existence of unbroken continuity in Indian folk religion from Harappan times to the present day.

The Daimabad hoard (in the Godavari valley, Maharashtra), with its monumental bronze statue of a water buffalo, proves that the buffalo was a sacred animal in central Maharashtra in Late Harappan times, around 1800 BCE. Parpola emphasizes that everywhere in South India the water buffalo was until recently the preferred victim for the goddess, although other male animals could be offered, nowadays they are chiefly goats or chickens.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!szWI!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8b9624bf-a29a-491b-8293-fcd61ccd6a95_800x819.png)





Parpola’s reconstruction of the Harappan sacred marriage (*hieros gamos*) provides the theological framework within which the buffalo-deity, the warrior-goddess, and the Seven Mothers all cohere. Drawing on parallels with the West Asian cult of *Inanna/Ištar*, he argues that in the principal yearly festival of Harappan villages, the marriage of the guardian goddess was celebrated, involving the sacrifice of the bull or buffalo who represented the goddess’s husband:

> “In the Harappan ‘sacred marriage’ the sacrificed bridegroom of the
> goddess was a water buffalo or bison bull. The sacrificial buffalo may
> be assumed to personify the important Harappan god called Proto-Śiva,
> who wears the horns of the water buffalo, and his human counterpart,
> the king.” (Parpola 2015: 308–309)

This ritual was absorbed by the first wave of Indo-Aryan speakers (Atharvavedic Aryans), who replaced the water buffalo with the horse as the sacrificed bridegroom, their own most prestigious sacrificial animal. But a telltale linguistic fossil survives: the title of the king’s chief queen who lies with the horse in the Vedic *aśvamedha* is *mahiṣī*, literally meaning “water buffalo cow.”

> “A reminiscence of the Harappan buffalo is the title of the king’s
> chief queen who lies with the horse, *mahiṣī*, ‘water buffalo cow.’
> The chief queen is said to personify the Earth and the most important
> goddess of the Vedic Brāhmaṇa texts, Vāc, ‘Sound.’” (Parpola 2015:
> 309)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Jnay!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4f21b1eb-86c5-4229-b81b-09671f660fe7_1024x559.png)





The goddess *Vāc*, as the virginal daughter of *Prajāpati*, the goddess of victory called *siṁhī* (lioness), worshipped at the *mahāvrata* festival with war-drums and sumptuous feasting, is thus identified by Parpola as the direct Vedic predecessor of Durgā. The *mahāvrata* was originally celebrated at the autumnal equinox, the traditional time of starting war expeditions after the rains, and survives to the present day in the navarātri/daśahrā festival of Goddess Durgā, who, on the tenth day of victory, vanquishes her ferocious opponent, the Buffalo Demon (Parpola 2015: 309).

The iconography of the lion-riding warrior goddess *Durgā’s* fight with the buffalo demon *Mahiṣāsura*, though it emerges in Indian literature very late, around 700 CE, can be traced back to the first centuries CE. Parpola argues it was introduced to India by the*Kuṣāṇas*, who, a little earlier, had in Afghanistan adopted the local worship of the lion-escorted goddess Nanaya, herself a variant of the Mesopotamian *Inanna/Ištar*. But a tiger-riding goddess of war is attested also in a Harappan cylinder seal from Kalibangan (K-65), and several Ṛgvedic hymns (RV 5.29.7–8) in which hundreds of water buffaloes are killed for Indra to consume before a battle prove the existence of such rituals in the Indus Valley at the end of the second millennium BCE, when the Ṛgvedic Aryans were reaching South Asia. The water buffalo sacrifice is mentioned in later Vedic literature in a single context: as a subsidiary victim in the horse sacrifice, sacred to *Varuṇa*, the divine king.

### The M-312 Seal: Proto-Mahiṣāsuramardinī in Combat with Mahiṣa



A second seal of critical importance is M-312 (in Parpola’s *Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions*), which depicts a scene long misidentified as *jallikkaṭṭu* (bull-baiting) but which Indus Valley archaeologists from Ernest Mackay (1935) onward have consistently interpreted as a mythological combat between a water buffalo and human figures. Mackay described it as another seal-amulet depicting a buffalo that has obviously attacked a group of people and stands triumphant amid its victims (*The Indus Civilization*, 1935: 32). The crucial detail is the figure caught in the buffalo’s left horn. While four male figures are shown vaulting away from the buffalo’s body, this figure, wearing a skirt and a single braid (*ēka-vēṇī*), is female. Alf Hiltebeitel (*Anthropos*73:5-6, 1978: 772) confirmed that the horns are those of a wild buffalo, not a zebu bull, and that the upward curve of the horns, which characterizes the buffalo on all these seals, portrays the animal at its most aggressive.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FAhE!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F072ed08d-1c14-467c-99b2-3bbe893897b4_698x616.png)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FAhE!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F072ed08d-1c14-467c-99b2-3bbe893897b4_698x616.png)





This scene has been interpreted as a *Proto-Durgā* mythological battle in which the goddess is in combat with the buffalo demon (*Mahiṣa*), a foundational narrative that would later crystallize into the *Mahiṣāsuramardinī* iconography. A parallel seal from Banawali (in Haryana) depicts all-female figures in combat with the buffalo, some wearing bangles, possibly an early iconographic form of the Sapta Mātṛkā collectively engaged in buffalo combat. If correct, this links the M-312 combat scene directly to the Seven Sisters complex and confirms that the goddess-buffalo confrontation was not an individual combat but a collective ritual involving the entire mother-goddess group.

### Proto-Śiva-Paśupati’s Crocodile Twin



The crocodile, specifically the long‑snouted gharial (*Gavialis gangeticus*), was among the most important divine animals of the Indus or Harappan Civilization (c. 2600–1900 BCE), appearing on seals, molded tablets, amulets, and painted pottery from Mohenjo‑daro, Harappa, Amri, and Binjor. In the reading now dominant among Indologists, the gharial functioned as a male sky‑ and fertility‑god, paired with a tiger‑riding earth goddess, and prefigured later Hindu conceptions of Varuṇa, Makara, and the tantric divine couple. Parpola’s 2011 monograph *Crocodile in the Indus Civilization and later South Asian traditions,*and his 2015 book*The Roots of Hinduism,*argue that the cult involved thesacrifice of the firstborn for fertility, a practice faintly echoed by the Vedic Śunaḥśepa legend. Seals and tablets from Mohenjo‑daro and Amri, dating to roughly 4,000 years ago, depict gharials with fish in their mouths, surrounded by fish, or flanking a horned deity.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5u3a!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb5927569-4f31-405b-b10c-7bf09081dba6_712x225.png)

The most famous Indus image, the so‑called Paśupati or Proto‑Śiva seal (M‑304) from Mohenjo‑daro, showing a buffalo‑horned yogic figure flanked by tiger, elephant, rhinoceros and buffalo has a direct structural counterpart in a set of mass‑produced moulds (M‑439, M‑440, M‑441, M‑1393, M‑1394, M‑1395) in which the human yogic deity is replaced by a horned gharial surrounded by the same four animals. Alf Hiltebeitel’s 1978 reanalysis argued that the gharial is the theriomorphic form of the horned god represented in M‑304.

The most daring reinterpretation concerns tablet H‑180 from Harappa, incorrectly interpreted by John Marshall (1931), and later identified as a female in intercourse with a phallic crocodile scene by Philip Rawson (1968, 1972), which Parpola formally endorsed the reading as a gharial depicted as fertilizing a human female (2015, Fig. 15.6). The crocodile is often identified as a male deity, similar to the male buffalo deity, with continuity into modern Hinduism.

Parpola identified an unbroken continuity in the practice of unique crocodile worship in 50 tribal villages of southern Gujarat, where Gujarati tribals erected horizontal wooden crocodile images on vertical poles. This crocodile-deity, often associated with Vedic *Varuṇa*, the god of waters and lord of aquatic animals, who in Hindu iconography rides a crocodile, is associated with the night sky and the heavenly crocodile, a constellation of stars with the pole star in its tail. Parpola proposed that the Harappans had two principal male gods connected with kingship, the old divine king comparable to Vedic *Varuṇa* and *Prajāpati* and to Hindu *Śiva* in his ascetic aspect, ruling in his central palace or capital, and his young alter ego, the crown prince comparable to Vedic *Rudra*, and also with Prince *Rohita* from the *Śunaḥśepa* legend, who was in charge of military expeditions, as in the Vedic royal consecration. The young god is identified as the red, newly born sun of the morning, and the new year, the sun (re)born at the asterism of the Pleiades, the war-god Skanda nursed by the Kṛttikās, also called Kumāra. Parpola connected two-god dualism with two halves of the year, which ended, respectively, in the *mahāvrata* and *viṣuvat* festivals at the autumnal and vernal equinoxes.

The IVC evidence for Skanda’s origins is circumstantial at best, but the structural elements that would later define the Skanda-Mātṛkā complex, groups of mother-goddesses, a young martial male deity born at the Pleiades asterism, the sacred marriage of goddess and buffalo, and pillar/post worship have deep roots in the northwestern subcontinent. Whether these roots extend into the IVC remains unproven but is crucial to any conclusion. Based on *Maitrāyaṇī Saṃhitā* 2.9.1 mantras in relation to what he terms pre-Vedic warrior-gods, arguing for a substrate of martial deity worship in the northwestern regions of the subcontinent that predates the fully formed Vedic tradition. While Parpola’s arguments for IVC continuity have been influential, Mann (2012: 11) maintained a more conservative position, postulating that Kārttikeya, as a full-fledged character, does not appear until the Mahābhārata and very late Vedic ancillary texts. He cannot be traced to the Vedas themselves as a well-developed figure, and cannot be traced beyond the approximate date of the fourth century BCE from the evidence supplied by religious texts.
