# Kuru–Pāṇḍava Descent Claim of the Pāṇḍyas

### A Textual, Epigraphic, and Genealogical Perspective

The Pāṇḍyas of Madurai occupy a peculiar position in early Indian history. They are among the earliest South Indian dynasties attested in independent epigraphic and foreign-textual evidence, including the 3rd-century BCE Aśokan edicts, the Mauryan*Arthaśāstra*, and the late-fourth-century BCE Megasthenes, yet their formal self-presentation as a *Candravaṃśa*-Pāṇḍava-descended *kṣatriya* lineage surfaces only with the Velvikkuṭi plates of *c.* 769–770 CE, roughly a millennium later, though earlier Caṅkam poems refer to them as one. The interpretive question is therefore not whether the early Pāṇḍyas who ruled from Korkai were historically Pāṇḍava descendants, no source any historian can credit makes that case, but rather how, when, and why the Kuru–Pāṇḍava genealogical idiom became operative in their self-presentation, and what its operation reveals about the formation of early Tamil polity and the larger pan-subcontinental work performed by the *Mahābhārata*.

This study is organized into five sections, followed by a section for further discussion.

## Pāṇḍyas as Kavuriyar in Tamil Sources



The earliest Tamil attestation of the Pāṇḍyas as *Kavuriyar* (கவுரியர்) appears in the *Akanāṉūṟu* anthology, where the king *Teṉṉaṉ*, the Southerner, a standing Pāṇḍya epithet, is repeatedly placed in this register:

> *தெனாஅது வெல் போர்க் கவுரியர் நல் நாட்டு…நல் இசைத் தென்னன் - அகநானூறு 342  
> teṉāatu vel pōrk kavuriyar nal nāṭṭu…nal icait teṉṉaṉ - Akanāṉūṟu 342*

In a second instance from the poet Maturaik Kūttaṉār Kaṭuvaṉ Maḷḷaṉār, in the context of an allusion to Rāma’s presence at Dhanuṣkoṭi, the poet identifies *Kōṭi* as part of the Pāṇḍya kingdom and styles the dynasty *Kavuriyar*:

> *வென் வேற் கவுரியர் தொல் முது கோடி… - அகநானூறு 70  
> veṉ vēṟ kavuriyar tol mutu kōṭi… - Akanāṉūṟu 70*

A third instance occurs in *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 3, in which the Pāṇḍyan king Karuṅkai Oḷvāḷ Perumpeyarvaḻuti is praised as a great benefactor and addressed in the vocative as a member of the *Kavuriyar* lineage:

> *தவிரா ஈகை, கவுரியர் மருக! - புறநானூறு 03  
> tavirā īkai, kavuriyar maruka! - Puṟanāṉūṟu 3*

The Tamil form *Kavuriyar* transparently derives from Sanskrit *Kauravya* (कौरव्य), the *vṛddhi* derivative of *Kuru*, meaning “descendant of Kuru” (cf. *kauravyavaṃśadāve ‘smin ka eṣa śalabhāyate*, Bhaṭṭanārāyaṇa’s *Veṇīsaṃhāra* 1.19). Although the *formal* self-proclamation of Kuru descent in copper-plate *praśasti* appears only with the eighth-century Velvikkuṭi grant, the Caṅkam attestations show that the Tamil literary identification of the Pāṇḍyas as *Kuru* descendants was already in circulation centuries earlier.

The most often cited Tamil source for the Kuru claim is the*Cilappatikāram'*s designation of the Pāṇḍya dynasty as *Kavuriyar* (Subrahmanian 1966, p. 314; Zvelebil 1973). In *Mahābhārata* usage, *Kaurava* frequently denotes the broader Bharata–Kuru lineage of which the five Pāṇḍavas are themselves members; the *Cilappatikāram*’s label is, accordingly, genealogically generous rather than committal:

> நிலம் தரு திருவின் நிழல் வாய் நேமி கடம் பூண்டு உருட்டும் கௌரியர் பெரும் சீர்க்
> கோலின் செம்மையும்…   
> *nilam taru tiruviṉ niḻal vāy nēmi kaṭam pūṇṭu uruṭṭum* *kauriyar
> perum cīrk kōliṉ cemmaiyum…* - *Cilappatikāram* 15

By the time the medieval Pāṇḍyas revived their rule under Kaṭuṅkōṉ after the Kalabhra interregnum, the Velvikkuṭi *praśasti* of 769–770 CE was prepared to formalize and elaborate what had already been in literary circulation for several centuries.

## Velvikkuṭi Praśasti: Descent from Purūravas Aila



The Pāṇḍya dynasty’s later Sanskrit *praśasti*, formalized most fully in the Velvikkuṭi copper-plate grant of Jaṭila Parāntaka Neṭuñcaṭaiyaṉ Varaguṇavarman I (*c.* 769–770 CE; edited by H. Krishna Sastri in *Epigraphia Indica* 17 \[1923–24\], no. 16, pp. 291–309, with the Sanskrit *praśasti* discussed at pp. 293 and 305), traces the dynasty’s origin in a strikingly oblique way. The Sanskrit portion was composed by *Varodayabhaṭṭa Sarvakratuyājin* and inscribed in Grantha script in lines 1–30. It does *not* graft the line onto the Pūru–Bharata–Kuru–Pāṇḍu branch via Yayāti’s youngest son, the genealogical track that would have made the Pāṇḍyas junior coevals of the *Mahābhārata* Pāṇḍavas. Instead, it grafts the line onto Purūravas Aila, the apical Lunar king and grandfather of Yayāti, *before* the fivefold branching among Yayāti’s sons (Yadu, Turvasu, Druhyu, Anu, Pūru), as narrated in *Mahābhārata* Ādi Parva 79–84 (Ganguli vulgate; BORI 1.70–95).

The relevant passage of the *praśasti*, in Sastri’s summary translation, reads:

> *“At the end of the previous kalpa (age), a king named Pāṇḍya ruled
> the coastal region. At the beginning of the present kalpa, this king
> was reborn as Budha, the son of the Moon. Budha’s son Purūravas
> introduced the dynasty’s emblem, a pair of fish, and shared his throne
> and taxes with Indra. Purūravas’ descendant Māravarman was a patron of
> the learned, who conquered several enemies and gave away heaps of
> gold.”*

Three strategic moves are visible in this short passage. First, the *praśasti* posits an *eponymous primordial king named Pāṇḍya* who ruled the coastal region at the end of the previous *kalpa* and was reborn at the beginning of the present *kalpa* as *Budha*, son of the Moon. This makes the Pāṇḍya dynastic identity coterminous with the generative origin of the Lunar dynasty itself. Second, Budha’s son *Purūravas* is credited with introducing the dynasty’s twin-fish emblem (*matsya-yugma*; Tamil *iraṭṭai mīṉ*) and with sharing throne and taxes with Indra, a Sanskritising retrojection of the dynasty’s heraldic identity into the apical Lunar king. Third, the *praśasti* then skips directly to the historical Pāṇḍya line beginning with Māravarman, omitting the entire *Āyu–Nahuṣa–Yayāti–Pūru–Bharata–Kuru sequence*.

The Purāṇic Lunar-dynastic genealogy preserved in the *Vāyu*, *Matsya*, *Brahmāṇḍa*, *Viṣṇu*, *Bhāgavata*, and *Harivaṃśa* recensions, and systematised by Pargiter (*Ancient Indian Historical Tradition*, 1922, esp. ch. IX “The Aila or Lunar race,” pp. 99–118, and ch. XII “Table of Royal Genealogies,” pp. 138–149), supplied four possible grafting-points for a southern dynasty seeking *Soma-vaṃśa* affiliation: (a) descent from a specific *Mahābhārata* Pāṇḍava; (b) descent from the Pūru–Bharata–Kuru–Pāṇḍu trunk; (c) descent from one of Yayāti’s other four sons (Yadu, Turvasu, Druhyu, Anu); or (d) descent from Purūravas himself, *before* the Yayāti branching. The Pāṇḍya *praśasti* takes option (d). This choice avoids the claim to a specific epic ancestor and the implicit juniority that any branch descent would have entailed relative to the *Mahābhārata* Pāṇḍavas. It also avoids the curses pronounced by Yayāti on his four elder sons (Yadu, Turvasu, Druhyu, Anu) for refusing to accept his decrepitude — curses that would otherwise compromise any line claiming descent through them. The Pāṇḍyas thereby position themselves *senior* to the *Mahābhārata* Pāṇḍavas in the Lunar genealogy, occupying the senior trunk before any of its branchings.

The strategy is striking in its economy. By identifying the primordial Pāṇḍya with Budha, the son of the Moon and father of Purūravas, the *praśasti* makes the dynasty coterminous with the very origin of the Lunar race. The Mahābhārata Pāṇḍavas, descending from Pūru through ten further generations to Pāṇḍu, become *junior* offshoots of a line whose senior trunk is held by the Pāṇḍyas of Madurai.

## Cinnamaṉūr Plates: Adjacency to the Pāṇḍavas, Not Descent from Them



The Larger Cinnamaṉūr Plates (*South Indian Inscriptions* Vol. III, Part IV, no. 206, ed. H. Krishna Sastri, Archaeological Survey of India / Government Press, Madras 1929, pp. 441–460), issued by *Maravarman Rājasiṃha II* (r. *c.* 900–915 CE, son of Parāntaka Vīranārāyaṇa and contemporary of the Cōḻa Parāntaka I), significantly escalate the rhetorical claims of the Velvikkuṭi *praśasti*. The Sanskrit *praśasti* portion, in Grantha script, ascribes to unnamed Pāṇḍya ancestors:

- occupying or sharing the throne of Indra (v. 4), echoing the
  Velvikkuṭi claim that Purūravas shared the throne and taxes with
  Indra;

- causing the Ten-Headed (Rāvaṇa of Laṅkā) to sue for peace (v. 5);

- conquering Arjuna (v. 7), the central adversarial *Mahābhārata* claim,
  positioning the Pāṇḍya as Arjuna’s vanquisher rather than his
  descendant;

- a reference to a king who cut off his own head to protect his master,
  and to a *Sundara-Pāṇḍya* who had mastered all the śāstras (v. 8); and

- performance of the *Rājasūya* and *Aśvamedha* by many kings of the
  family (v. 9), claiming *cakravartin* status parallel to Yudhiṣṭhira.

Across all these claims, the dynasty’s strategy is consistently adjacent to the Mahābhārata Pāṇḍavas, ally on the battlefield, assistant in curse-removal, opponent in single combat, *patron* of the epic’s vernacular translation, but never *descendant*. The four positions exhaust the possible non-descent relations to the Pāṇḍavas and are taken simultaneously, which is structurally incompatible with claiming descent from any specific Pāṇḍava. The Pāṇḍya genealogical strategy is consistently *parallel* to, rather than derivative from, the Mahābhārata Pāṇḍavas. The *Cilappatikāram*’s earlier Tamil designation of the Pāṇḍyas as *Kavuriyar* (Kauravya) derives its semantic warrant from the broad *Mahābhārata* sense of *Kaurava* as a descendant of Kuru itself, ultimately traceable to Purūravas via Pūru, while leaving the precise line of descent strategically undefined.

## Historical Attestations of the Early Pāṇḍyas



The historical record of the early Pāṇḍyas, spanning roughly 350 years from the mid-third century BCE through the mid-second century CE, consistently fails*to*identify them as Pāṇḍava or Kuru descendants. This consistency across multiple independent witnesses — Mauryan, Kaliṅgan, Greek, Roman, Sanskrit, and Tamil is itself significant.

### The Aśokan Edicts (c. 257–256 BCE)



Aśoka’s Major Rock Edict II names the Coḷa, Pāṇḍya (*Pāṃḍiya*), Satiyaputa, Keralaputa, and Tāmraparṇī as polities outside but neighboring his dominions, recipients of his medical and arboricultural patronage; Major Rock Edict XIII reasserts the Coḷa and Pāṇḍya at the southern frontier of his *Dhamma*-victory (Hultzsch, *Inscriptions of Asoka, CII* I, 1925, pp. 27–30, 47–49):

> *Sarvata Devānāṃpriyasa Priyadasino rāño vijite . . . evaṃ pi
> prachantesu yathā Coḍā Pāṃḍiyā Satiyaputo Kelalaputo Tāmbapaṃṇi . . .*
>
> *“Everywhere in the dominion of King Devānāṃpriya Priyadarśin, and
> likewise among his neighbours such as the Cōḷas, Pāṇḍyas,
> Satiyaputras, Keralaputras, and as far as Tāmbapaṃṇi (Sri Lanka) . .
> ., two kinds of medical treatment have been established, for humans
> and for animals.”*

The textual placement confirms that the Pāṇḍyas were independent of Mauryan suzerainty, sufficiently organized to be known to Aśokan diplomacy, and culturally distinct from the Mauryan north by the mid-third century BCE. The Jambai Tamil-Brāhmī inscription, later identified with the Atiyamāṉ chieftain Neṭumāṉ Añci, has further confirmed *Satiyaputra* as the designation of the Atiyamāṉ chiefdom.

### The Hāthīgumphā Inscription of Khāravela



In line 11 of the seventeen-line Prakrit inscription on the Udayagiri hills, the Kaliṅga ruler Khāravela claims in his eleventh regnal year to have broken up the *Tramira-deśa-saṅghāta*, the confederacy of the *Tamil/Dramiḷa* countries, with the qualifying phrase *terasa-vaṣa-śatika*, a hundred-and-thirteen-year-old confederacy. If a *c.* 165–155 BCE date for Khāravela is accepted, the formation of the Tamil confederacy is pushed back to *c.* 268 BCE, contemporaneous with Aśoka. Khāravela also claims to have received tribute, including pearls, from the Pāṇḍya king.

### Tamil-Brāhmī Inscriptions



Iravatham Mahadevan’s *Early Tamil Epigraphy: From the Earliest Times to the Sixth Century A.D.* (Cre-A and Harvard Oriental Series 62, 2003) remains the standard corpus. Six Tamil-Brāhmī inscriptions in five caves from Māṅkulam are palaeographically dated by Mahadevan to the late third century BCE (Mahadevan 2003, nos. 1–5). Inscription 1 records that *Kaṭalaṉ Vaḻuti* (கடலன் வழுதி), a servant of *Neṭuñceḻiyaṉ* (நெடுஞ்செழியன்), caused a stone bed to be cut for the Jain monk *Nanta-siri Kuvaṉ* (நந்தஶ்ரீ குவன்). Inscription 5 mentions *Antai Acutaṉ* (அந்தை அசுதன்) *kāviti*, a financial/administrative officer and superintendent of pearls of the *veḷarai* merchant guild. The Maṅkulam Neṭuñceḻiyaṉ is generally taken to predate both the *Maturaikkāñci*’s Talaiyālaṅkāṉam victor and Palyākacālai Mutukuṭumi Peruvaḻuti.

### Greek and Roman Sources



Megasthenes, the Seleucid ambassador to Candragupta Maurya (*c.* 304–290 BCE), describes the Pāṇḍya/*Pandaia*/*Pandaiē* (Πανδαίη) country as “the only nation throughout India ruled by women,” with 365 villages and a queen Pandaia who was the daughter of Heracles, born in India. Pliny (*Naturalis Historia* VI.23) reports that Heracles preferred this daughter and bestowed on her sole female sovereignty over the *Pandae*, a memory that may reflect matrilineal succession traditions.

The *Periplus Maris Erythraei* §§ 54–58 (*c.* 60 CE; Casson 1989, pp. 84–87, 215–28) names the Pāṇḍya king (*Pandion*), his pearl-port *Kolchoi* (Korkai), and the inland capital with its emporium at *Argaru*. Patañjali’s *Mahābhāṣya*, commenting around Pāṇini 4.1.168, lists *Coḍa, Pāṇḍya,* and *Kerala* among southern *janapadas* (Kielhorn-Abhyankar ed., vol. II; Nilakanta Sastri 1976, pp. 86–87). Ptolemy’s *Geographia* VII.1 confirms and extends the picture, locating *Modoura basileion Pandionos* — Madurai, the royal seat of the Pandion — in the southern interior.

At no point in this 350-year span do any of these sources identify the Pāṇḍyas as descendants of the Pāṇḍavas or Kurus. The Kuru-descent claim is, on the available evidence, a post-Kalabhra construction first formalized in Sanskrit *praśasti* in the late eighth century, though the Tamil *Kavuriyar* identification is, as Section I showed, attested several centuries earlier in Caṅkam poetry.

## Mahābhārata Textual Hooks for the Pāṇḍya Claim



The *Mahābhārata* itself supplies four interrelated textual hooks on which later Pāṇḍya genealogists could and did construct their charter. These appear in the *Sabhā Parva*, the *Karṇa Parva*, the *Ādi Parva*, and the *Aśvamedhika Parva*. Each merits detailed analysis.

### Sabhā Parva: Sahadeva’s Southern Digvijaya



In the *Sabhā Parva* (BORI Critical Edition 2.28), Sahadeva’s southern *digvijaya* — undertaken in preparation for Yudhiṣṭhira’s *Rājasūya* incorporates the Pāṇḍya king as a tributary (van Buitenen 1975, II:80–82):

> पाण्ड्यांश्च द्रविडांश्चैव सहितांश्चोड्रकेरलैः । अन्ध्रांस्तलवनांश्चैव कलिङ्गानोष्ट्रकर्णिकान्
> ॥ 2.28.48 ॥ *pāṇḍyāṃś ca draviḍāṃś caiva sahitāṃś coḍrakeralaiḥ*
> *andhrāṃs talavanāṃś caiva kaliṅgān oṣṭrakarṇikān*

This is followed by 2.28.49, which describes the farthest extent of the known regions and kingdoms in the *Mahābhārata*‘s geographical imagination:

> अन्ताखीं चैव रोमां च यवनानां पुरं तथा । दूतैरेव वशे चक्रे करं चैनानदापयत् ॥ 2.28.49 ॥
>   
> *antākhīṃ caiva romāṃ ca yavanānāṃ puraṃ tathā* *dūtair eva vaśe cakre
> karaṃ caināna dāpayat*

These two verses state that the Pāṇḍyas and the Draviḍas, together with the Uḍra-Keralas, the Andhras and Talavanas, the Kaliṅgas and Oṣṭrakarṇikas, and also *Antākhī* (Antioch), *Romā* (Rome), and *the city of the Yavanas/Greeks*, were brought under his sway *by envoys alone* (*dūtair eva*) and made to pay tribute. The catalogue *pāṇḍya–draviḍa–uḍra-kerala–andhra–talavana–kaliṅga–oṣṭrakarṇika* culminates in *antākhī, romā,* and *yavanānāṃ puram*. This places the Pāṇḍyas at the very edge of the *Mahābhārata*’s geographical imagination, on a continuum with the Hellenistic Mediterranean — a remarkable detail that suggests this passage post-dates Indo-Roman commercial awareness in the closing centuries BCE. A further point worth noting: *Draviḍas* are mentioned as a *separate* entity adjacent to the Pāṇḍyas and Keralas, not as a collective term subsuming them.

Sahadeva himself is repeatedly identified by epithets that reinforce his Kuru identity, *kauravya* (2.28.2c, 2.28.33a, “of the Kuru lineage”) and *kurunandana* (2.28.15a, 2.28.32a, “delight of the Kurus”). Immediately after 2.28.49, Sahadeva proceeds to *Bharukaccha* (Bhṛgukaccha/Broach), from where he sends envoys to *Vibhīṣaṇa,* the *Paulastya*, the grandson of Pulastya and king of Laṅkā (2.28.50–53):

> भरुकच्छं गतो धीमान्दूतान्माद्रवतीसुतः । प्रेषयामास राजेन्द्र पौलस्त्याय महात्मने ॥
> 2.28.50 ॥ विभीषणाय धर्मात्मा प्रीतिपूर्वमरिंदमः ॥ 2.28.50e ॥

This places the Pāṇḍya country geographically between the envoy route to the south and the final transmarine extension to Laṅkā, consistent with its later historical location in the deep south.

### Karṇa Parva: Malayadhvaja, the Pāṇḍya of the Sandalwood Standard



In the *Karṇa Parva* (BORI Critical Edition 8.15; vulgate / Ganguli Section 20), the Pāṇḍya king *Malayadhvaja*, “he whose standard bears the device of the Malaya mountain” (*malaya-pratima-ketu*; 8.15.32), fights for the Pāṇḍavas at Kurukṣetra on the sixteenth day of the war and is killed by Aśvatthāman after destroying Karṇa’s army. The text titles him *Malayeśvara* (8.15.35) and *adri-pati-īśvara* (8.15.36), anchoring the epic Pāṇḍya unambiguously in the southern Malaya range. His standard is*candanāguru-bhūṣitam*(8.15.32), adorned with sandalwood and*aguru,*the signature products of the Malaya region, as named in the*Arthaśāstra*and the*Periplus*.

Sañjaya’s narrative assessment that the Pāṇḍya regarded himself as equal to Karṇa, Bhīṣma, Vāsudeva, and Arjuna (8.15.4) is among the most exalted epic appraisals of any non-central warrior and very plausibly reflects an interpolated southern-redactorial elevation of the dynastic ancestor. The death-scene’s framing, in which the king’s body and elephant are divided into ten parts, likened to sacrificial *havis* for the ten deities (8.15.40–42), and the closing simile of the Kauravas worshipping Aśvatthāman as Indra worshipped Viṣṇu after the defeat of Bali (8.15.43), casts the Pāṇḍya in the *Soma-vaṃśa*, asura-king-of-high-ritual-stature register that the Velvikkuṭi *praśasti* of 769–770 CE would later inherit and develop.

### Ādi Parva and Aśvamedhika Parva: The Citrāṅgadā–Babhruvāhana Episode



The *Ādi Parva* (BORI 1.207; Ganguli vulgate, *Arjuna-vanavāsa-parva* Section 217) and the *Aśvamedhika Parva* (BORI 14.78–82; Ganguli vulgate Section 79 ff.) together recount the Citrāṅgadā–Babhruvāhana episode, which provides the *Mahābhārata*’s most institutionally specific textual hook for southern matrilineal kingship.

During Arjuna’s southern wanderings, after visiting the Mahendra mountain in Kaliṅga and proceeding southward *along the sea-shore* (*samudratīreṇa*, 1.207.13c) — an itinerary that decisively rules out the conventional north-Indian identification of the destination with present-day Manipur in the northeast — he reaches *Maṇalūra* (variant *Maṇipūra* in the *Aśvamedhika Parva*):

> महेन्द्रपर्वतं दृष्ट्वा तापसैरुपशोभितम् । समुद्रतीरेण शनैर्मणलूरं जगाम ह ॥ 1.207.13 ॥
> *mahendraparvataṃ dṛṣṭvā tāpasair upaśobhitam* *samudratīreṇa śanair
> maṇalūraṃ jagāma ha*

“Having seen the Mahendra mountain adorned by ascetics, slowly along the sea-shore (*samudra-tīreṇa*) Arjuna proceeded to Maṇalūra.”

He is then welcomed by the king *Maṇalūreśvara* / *Maṇipūreśvara* Citravāhana:

> अभ्यगच्छन्महीपतिम् । मणलूरेश्वरं राजन्धर्मज्ञं चित्रवाहनम् ॥ 1.207.14de ॥
> *abhyagacchan mahīpatim* *maṇalūreśvaraṃ rājan dharmajñaṃ
> citravāhanam*

“He approached the lord of the earth, the lord of Maṇalūra (*maṇalūreśvaram*), the righteous Citravāhana.”

The *Mahābhārata* leaves the southern location of Maṇalūra/Maṇipura ambiguous in detail; the standard north-Indian commentarial identification with present-day Manipur is geographically forced, as Manipur is not reached on Arjuna’s stipulated southern itinerary along the *samudra-tīra*. The king’s lineage is restricted by a Śiva-boon to one child per generation; having only a daughter, Citrāṅgadā, he has formally designated her *putrikā* (1.207.21) under the Dharmaśāstric institution of *putrikā-putra* (cf. *Manusmṛti* 9.127–141; *Yājñavalkyasmṛti* 2.128–132), and stipulates that the son born of her shall remain at Maṇalūra as *kula-kṛt* (1.207.22). Arjuna accepts, resides there for three years (*trihimāḥ samāḥ*, 1.207.23), and fathers Babhruvāhana before departing.

In the *Aśvamedhika Parva*, Babhruvāhana, urged by his stepmother Ulūpī, Arjuna’s Nāga wife and mother of Iravān / Aravāṉ, who breaks through the earth from her Nāga realm to enforce *kṣatra-dharma* (14.78.8–12), engages his unrecognized father in single combat to defend his *putrikā*-inherited kingdom against the sacrificial-horse expedition. He pierces Arjuna in the heart with a sharp arrow (14.78.33–35); Arjuna falls. Citrāṅgadā arrives at the battlefield grieving (14.78.38–39); Ulūpī then revives Arjuna with the *mṛta-sañjīvana-maṇi* (14.80). Arjuna acknowledges his son’s prowess and identifies him as *citrāṅgadātmaja*, “son of Citrāṅgadā” (14.78.25), a matrilineal vocative consistent with the *putrikā* arrangement.

The narrative’s central inversion, that the matrilineal-southern son of Arjuna *defeats and kills* the Pāṇḍava patriarch in single combat, with only Ulūpī’s *maṇi* restoring him, provides the precise mythic structure that a later peripheral matrilineal-southern dynasty seeking *Mahābhārata*-charter legitimacy would find most useful: a Sanskrit-textual endorsement of southern matrilineal succession overcoming northern patriliny in single combat. The geographical anchoring (*samudra-tīreṇa*, the Mahendra route, the deep southeastern peninsula), the institutional anchoring (*putrikā-putra* under classical Dharmaśāstra), and the narrative anchoring (matrilineal defeat of patrilineal Pāṇḍava arms) together constitute exactly the textual scaffolding a later Madurai dynasty would need.

## Discussion: The Genealogical Strategy and Its Significance



The evidence assembled in the foregoing sections permits several interlocking conclusions about the Kuru–Pāṇḍava descent claim of the Pāṇḍyas of Madurai and what it reveals about the formation of early Tamil polity and the political work of the *Mahābhārata*.

First, the historical Pāṇḍyas from the third century BCE through the second century CE were not, on any independent epigraphic or foreign textual evidence, identified as Pāṇḍava or Kuru descendants. The Aśokan edicts, the Hāthīgumphā inscription of Khāravela, the Tamil-Brāhmī cave inscriptions of Māṅkulam and elsewhere, Megasthenes (and the Pliny–Strabo–Polyaenus tradition deriving from him), the *Periplus Maris Erythraei*, Patañjali’s *Mahābhāṣya*, and Ptolemy’s *Geographia*, together spanning roughly 350 years, all describe the Pāṇḍyas as an established southern polity, but none gives them a *Mahābhārata* genealogy. The Kuru-descent claim is, on the evidence, a *constructed* charter rather than an inherited one.

Second, the construction operated on two different temporal levels. In the Tamil literary register, the identification of the Pāṇḍyas as *Kavuriyar*, descendants of Kuru in the broad *Mahābhārata* sense, is already attested in Caṅkam poetry (*Akanāṉūṟu* 70, 342; *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 3) and is fully developed in the *Cilappatikāram*. This Tamil literary genealogy is genealogically generous rather than committal; it identifies the dynasty with the broader Bharata–Kuru lineage but does not pin it to any specific Pāṇḍava brother. In the Sanskrit epigraphic register, the formal *praśasti* claim surfaces only with the Velvikkuṭi grant of *c.* 769–770 CE, in the immediate post-Kalabhra dynastic restoration under Kaṭuṅkōṉ’s descendants. The Sanskrit register formalizes *and* elaborates what was already in Tamil literary circulation.

Third, the strategy of formalization is itself analytically revealing. Confronted with four possible grafting-points in the Purāṇic Lunar-dynastic genealogy, a specific Pāṇḍava, the Pūru–Bharata–Kuru–Pāṇḍu trunk, one of Yayāti’s other four sons, or Purūravas Aila, before the Yayāti branching, but the Velvikkuṭi praśasti takes the most senior grafting-point. It identifies the primordial Pāṇḍya as the kalpa-rebirth of Budha himself, making the dynastic identity coterminous with the generative origin of the Lunar race. This positions the Pāṇḍyas as ontologically senior to the Mahābhārata Pāṇḍavas in the Lunar genealogy. The implicit hierarchy is the inverse of what conventional Sanskritising aspirations would generate: rather than claiming junior affiliation with a prestigious northern model, the dynasty claims senior parallel status with that model.

Fourth, the Larger Cinnamaṉūr Plates of the early tenth century reinforce this parallel-prestige strategy with a *four-position* construction. The Pāṇḍya ancestors are simultaneously allies of the Pāṇḍavas, *assistants* to them, *opponents* of them, conquerors of Arjuna in single combat, and*patrons*of the epic itself. These four positions exhaust the structural possibilities for a non-descent relation to the *Mahābhārata* Pāṇḍavas. Taken together, they are structurally incompatible with any claim of lineal descent, yet they capture all the cultural-political prestige of *Mahābhārata*-adjacency.

Fifth, the *Mahābhārata* itself provides remarkably rich textual hooks for this strategy. The *Sabhā Parva*’s placement of the Pāṇḍyas on the southern envoy-route to Laṅkā establishes their epic-geographical position; the *Karṇa Parva*’s Malayadhvaja fighting on the Pāṇḍava side, his sandalwood-and-*aguru* standard explicitly anchored in the Malaya range, his death framed in the *Soma-vaṃśa*/Bali-defeated-by-Viṣṇu register anticipates the *Soma-vaṃśa* affiliation that the Velvikkuṭi *praśasti* would later make explicit. The *Ādi Parva*’s Citrāṅgadā–Babhruvāhana episode, with its geographically southern coastal itinerary, its technically precise *putrikā* institution, and its narrative endorsement of matrilineal-southern succession defeating northern patriliny in single combat, provides a Sanskritic-textual scaffolding for the very kind of southern peripheral matrilineal kingship that historically existed along the southwestern and southeastern Indian seaboards. The dynasty’s genealogists did not invent their charter; they recognized, with considerable analytical acuity, that the *Mahābhārata* had already produced precisely the narrative their dynasty required.

Sixth, the larger significance of the Pāṇḍya case for the historiography of South Indian dynastic ideology is that it complicates the conventional Sanskritisation model. In the framework first articulated by M. N. Srinivas and refined by Burton Stein and others, Sanskritisation typically operates as a downward-and-inward borrowing, in which lower or peripheral groups adopt prestigious Brahmanical practices to claim higher status. The Pāṇḍya strategy does not fit this template. The dynasty does not claim junior affiliation with a northern Sanskritic model; it claims senior parallel status with that model, retrojecting its identity into the *kalpa* preceding the present one and asserting *Mahābhārata*-adjacency rather than *Mahābhārata*-descent. This is *Sanskritisation as creative,* competitive parallel-construction, not as derivative borrowing.

The *how* and *when* of the Kuru–Pāṇḍava genealogical idiom in Pāṇḍya self-presentation can therefore be summarised in a single trajectory: *Tamil literary identification as Kavuriyar, from the Caṅkam period and the Cilappatikāram, through the early centuries CE* → *Sanskritic formal praśasti claim of descent from Purūravas Aila (Velvikkuṭi grant, c. 769–770 CE)* → *elaborated four-position parallel-prestige Mahābhārata-adjacency (Larger Cinnamaṉūr Plates, c. 900–915 CE)*. The *why* is at once political, providing a Brahmanical *kṣatriya* charter for post-Kalabhra dynastic restoration, and cultural, articulating a Tamil dynastic identity that could claim parity with the prestige structures of the pan-subcontinental Sanskrit world without subordinating itself to them. The Pāṇḍya case shows the *Mahābhārata* operating, in early-medieval South India, not as the genealogical anchor of a single subordinate dynasty but as a flexible textual resource that a senior southern parallel could mobilize for its own purposes.
