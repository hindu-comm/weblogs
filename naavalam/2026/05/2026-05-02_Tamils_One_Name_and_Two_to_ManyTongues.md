*Tamiḻ* (தமிழ்), the name by which the language has called itself for at least two and a half millennia, and the phenomenon of the structural diglossia by which that language has historically been divided into a *centamiḻ,* high (செந்தமிழ்) and a *koṭuntamiḻ,* low (கொடுந்தமிழ்) register is something to be understood. The two topics share more than juxtaposition; the very theorization of high and low Tamil is older than the term *diglossia*, embedded in the same *Tolkāppiyam* (தொல்காப்பியம்) that contains the earliest surviving instance of the word *tamiḻ* itself.

Few language-names are so densely freighted with semantic and political meaning as *Tamiḻ*. The word denotes the language, the people (*tamiḻar*, தமிழர்), the region (*tamiḻakam*, தமிழகம்; *tamiḻ-nāṭu*, தமிழ்நாடு), and the complex of aesthetic and moral values. Its phonological shape is itself rare; the final consonant *-ḻ* is a retroflex approximant peculiar to Tamil and Malayalam, indeed treated by indigenous grammarians as one of the *tamiḻ-eḻuttu* (the Tamil letters) that mark the language as such. The word names what names it.

The earliest secure attestations cluster between the third century BCE and the second century CE, distributed across at least four scriptural and epigraphic ecologies as follows: the indigenous Tamil-Brāhmī inscriptions of the cave-shelters of southern Tamiḻakam, Pali and Prakrit references in the Sri Lankan, Mauryan and Kaliṅgan epigraphic record, the bardic *Caṅkam* (சங்கம்) anthologies and the *Tolkāppiyam*, and the Greek and Latin geographers of the early Mediterranean trade. From an etymological perspective, modern philology has dissected the name from at least three angles: Caldwell’s nineteenth-century derivation from Sanskrit *Drāviḍa*; Zvelebil’s mid-twentieth-century reconstruction from a native verbal stem; and Krishnamurti’s and Southworth’s late-twentieth-century reformulations. None of these has produced a fully satisfactory account that obeys the regular sound-laws of Proto-Dravidian (PDr) reconstruction.

## Modern Etymological Proposals for Tamiḻ



Robert Caldwell’s *A Comparative Grammar of the Dravidian Languages* (1856) inaugurated comparative Dravidian linguistics and coined the family-name *Dravidian*, derived from Sanskrit *drāviḍa*. Caldwell was the first Western philologist to address the etymology of *tamiḻ* itself; he proposed, tentatively, the following derivational chain:

> Sanskrit *Drāviḍa* \> Prakrit *Damiḷa* \> Tamil *Tamiḻ*

Caldwell drew the Sanskrit *drāviḍa* from the *Tantravārttika* of Kumārila Bhaṭṭa (7th c. CE), the *Manusmṛti* (X.43–44), and the *Mahābhārata*, where *drāviḍa* appears as a southern people-name. He recognized *damiḷa* in Pali sources and posited *tamiḻ* as a back-formation. The proposal was provisional even in Caldwell’s own treatment; he acknowledged the difficulty of reconciling the *-ṛ-/-r-* in Sanskrit *drāviḍa* with the Tamil *-ḻ-*, and the awkwardness of deriving the native form from the foreign one.

The proposal has been definitively rejected by twentieth-century scholarship for two reasons. First, the chronology is incorrect to derive his proposal; the Pali and Prakrit forms *damiḷa-, dameḍa-, damela-* are securely attested from the third century BCE, while Sanskrit *drāviḍa* in this sense first appears in literary Sanskrit only in the Gupta period (4th c. CE) or possibly in the *Mahābhārata*’s late layers, that is, several centuries later. Second, the phonological direction is wrong where Indo-Aryan phonotactics tolerate *-r-* but not *-ḻ-*; the natural direction of borrowing is from a language with *-ḻ-* into a language without it, yielding *-l-/-ḷ-/-r-/-ḍ-* substitutes, not the reverse. The retroflex approximant *-ḻ-* is a marked, language-specific phoneme of the southern Dravidian languages; its presence in *tamiḻ* is decisive for native priority.

Kamil V. Zvelebil, the foremost Western Tamilist of the twentieth century, addressed the etymology in *The Smile of Murugan* (1973), *Dravidian Linguistics: An Introduction* (1990), and *Companion Studies to the History of Tamil Literature* (1992). His proposal involves two distinct moves.

First, on the relation of *tamiḻ* to *damiḷa* and *drāviḍa*, Zvelebil reversed Caldwell’s chain of derivation. He argued, on the basis of the chronological priority of Pali *damiḷa* over Sanskrit *drāviḍa* and the phonological naturalness of Indic adaptation of foreign *-ḻ-*.

> Tamil *tamiḻ* \> Prakrit *damiḷa-* (\> Pali *damiḷa-*, Sri Lankan
> Brāhmī *dameḍa-*, *damela-*) \> Sanskritised *drāviḍa-* (with regular
> Sanskritising hyper-correction of Prakrit *d-/-l-* into *dr-/-ḍ-* and
> the addition of a learned *-r-*).

The Sanskrit form, on this account, is an academic refashioning by Sanskrit grammarians and *paṇḍitas* of a Prakrit form already widely current.

Zvelebil writes (1990: xxi): “The forms *damiḷa/damila* almost certainly provide a connection of *dr(a/ā)viḍa* … *tamiḷ \< tamiẓ* … whereby the further development might have been *tamiẓ \> damiḷ \> damiḷa- → drāviḍa-*.”

This reversal is now accepted in mainstream Dravidian and Indological scholarship (Krishnamurti 2003: 2; Andronov 2003: 11; Steever 1998: 6; Mahadevan 2003).

Second, on the further etymology of *tamiḻ* itself, Zvelebil offered a tentative reconstruction:

> *tamiḻ* \< *tam-iḻ* \< *\*tav-iḻ* \< *\*tak-iḻ*

where *\*tak-* is the Old Tamil verbal root meaning “to be fitting, to be proper” (cf. Tamil *taku-* “to be suitable”, *takuti* “fitness, propriety”) and *-iḻ* is a suffix indicating “process, unfolding, sound” (cognate, on his account, with the *-iḻ* of *moḻi* “word, speech”). The whole would mean “the proper way of sounding,” “the fitting language,” a self-praising autonym of the kind common across language families. Zvelebil himself flagged the difficulty of the development *\*tak- \> tav- \> tam-* requires multiple unmotivated sound-changes, none of which is regular under known Proto-Dravidian phonology.

Bhadriraju Krishnamurti’s *The Dravidian Languages* (2003), the standard reference work for the family, treats the etymology with characteristic philological caution. In the opening pages of the book, Krishnamurti endorses Zvelebil’s *tamiḻ \> damiḷa \> drāviḍa* directionality unequivocally, on the chronological and phonological grounds noted above. The Sanskrit forms attested in the late period are *dramila-, dramiḍa-, draviḍa-, drāviḍa-* in the *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* (6th c.), *Daśakumāracarita* (7th c.), *Kādambarī* (early 7th c.), and *Avantisundarīkathā* of Daṇḍin (7th c.). Krishnamurti treats them as later Sanskritising elaborations, with the *-r-* a hyper-correct learned insertion modeled on Sanskrit phonotactics.

On the further etymology of *tamiḻ*, however, Krishnamurti is skeptical. He notes that none of the proposed compound analyses yield a fully regular derivation under the currently reconstructed Proto-Dravidian sound laws. He concludes that the etymology is, strictly speaking, unknown.

## The *\*tam-miḻ* Proposal of Southworth



Franklin C. Southworth, in his 1998 paper “On the Origin of the Word *Tamiẓ*“(*International Journal of Dravidian Linguistics* 27.1: 129–132) and in *Linguistic Archaeology of South Asia* (2005: 295), proposed:

> *tamiḻ* \< *\*tam-miḻ*

where *tam-* is the Proto-Dravidian self-referential/reflexive pronoun (”self, own”), a root well-established in comparative Dravidian (cf. Tamil *taṉ*, *tamatu*, Telugu *tana*, Kannada *tan-*, Malayalam *tan-*, all from *PDr \*tām*, “self”; DEDR 5160), and *-miḻ* is a reduced form of the verbal noun cognate with *moḻi* (மொழி, “word, speech”) \< *PDr \*moẓ-i*. The compound thus yields:

> *\*tam-miḻ* \> *tamiḻ* → our own resonant sounding-forth, our own
> characteristic utterance

Southworth’s proposal has been treated dismissively in most subsequent literature, principally on the grounds of an irregular vowel correspondence (Krishnamurti 2003: 2 n. 2). When the proposal’s components are examined in their full comparative-Dravidian and typological context, and when the proposal is reconstructed from the *miḻ-* root rather than from a phonologically reduced *moḻi*, the *\*tam-miḻ* derivation emerges as the most defensible of the existing proposals, requiring no irregular sound-changes and resting on a productive Old Tamil sound-root family that the Caṅkam corpus amply attests. It yields a plausible reconstruction whose semantic, morphological, and typological coherence outweighs its single phonological irregularity.

The first element of Southworth’s compound is on the firmest possible comparative footing. The Proto-Dravidian self-referential/reflexive/reciprocal pronoun *\*tām* with short-vowel allomorph *\*tam-* is DEDR 5160 in Burrow & Emeneau’s *A Dravidian Etymological Dictionary* (2nd ed., 1984), and is among the most thoroughly attested reconstructions in the family.

Malayalam *tām, tāṅṅaḷ, tan, tanne, taṅṅaḷ*

Kannada *tām, tān, tannu, tamma*

Telugu *tāmu, tana, tama, tāḍu*

Tulu *tāma, tana, tāmaḷ* Kota *tām, ta·n*

Toda *tom, tan*

Kodagu *tāma, taṇṇa* Gondi *taṟṟām, tana*

Kui *tān, tana*

Kuvi *tān, tana*

Malto *tām, tan-*

Brahui *tēn, ten-*

The pronoun functions across the family as a reflexive, reciprocal, and third-person honorific, and, crucially, as a possessive of collective self-identification, with the meaning of*'our own thing'*or*'what belongs to us as a group'*. In Tamil, *tam-* in compounds carries this last sense densely, where other words such as *tam-pi* (younger brother, the self-born brother), *tam-akkai* (elder sister), *tam-nāṭu / tam-nāṭṭār* (our country / our countrymen), *tam-uyir* (their own life), *tam-makkaḷ* (their own people/children), and *tam-moḻi* (their own language) all preserve the *tam-* element with this collective-possessive sense.

The compound *tam-moḻi* “our own language” is morphologically transparent and structurally parallel to many other *tam-* compounds in classical and modern Tamil. It stands in standing opposition to *vaṭa-moḻi* (வடமொழி, northern speech or Sanskrit), the latter explicitly codified in the *Tolkāppiyam*’s *Collatikāram* fourfold lexical classification (*iyar-col, vaṭa-moḻi, ticai-col, tisai-col*); the existence of the contrastive category *vaṭa-moḻi* presupposes a category of native speech for which *tam-moḻi* is the natural designation.

## Two Sound-Roots in Old Tamil



The proposal I would like to argue is that the second element of Southworth’s compound rests on the existence of a productive Old Tamil sound-root *miḻ-/imiḻ-*, distinct from but related to the standard root *moḻi*. It doesn’t require the phonological changes *moḻi* underwent an irregular vowel change to yield *miḻ*, but that Old Tamil possesses two parallel sound-roots, both productive in compound formation, and that *tamiḻ* is a compound formed directly from the second of these roots.

The standard Proto-Dravidian root for “word, speech, language” is DEDR 4989: *\*moḻ-i*. The verb means “to speak, to utter”; the deverbal noun *moḻi* means “word, speech, language.” It is one of the most frequent and culturally central roots in the family.

**DEDR 4989:**

PDr *\*moḻ-V*

Tamil *moḻi ‘to say, speak; n. word, saying, language.’*

Malayalam *moḻi ‘word’ miḻāvu, miḻaku ‘drum.’*

Kannada *moḻe* in older usage, *moḻagu ‘to sound as certain musical instruments’*

Telugu *mrō̃gu, mrōyu, mrōvu* ‘*to sound.’*

Konda *miṛi-*

Tulu *molpu*

Kota *moṛv* ‘*a child’s continuous crying.’*

Kolami *muḍ- (muṭṭ-) ‘to talk, speak (a language)’*

Toda *möṣ-*

Naikiri *muṛ-, muṭṭa ‘to speak, language.’*

Note carefully what DEDR 4989 itself records. The entry is conventionally headed by *moḻi* with *-o-*, but the sister languages preserve the root with significant vocalic variation. Malayalam preserves alongside *moḻi* the forms *miḻāvu* and *miḻaku*, meaning “drum,” that is, with *-i-* vocalism and the semantic field of resonant sound. Konda preserves *miṛi-* with *-i-*. Kolami preserves *muḍ-/muṭṭ-* with *-u-*. Telugu preserves *mrō-* forms specialized in the meaning “to sound.” The DEDR entry itself thus testifies to the vocalic plurality of the root family across Dravidian, and to the semantic specialization of certain *-i-* allomorphs in the field of resonant or instrumental sound. The Old Tamil situation is consistent with this comparative picture and not anomalous within it.

Krishnamurti’s objection to Southworth’s proposal rests on the assumption that Old Tamil should preserve only the *-o-* allomorph of this root family, and that any compound formed from the speech-root must therefore go through *moḻi* with *-o-*. The Caṅkam corpus shows otherwise.

## The *imiḻ-* Family in the Caṅkam Corpus



Old Tamil possesses, alongside the*moḻi*family, with*-o-*, a productive sound-root*imiḻ-/miḻ-*with*-i-*vocalism. This root is not a marginal residue or a hapax. It is one of the central sound-verbs of the Caṅkam-period poetic vocabulary, attested across multiple major anthologies and forming productive compounds throughout the corpus.

The verbal stem *miḻaṟṟu* (மிழற்று) “to babble, to chirp, to speak indistinctly, to twitter” is recorded in the *Madras Tamil Lexicon* (1924–1936), with the verbal noun *miḻaṟṟal* (மிழற்றல்) and the agent noun *miḻaṟṟi* (a babbler, a child speaking, a bird singing). The verb is attested in the Caṅkam-period poetry.

***Akanāṉūṟu*** **54**

> ***நீர் குடி சுவையின் தீவிய மிழற்றி***
>
> *nīr kuṭi cuvaiyiṉ tīviya miḻaṟṟi*
>
> *“like the sweetness of drinking water, and talks sweetly”*

**Paṭṭiṉappālai 20**

> ***பைங்கிளி மிழற்றும் பால் ஆர் செழு நகர்த்***
>
> *paiṅkiḷi miḻaṟṟum pāl ār cĕḻu nakart*
>
> *“the rich city where green parrots chirp, full of milk”*

The verbal stem *imiḻ-* (இமிழ்) “to resound, to boom, to ring out” appears across the Caṅkam corpus with even greater frequency, and forms a remarkable range of productive compounds in the head and modifier positions. The pattern is consistent across the corpus. The *imiḻ-* root forms finite verbs (*imiḻa, imiḻntu, imiḻnta*), participles, and a substantial set of compounds in which it occupies the head or modifier position: *imiḻ-icai* (resounding music), *imiḻ-iyam* (resounding instrument), *imiḻ-muracu* (resounding drum), *imiḻ-muḻakku* (resounding roar), *pāṭ-imiḻ* (song-resounding), *teri-imiḻ* (clearly-resounding). This is exactly the same morphological pattern as the *moḻi* compounds (*vāy-moḻi, vaṭa-moḻi, tāy-moḻi, tam-moḻi, tiru-moḻi, kaṭu-moḻi*). The two roots, *imiḻ-* and *moḻi*, are morphologically parallel, behaving as a productive sound-verb pair occupying the same compositional slot.

The semantic field of the *imiḻ-* root is the full range of dignified expressive resonance. The root is, in cultural-semantic terms, the verb of choice for describing the sound-side of culturally significant occasions, the moments when a community comes together to recognize itself in dignified shared sound.

The *-i-/-o-* alternation in this root family is therefore not a marginal anomaly to be explained away by ad hoc reduction. It is a productive Old Tamil pattern attested across the major *Eṭṭuttokai* and *Pattuppāṭṭu* anthologies, consistent with the limited but real Proto-Dravidian apophony Krishnamurti himself documents (2003: 90–94), and convergent with the comparative evidence from Malayalam *miḻāvu/miḻaku*, Konda *miṛi-*, and the broader DEDR 4989 picture.

## The Compound *\*tam-miḻ* \> *tamiḻ*



On this analysis, Southworth’s reconstruction *\*tam-miḻ* is a compound formed from the *miḻ-* root drawn from the *-i-* allomorph of the sound-verb family, not a phonologically reduced compound formed from *moḻi*. The compound consists of:

- *tam-*: the well-attested Proto-Dravidian
  self-referential/reflexive/collective-possessive pronoun (DEDR 5160),
  reflected in Tamil *taṉ, tām, tamatu, tammil*, and productive across
  Tamil compounds in the sense “one’s own / our own.”

- *-miḻ*: the *-i-* allomorph of the sound-root family, drawn directly
  from the *imiḻ-/miḻ-* family attested across the Caṅkam corpus.

The meaning of the compound is “our own resounding/sounding-forth, our own characteristic resonant utterance, our own *imiḻ-icai*.” The compound is morphologically of the same type as *vaṭa-moḻi* “northern speech” (modifier + sound-root noun): *tam-* modifies the sound-root *miḻ*, naming a particular kind of expressive resonance, namely, “ours.”

The phonological derivation from *\*tam-miḻ* to surviving *tamiḻ* requires only one regular Tamil sound-change. The compound *\*tam-miḻ* contains, at the morpheme seam, a doubled *mm* from the final *m* of *tam-* meeting the initial *m* of *-miḻ*. Tamil regularly simplifies geminate clusters at compound boundaries when the compound becomes lexicalized. Krishnamurti (2003: 165–167) documents this as a standard process of cluster simplification in Dravidian; Steever (1998) discusses it specifically for Tamil. The regular outcome is *\*tammiḻ* \> *tamiḻ*, with one *m* dropped. The form *miḻ* in *\*tam-miḻ* already ends in the consonant *-ḻ*; there is no final short vowel to drop. The reconstruction does not require apocope. It does not require any vowel changes. It does not require any irregular sound changes. The total phonological journey from *\*tam-miḻ* to *tamiḻ* is a single regular degemination.

This is the cleanest possible derivation. The compound is built from elements that are both productive in Old Tamil. The phonology requires one regular sound change. The result matches the surviving form exactly. The reconstruction is, by every standard philological criterion, well-formed.

## *Tam-moḻi* and *tamiḻ* as Parallel Compounds



The productive Tamil compound *tam-moḻi* “our own language” coexists alongside the autonym *tamiḻ* in classical and modern Tamil. On the parallel-root analysis, this is exactly what we should expect. *Tam-moḻi* is a transparent compound formed from the *moḻi* root, used productively when speakers want to refer to “one’s own/our own language” as a descriptive concept. *Tamiḻ* is an earlier compound formed from the *miḻ-* root, used as the proper-name autonym of the language community. The two compounds are parallel formations from related but distinct roots, not phonological variants of a single compound.

This parallelism explains a fact that the older*tam-moḻi*\>*tamiḻ*derivation could not easily account for: the productive*tam-moḻi*compound has not displaced the fossilized*tamiḻ*, and*tamiḻ*has not been reanalyzed as a phonologically reduced*tam-moḻi*. The two have coexisted because they are not the same word at different stages of reduction. They are different words formed from different roots, occupying different functional slots in the language. *Tam-moḻi* is the analytic “our language”; *tamiḻ* is the autonym “Tamil.” They are siblings, not parent and child.

## The *Akanāṉūṟu* 227 Attestation



Most striking of all the Caṅkam attestations is *Akanāṉūṟu* 227.

> ***தமிழ் அகப் படுத்த இமிழிசை முரசின்***
>
> *tamiḻ akap-paṭutta imiḻ-icai muracin*
>
> *“of the drum whose resounding music (imiḻ-icai) has gathered Tamil
> (tamiḻ) into itself”*

The line places *tamiḻ* and *imiḻ-icai* in metrical proximity and conceptual continuity within a single hemistich. The great royal *muracu* drum, the standard Caṅkam emblem of dignified collective sound, of sovereignty, of the audible self-presentation of community, is described as having “gathered Tamil into itself” through its *imiḻ-icai*. Tamil identity is constituted, in the line, by participation in resonant sound. The poem’s underlying logic is that the community’s name and the community’s characteristic resonance belong to a single order of meaning.

The paronomastic echo between *tam-iḻ* and *im-iḻ*, both ending in -iḻ, is, in a poet of Caṅkam metrical sophistication, almost certainly deliberate. The Caṅkam poets are virtuosos of sound-patterning; the *aṇi* (ornament) tradition cultivates exactly this kind of *yamaka*-like sonic kinship between thematically related words. The audible echo *tam-iḻ ... im-iḻ* in *Akanāṉūṟu* 227 exploits, and presupposes, that *tamiḻ* and *imiḻ* are felt to share a sonic-semantic kinship.

This evidence is internal to the Caṅkam corpus itself, and it perceives *tamiḻ* and *imiḻ* as belonging to the same sonic-semantic order. The minimal claim from this evidence is that the *-iḻ* shared by *tamiḻ* and *imiḻ-* was perceptually salient to Caṅkam-period Tamil ears. The maximal claim is that the *Akanāṉūṟu* 227 poet was consciously activating an etymological awareness that *tamiḻ* contains a *-miḻ* element belonging to the *imiḻ-* family. Even the minimal claim supports the reconstruction. It means that the *-miḻ* of *\*tam-miḻ* was a perceptually available morpheme in the Caṅkam-period speech community, available for poetic exploitation, which is precisely what the parallel-root analysis predicts.

Southworth’s reconstruction *\*tam-miḻ*, on this reading, is not an external philological hypothesis projected onto the language. It is the recovery of an etymological structure that the Caṅkam poets themselves heard and exploited.

## The Cultural-Semantic Field



The parallel-root analysis enables a deeper interpretation of the autonym*tamiḻ*. If the *-miḻ* of *tamiḻ* belongs to the *imiḻ-* family, then *tamiḻ* names the language community in the same semantic terms that the corpus uses for moments of dignified collective resonance.

What unites the contexts in which *imiḻ-* appears? They are all moments of organized collective experience, moments when a community comes together to recognize itself in a dignified shared sound: the wedding gathered by drums, the kingship gathered by the *muracu*, the warrior gathered into himself by his own resonance, the dancers gathered in the *tuṇaṅkai*, the listener gathered into the resounding sea or sky. The*imiḻ-*family does not describe random noise; it describes structured, meaningful, collectively recognized resonance, sound that constitutes occasion, that gathers community, that proclaims order, that organizes participation.

If *tamiḻ* is *\*tam-miḻ*, “our own *imiḻ-*“, then the autonym names the language community in exactly the same conceptual terms that the corpus consistently uses for moments of organized collective auditory recognition. *Tamiḻ*is not just “our speech”; it is the resonance by which the community recognizes itself, parallel to the*imiḻ-icai muracu*that gathers the kingdom and the*imiḻ-iyam*that organizes the music. The autonym, on this reading, claims for the language community the same dignified status as the royal drum and the classical instrument: a culturally constituted resonance through which a collective identity becomes audible to itself.

## Krishnamurti’s Objections



The original Krishnamurti objection to Southworth was that the regular Proto-Dravidian root for “speech” is *\*moḻ-* with *-o-*, and that the change *o \> i* required to derive *tamiḻ* from a *tam-moḻi* compound is not a regular Dravidian sound-change. This objection, as stated, is correct. The change of *o \> i* is not regular, and a derivation *tam-moḻi* \> *tamiḻ* therefore depends on an irregular and unmotivated step.

The parallel-root analysis answers this objection decisively, not by attempting to rehabilitate the irregular sound-change, but by dissolving the premise on which the objection rests. The objection assumes that *tamiḻ* must be derived from *moḻi* (with *-o-*) by some sequence of reductions including a vowel change. The parallel-root analysis shows that this assumption is wrong. *Tamiḻ* is not derived from *moḻi* at all. It is derived from a separate but related root *miḻ-/imiḻ-* (with *-i-*), which is independently attested across the Caṅkam corpus as a productive root of Old Tamil.

On this analysis, Krishnamurti’s question, “how does *moḻi* become *miḻ*?”, does not arise. The two roots coexist in Old Tamil as distinct productive elements. The autonym *tamiḻ* is built directly from the *-i-* root, with no intermediate sound changes. The productive compound *tam-moḻi* “our own language” is built from the *-o-* root and remains transparently formed throughout the language’s history. Both compounds are well-formed; neither is a phonological reduction of the other.

What of the relationship between the two roots? In the parallel-root analysis, this becomes a separate question, one that can be addressed in its own terms, not under the pressure to derive *tamiḻ* from *moḻi*. The two roots may be reflexes of an ablauting Proto-Dravidian root *\*m-ẓ-* with *o*/*i* alternation, parallel to the limited apophony Krishnamurti himself documents (2003: 90–94). They may be two closely related sister-roots sharing the *m-ẓ-* shape but historically distinct. Not to mention the *muḻ-* verb root reflex in Old Tamil with a similar semantic meaning. They may be independent forms that have come to share semantic territory through convergence. The argument for *tamiḻ* \< *\*tam-miḻ* does not require us to choose between these alternatives. Whichever historical-comparative analysis turns out to be correct, the Old Tamil situation is the same: two productive sound-roots coexist, and the autonym is formed from one of them. The Krishnamurti objection, framed as a problem of irregular sound-change, simply does not apply to the parallel-root analysis.

## The Strength of the Reconstruction



The compound *\*tam-miḻ* is structurally regular, a *tam-* element compounded with a sound-root noun. This is the same compound type as *vaṭa-moḻi* “northern speech,” *kaṭu-moḻi* “harsh word,” *pāṭ-imiḻ* “song-resounding,” and many others. There is nothing morphologically unusual about the formation.

The derivation from *\*tam-miḻ* to surviving *tamiḻ* requires only one regular sound-change: degemination of *m-m* across the compound morpheme boundary, a standard Dravidian process documented by Krishnamurti and Steever. No vowel changes are required. No apocope is required. The phonological journey is the most economical possible derivation from reconstruction to surviving form.

Both elements of the compound are independently attested. The first element *tam-* is amply attested across Dravidian (DEDR 5160) and is productive in Tamil compounds. The second element *miḻ-/imiḻ-* is amply attested in Caṅkam Tamil through citations across *Akanāṉūṟu*, *Maturai-k-kāñci*, *Kalittokai*, and *Malaipaṭukaṭām*. Both elements are real components of Old Tamil; the compound is built from real material, not from inferred or hypothetical roots.

Cross-linguistically, autonyms meaning “our speech,” “our sounding-forth,” “our characteristic utterance” are among the most common patterns of language-community naming. *\*tam-miḻ* fits this typological pattern exactly. The formation of an autonym meaning “our own resonant utterance” fits the contact situation of pre-classical Tamiḻakam. The *Tolkāppiyam*’s codified opposition between *iyaṟ-col* (native words), *vaṭa-moḻi* (northern/Sanskrit words), *ticai-col* (regional words), and *tiri-col* (foreign words) presupposes exactly the sociolinguistic awareness that would generate a self-name for the language community. The autonym crystallizes a self/other distinction that the *Tolkāppiyam* itself codifies.

## Two Tongues



Tamil is among the most rigorously diglossic of the world’s literary languages. Charles A. Ferguson, in his classic 1959 paper, took it alongside Modern Greek (Katharevousa / Demotic), Arabic (al-fuṣḥā / al-ʿāmmiyya), Swiss German (Schriftdeutsch / Schweizerdeutsch), and Haitian Creole (français / kreyòl) as one of his four exemplary cases of diglossia, defined as a stable bilingualism within a single language community in which a “High” (H) variety, codified, learned through formal education, and used in formal writing, oratory, religion, and education, coexists with a “Low” (L) variety, learned natively, used in conversation, personal communication, and most spoken life. The two varieties have complementary distribution, mutually exclusive functions, and grammatical, phonological, and lexical differences sufficient that a speaker of L who has not been schooled cannot fully understand H.

Tamil’s H variety is *centamiḻ* (செந்தமிழ், “refined Tamil,” literally “straight/proper Tamil,” *cem-* + *tamiḻ*), which is the descendant of the literary register of the *Caṅkam* and *bhakti* poets, codified by the *Tolkāppiyam* and stabilized by the medieval grammarians. Its L variety is *koṭuntamiḻ* (கொடுந்தமிழ், “crooked Tamil,” “deviant Tamil,” *koṭu-* + *tamiḻ*), the umbrella term for the regional, social, and stylistic colloquials of everyday speech. The terms themselves come from the *Tolkāppiyam*, long predating Ferguson by some two millennia.

### Ceyyuḷ and Vaḻakku



The *Tolkāppiyam* makes two related distinctions that together constitute its theory of register and dialect.

The first is between *ceyyuḷ-moḻi* (செய்யுள் மொழி, “the language of composed verse”) and *vaḻakku-moḻi* (வழக்கு மொழி, “the language of practice/usage”). *Ceyyuḷ* is the elevated language of poetic composition, governed by metrical, prosodic, and stylistic rules; *vaḻakku* is the common speech of ordinary life. The distinction is not a binary of “literary vs. spoken” simpliciter;*vaḻakku*itself can be written, and Tolkāppiyar provides for written*vaḻakku*in correspondence and inscription, but rather a distinction of stylistic register, with *ceyyuḷ* as the marked, codified, prestige form. The *Collatikāram* (சொல்லதிகாரம், “Treatise on the Word”), *Eccaviyal* and *Iṭaiyiyal* sections deal extensively with the morphological and syntactic differences between the two: contracted verb-forms, pronoun-substitutions, word-order liberties of poetry, all permitted in *ceyyuḷ* and proscribed in formal *vaḻakku*.

The second distinction is geographical and dialectal. The *Tolkāppiyam* identifies a central *centamiḻ-nāṭu* (செந்தமிழ் நாடு, “the land of refined Tamil”), analogous to Āryāvarta of Sanskritic traditions, bounded, according to the formulation made canonical by the medieval commentators, by the Veṅkaṭam hills (Tirupati) in the north and Kumari (Kanyakumari) in the south, with the western and eastern seas on either side. Surrounding this central region are twelve *koṭuntamiḻ-nāṭu* (கொடுந்தமிழ் நாடு, “lands of crooked/dialectal Tamil”): the *Pāṇṭi-nāṭu, Veṇ-nāṭu, Kuṭa-nāṭu, Kuṭṭa-nāṭu, Karkā-nāṭu, Pūḻi-nāṭu, Aruvā-nāṭu, Aruvā-vaṭa-talai, Cītam-nāṭu, Malaya-nāṭu, Kuṟumpōṟai-nāṭu* and *Teṉ-pāṇṭi-nāṭu*. The dialects of these regions are*koṭuntamiḻ*, recognizably Tamil but locally inflected, less standardized, and, in Tolkāppiyar’s view, less suitable for formal composition.

The *Collatikāram* further provides a four-fold classification of words by source: *iyaṟ-col* (இயற்சொல், native Tamil words), *tiri-col* (திரிசொல், regional/dialectal words from the *koṭuntamiḻ* areas), *vaṭa-col* / *vaṭa-moḻi* (வடசொல், Sanskrit / northern words), and *ticai-col* (திசைச்சொல், foreign words from non-Tamil non-Sanskrit sources). Rules of nativization are specified: Sanskrit consonants alien to Tamil (*kha, gha, cha, jha, ṭha, ḍha, tha, dha, pha, bha, śa, ṣa, sa, ha*) must be adapted to native Tamil phonology, normally by substitution to the corresponding plain stop or fricative.

This is, in nuce, a pre-modern theory of diglossia: a recognition of stylistic registers, dialectal variation, lexical layering, and codification. Annamalai (2017: 480) observes that the *Tolkāppiyam*’s framework “anticipates the H/L architecture by some two thousand years,” though framed in the indigenous terms of *ceyyuḷ* / *vaḻakku* and *centamiḻ* / *koṭuntamiḻ* rather than as a theory of social diglossia.

### Medieval persistence



The diglossic divide deepens in the medieval period. The *bhakti* hymns of the *Tēvāram* (தேவாரம், 6th–9th c.), *Tiruvācakam* (திருவாசகம், 9th c.), and the *Nālāyira-tivya-pirapantam* (நாலாயிர திவ்விய பிரபந்தம், 6th–9th c.) develop a literary Tamil that is recognizably continuous with *Caṅkam centamiḻ* but more open to Sanskrit lexical borrowing. The temple inscriptions of the *Cōḻa* (சோழ) period (9th–13th c.), many thousands of them, on the walls of *Tañcāvūr* (தஞ்சாவூர்), *Kuṭantai* (குடந்தை), *Citamparam* (சிதம்பரம்) and elsewhere record an administrative and religious Tamil that is itself an intermediate register, more formal than spoken Tamil but less archaic than literary *centamiḻ*.

The grammatical tradition continues: the *Naṉṉūl* (நன்னூல், c. 13th c.) of Pavaṇanti Muṉivar replaces the *Tolkāppiyam* as the working pedagogical grammar; the *Vīracōḻiyam* (வீரசோழியம், 11th c.) attempts to bring Tamil into Sanskritic grammatical templates. The medieval commentators on the *Tolkāppiyam*, Iḷampūraṇar (12th c.), Cēṉāvaraiyar (13th c.), Pērāciriyar (13th c.), Naccinārkkiniyar (14th c.) are the great expositors of the H / L architecture.

By the late medieval period (14th–17th c.), the gap between literary Tamil and spoken Tamil had widened significantly. The literary register has remained relatively conservative, while the spoken language has undergone sound changes (loss of*-r-*in certain environments, vowel reductions, simplifications of verb forms) that produce a recognizably modern colloquial. The vehicular Tamil of the *Vijayanagar* (15th–16th c.) and *Nāyak* (16th–18th c.) periods represents a further administrative register, with extensive *vaṭa-moḻi* (Sanskrit and Telugu) elements.

## Discussion



In conclusion, the connection between the two questions of this essay. The *Tolkāppiyam*, in its very formulation of the metalinguistic phrase *tamiḻ-eṉ-kiḷavi* (" the word called *tamiḻ*“), is performing an act that simultaneously names the language and constitutes it as the proper object of grammatical reflection. The same text classifies that language as having two principal modes, *ceyyuḷ* and *vaḻakku*, *centamiḻ* and *koṭuntamiḻ,* and devotes its enormous intellectual labor to the codification of the higher mode.

What this means is that the name *tamiḻ*, in the canonical Tamil tradition, refers most properly to the codified high variety: when Tolkāppiyar speaks of *tamiḻ,* he means *centamiḻ*, the language as it should be, the language obedient to grammatical norm. The *koṭuntamiḻ* dialects are *tamiḻ* too, but in a derivative sense, *tamiḻ* deviated, *tamiḻ* in regional inflection.

The result is a Tamil whose name,*tamiḻ*, now has, perhaps, a wider semantic embrace than at any earlier moment, referring with comparable legitimacy to literary*centamiḻ*, to regional*koṭuntamiḻ*, to the language of cinema, of political oratory, of the WhatsApp message, and ofthe academic monograph. In this multiplicity we may see the modern fulfilment of what was already implicit in the *Tolkāppiyam*’s original act of naming: the recognition that *tamiḻ* is not one variety but an architecture of varieties, governed by the central registers of *ceyyuḷ* and *vaḻakku* but extending across a continuum of formality, region and social position, and held together by the shared name and the shared retroflex *-ḻ-* of which that name is itself the bearer.
