# Kampaṉ: Introduction to Irāmāvatāram, the Tamil Rāmāyaṇa

In the Cōḻa country of the twelfth century CE, the dates 1180–1250 CE are conventionally given, though debated, in the village of Tēraḻuntūr (தேரழுந்தூர்) on the Kāvēri delta, lived a poet whom Tamil literature would crown *Kavi-cakravarti*, “Emperor of Poets.” Kampaṉ (popularly Kamban கம்பன் or Kambar கம்பர்) is, by tradition, the son of a temple drummer, raised in the household of his patron Caṭaiyappa Vaḷḷal, a chieftain of the Paṇṇai lineage at Tiruveṇṇainallūr. In gratitude, Kampaṉ embeds his patron’s name once in every thousand verses of the work that bears his own.

That work is the *Irāmāvatāram* (Tamil: *இராமவதாரம்*, Sanskrit: *Rāmāvatāram*), known almost universally as the *Kampa Rāmāyaṇam (கம்ப ராமாயணம்)*. It comprises some 10,500 *viruttam* verses (Hart counts 10,534) distributed across six *kāṇṭams*, *Pāla*, *Ayōttiyā*, *Āraṇya*, *Kiṭkintā*, *Cuntara*, and *Yutta*. Kampaṉ himself stopped at the *Yutta-kāṇṭam*; the *Uttara-kāṇṭam* found in many later printed editions is the work of his contemporary Oṭṭakkūttar.

To call Kampaṉ a translator of Vālmīki is to mistake the work the way one would mistake calling Virgil a translator of Homer. The Irāmāvatāram is the Rāmāyaṇa refracted through Tamil prosody, Caṅkam aesthetics, the *bhakti* religiosity of the Āḻvārs, and the courtly grandeur of Cōḻa-period civic and architectural imagination.

## Case for the Tamil Epic’s Preeminence



Tamil scholars have argued for centuries that the Irāmāvatāram surpasses its Sanskrit source. The strongest scholar of Kampaṉ in English, George L. Hart, declines the comparison itself as absurd, like comparing apples and oranges, and he is right that the question is unhelpfully framed. What can be said without polemic is that the Tamil epic does several things its Sanskrit source does not attempt, and does so with a poetic intensity for which the *bhakti* tradition has long claimed preeminence.

Tamil prosody is a stricter craft than the Sanskrit *anuṣṭubh*. Kampaṉ deploys *etukai* (second-syllable rhyme) and *mōṉai* (initial alliteration) such that the consonants of a battle scene clatter, the vowels of a love scene melt, and the meter shifts to fit the mood of each *paṭalam*. Hart, in his introduction to *The Forest Book*, notes that the meter changes to reflect the content and that the verses adhere faithfully to the difficult rules of Tamil poetry.

Vālmīki’s Rāvaṇa is largely a *rākṣasa* who must be slain. Kampaṉ’s Rāvaṇa is a tragic emperor, a Śaiva devotee, a Vedic scholar, capable of restraint, ultimately undone by a single flaw rather than defined by evil. The Tamil Rāvaṇa famously refuses to touch Sītā against her will, and waits, with full knowledge, for his end. David Shulman, in his book Divine Order and Divine Evil in the Tamil Tale of Rāma (1979), reads that Kampaṉ’s Rāvaṇa as essential to his theological architecture: the epic is no longer the triumph of *dharma* but the tragedy of *adharma* lodged in a great soul.

Kampaṉ’s Sītā speaks more, refuses more, and chooses more. Shulman, in his book Fire and Flood: The Testing of Sītā in Kampaṉ’s *Irāmāvatāram* (1991), traces how the Tamil agni-parīkṣā carries a sharper indictment of Rāma than Vālmīki ever permits.

By Kampaṉ’s time, the *bhakti* movement had transformed the religious imagination of the Tamil country. The Āḻvārs had poured upon Tirumāl the melting devotional address that Kampaṉ now turns upon his Rāma, who is unmistakably Viṣṇu incarnate. The epic's emotional register shifts from heroic to hymnal. Kampaṉ inherits the Caṅkam tradition of *tiṇai*, the five interior landscapes (*kuṟiñci*, *mullai*, *marutam*, *neytal*, *pālai*), each of which encodes its own emotional grammar. His Rāma walks through a *Tamil* forest, with its monsoons, its birds, its prescribed moods. His Ayōttiyā is built with the architectural specificity of a Cōḻa capital. The story stops being foreign and starts being native.

## The Invocation: Kaṭavuḷ Vāḻttu



Kampaṉ’s *Irāmāvatāram* opens with a single quatrain that establishes its theological frame.

> உலகம் யாவையும் தாம் உளவாக்கலும்  
> நிலைபெறுத்தலும், நீக்கலும், நீங்கலா   
> அலகிலா விளையாட்டு உடையார், அவர்   
> தலைவர்; அன்னவர்க்கே சரண் நாங்களே.
>
> *ulakam yāvaiyum tām uḷavākkalum,* *nilai-peṟuttalum, nīkkalum,
> nīṅkalā* *alakilā viḷaiyāṭṭu uṭaiyār, avar* *talaivar; aṉṉavarkkē
> caraṇ nāṅkaḷē.*
>
> *He from whom all the worlds come into being,* *by whom they are held,
> into whom they dissolve,* *whose unending, untiring play has no
> measure,* *He alone is Lord; to Him alone do we surrender.*

In his first four lines, Kampaṉ establishes that this is not merely a hero’s tale but a god’s. The phrase*alakilā viḷaiyāṭṭu,*meaning play without measure, is the Tamil expression of*līlā*. Notably, one general observation is that the uses *tām uḷa-vākkalum*, a phrasing consistent with the Vedāntic view that creation is the unfolding of what already is.

## Coup de foudre: First Glances



When Rāma, walking through Mithilā, first sees Sītā on her balcony, Kampaṉ pauses the entire epic for one *viruttam*. Vālmīki dispatches the meeting in a few pleasant *ślokas*. Kampaṉ gives the following verses, perhaps the single most-cited stanza in all of Tamil literature, located in the *Mithilai-kāṭci-paṭalam* of the *Pāla Kāṇṭam*.

> *eṇṇarum nalattiṉāḷ iṉaiyaḷ niṉṟuḻi,* *kaṇṇoṭu kaṇṇiṉaik kavvi, oṉṟai
> oṉṟu* *uṇṇavum nilai peṟātu uṇarvum oṉṟiṭa,* *aṇṇalum nōkkiṉāṉ; avaḷum
> nōkkiṉāḷ.*

In Tamil script:

> எண்ணரும் நலத்தினாள் இனையள் நின்றுழி,   
> கண்ணொடு கண்ணினைக் கவ்வி, ஒன்றை ஒன்று   
> உண்ணவும் நிலை பெறாது உணர்வும் ஒன்றிட,   
> அண்ணலும் நோக்கினான்; அவளும் நோக்கினாள்.
>
> *eṇṇarum nalattiṉāḷ iṉaiyaḷ niṉṟuḻi,* *kaṇṇoṭu kaṇṇiṉaik kavvi, oṉṟai
> oṉṟu* *uṇṇavum nilai peṟātu uṇarvum oṉṟiṭa,* *aṇṇalum nōkkiṉāṉ; avaḷum
> nōkkiṉāḷ*
>
> *She of beauty beyond reckoning stood there,* *her eyes seized his
> eyes, and his eyes seized hers, each devouring the other, unable to
> hold still, their very awareness fusing,* *He gazed at her, she gazed
> back at him, locked eyes.*

The whole verse builds toward those final six syllables: *aṇṇalum nōkkiṉāṉ; avaḷum nōkkiṉāḷ*. *He gazed at her, she gazed back at him, and they locked eyes.* Coup de foudre, a French phrase frequently used in English, literally means a bolt of lightning, which is something we can compare to this stanza. It signifies an immediate, shocking, and overwhelming romantic connection.

To see why this is the most famous stanza in Tamil, look at the sound architecture. The *etukai*, second-letter rhyme falls on the geminate retroflex *ṇṇ*: *e**ṇṇ**arum, ka**ṇṇ**oṭu, u**ṇṇ**avum, a**ṇṇ**alum*. Four lines, four lines locked at the second syllable on the same doubled retroflex nasal. Then count the related n-class consonants across the rest of the verse, *nalattiṉāḷ, iṉaiyaḷ, niṉṟuḻi, kaṇṇiṉai, oṉṟai, oṉṟu, nilai, uṇarvum, oṉṟiṭa, nōkkiṉāṉ, nōkkiṉāḷ*. The verse is saturated with*ṇ*and*n*sounds, roughly 20 nasal consonants in 28 syllables. The retroflex *ṇṇ* is among the most distinctive sounds in Tamil, a long, held, intimate consonant produced with the tongue curled to the back of the palate. The verse *sounds* like two people slowly recognizing each other, held in a single continuous nasal hum like an indrawn breath.

And the final line is built as a perfect mirror. *aṇṇalum nōkkiṉāṉ - avaḷum nōkkiṉāḷ* seven syllables on each side; both halves opening on *a-*; both halves containing the same verb *nōkkiṉ-* ”looked, gazed”; the only difference is the masculine/feminine subject *aṇṇal*, “lord”, and *avaḷ*, “she”, and the masculine/feminine past-finite ending *-āṉ* and *-āḷ*. The grammar of the line is the meaning of the line. Two souls recognize each other before either knows what it is they recognize, and the syntax says so by becoming its own reflection. This is what *etukai* and *mōṉai* and metrical symmetry can do that no flat translation can deliver.

The episode, incidentally, is *not* in Vālmīki’s; it is a Tamil interpolation that later surfaces in Tulasīdās’s sixteenth-century Avadhī *Rāmacaritamānasa*, almost certainly under Kampaṉ’s influence.
