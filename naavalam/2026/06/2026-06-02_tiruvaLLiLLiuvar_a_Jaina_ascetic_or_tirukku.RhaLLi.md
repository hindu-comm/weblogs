## The Problem of the *Kāmattuppāl*



The *Kāmattuppāl* (காமத்துப்பால், Book of Love, *Kuṟaḷ* 1081–1330) is the third and final book of the *Tirukkuṟaḷ*. It comprises 25 chapters and 250 couplets, devoted in their entirety to the experience of erotic love between human beings. Within the architecture of the three books, *Aṟam* (virtue), *Poruḷ* (statecraft and wealth), *Iṉpam* (pleasure/love), the *Kāmattuppāl* presents the most interpretive difficulty for the Jain reading of Vaḷḷuvar.

The difficulty is structural. The first two books fit comfortably within the *trivarga* organization shared by Jain householder ethics, Brahmanical *dharmaśāstra*, and the wider pan-Indian *nīti* tradition. The third book does not. Its subject matter, clandestine union, lovesick longing, the lover’s quarrel, the heroine’s confidante, the long nights of separation, belongs to no Jain canonical or commentarial tradition. Nor does it belong to Vātsyāyana’s *Kāmasūtra*, despite the surface-level overlap of subject. Nor does it anticipate the later Bhakti erotic-devotional mode of the Āḻvārs and Nāyaṉārs, despite a real shared vocabulary.

Where, then, does the *Kāmattuppāl* come from? The answer lies within the indigenous Tamil *akam* poetic tradition: a body of conventions older than Vaḷḷuvar, codified in the *Tolkāppiyam*’s *Poruḷatikāram*, and exemplified across the eight Caṅkam anthologies (*Eṭṭuttokai*) and the ten long songs (*Pattuppāṭṭu*). These foundations are well established in Tamil scholarship (Ramanujan, Hart, Zvelebil, Selby, Shulman). Still, the systematic application of the *Kāmattuppāl* as the decisive piece of evidence for Vaḷḷuvar’s Tamil identity is not so much by prior scholarship.

### The Akam Tradition: An Indigenous Tamil Poetics



The *Tolkāppiyam* (தொல்காப்பியம்), the foundational treatise on Tamil grammar and poetics, is organized into three books: *Eḻuttatikāram* (phonology and orthography), *Collatikāram* (morphology and syntax), and *Poruḷatikāram* (poetics and subject matter). The *Poruḷatikāram* opens with the *Akattiṇaiyiyal* (அகத்திணையியல்) (chapter on *akam* conventions), followed by the *Puṟattiṇaiyiyal* (chapter on *puṟam* conventions), and includes the dedicated chapters *Kaḷaviyal* (களவியல்) (on clandestine love) and *Kaṟpiyal* (கற்பியல்) (on wedded love). Dating of the*Tolkāppiyam is* layered and contested. Zvelebil (*The Smile of Murugan*, Brill, 1973) places the text’s core in the first century BCE to the second century CE, with the *Poruḷatikāram* strata extending to the fourth–fifth centuries CE; Herman Tieken’s minority late dating (*Kāvya in South India*, Egbert Forsten, 2001) has been criticized by Hart, Monius, and others. Whatever the precise dates, the *Tolkāppiyam*’s *akam* framework predates the *Tirukkuṟaḷ* and supplies the structural grammar within which the *Kāmattuppāl* operates.

The foundational binary of Tamil poetics is *akam* (அகம், interior) and *puṟam* (புறம், exterior). *Akam* poetry concerns the private, intimate, and personal, love and its dramas; *puṟam* concerns the public, war, death, kingship, fame, and ethics. As A. K. Ramanujan put it in *Poems of Love and War* (Columbia University Press, 1985), the distinction is in-ness and out-ness. Zvelebil (1973) calls it the fundamental binary opposition of Caṅkam aesthetics; Kailasapathy (*Tamil Heroic Poetry*, Clarendon Press, 1968) treats it as central to the entire Caṅkam corpus; Shulman (*Tamil: A Biography*, Belknap/Harvard University Press, 2016) reaffirms it as the architecture of classical Tamil literary experience.

This binary has no parallel in Sanskrit literary theory. Sanskrit *kāvya* organizes by genre (*mahākāvya*, *nāṭaka*, *muktaka*), by *rasa* (the eight or nine aesthetic moods), or by subject matter, but not by the interior/exterior distinction in the structural way Tamil does. The *akam–puṟam* binary is one of the strongest markers of pre-Sanskritic Tamil literary autonomy.

### Five Tiṇai and Akam Dramatis Personae



*Akam* poetry is organized around five landscape-mood complexes (*tiṇai*, திணை), each combining a physical setting with a corresponding emotional situation. Each *tiṇai* is a triadic structure of *mutaṟporuḷ* (the primary elements: time and place), *karupporuḷ* (the secondary elements: flora, fauna, and the occupations of the inhabitants), and *uripporuḷ* (the proper subject: the emotional situation). The system is rigorously codified in the *Tolkāppiyam*’s *Akattiṇaiyiyal* and analyzed at length by Ramanujan (*The Interior Landscape*, Indiana University Press, 1967, Afterword) and Selby (*Tamil Love Poetry*, Columbia University Press, 2011).

Two additional *tiṇai* sit at the margins of the five: *kaikkiḷai* (one-sided or unrequited love) and *peruntiṇai* (mismatched or excessive passion). These are explicitly classified by the *Tolkāppiyam* as deviations from the proper five, not as further proper *tiṇai* themselves (Zvelebil 1973). The Kuṟaḷ’s invocation of clandestine union, anxious separation, and the lovers’ quarrel all fall squarely within the proper five.

*Akam's* poetry deploys a small cast of named character types whose speeches are not narrated by an authorial voice but are spoken directly. The standard cast comprises *Talaivaṉ* (தலைவன்), the hero/lover, *Talaivi* (தலைவி), the heroine/beloved, *Tōḻi* (தோழி), the heroine’s confidante and intermediary, *Pāṅkaṉ* (பாங்கன்), the hero’s friend or charioteer, *Cevilittāy* (செவிலித்தாய்), the foster-mother, and *Naṟṟāy* (நற்றாய்), the true (biological) mother. The poems are typically short interior monologues or dialogues, with the speaker identified only by convention and by the situation’s logic.

The *Tolkāppiyam* organizes *akam* relationships into two great phases. *Kaḷavu* (களவு, literally stealing or secrecy) is a clandestine union, pre-marital love conducted in secret. *Kaṟpu* (கற்பு, chastity) is wedded love, the chaste, socially sanctioned union that follows. The *Kaḷaviyal* chapter of the *Poruḷatikāram* treats the first; the *Kaṟpiyal* chapter treats the second. The progression from *kaḷavu* to *kaṟpu* is not merely chronological but structural: it organizes the dramatic logic of *akam* poetry as a whole (Zvelebil 1973). This *kaḷavu/kaṟpu* division is also the explicit organizing principle of the *Kāmattuppāl*, though the subdivision is best attested in the commentarial tradition rather than the text’s own headings.

## Kāmattuppāl as Akam Transposition



The *Kāmattuppāl* divides cleanly into two halves, matching the *Tolkāppiyam*’s *kaḷavu/kaṟpu* structure. The medieval commentator Parimēlaḻakar named the divisions as Kaḷaviyal (chapters 109–115, seventy couplets, on clandestine love) and Kaṟpiyal (chapters 116–133, one hundred and eighty couplets, on wedded love). The Kuṟaḷ’s own manuscript tradition does not always include these subheadings, and the eleventh-century commentator Maṇakkuṭavar’s couplet ordering differs from Parimēlaḻakar’s in roughly 120 instances; nonetheless, the substantive division between pre-marital and post-marital love is intrinsic to the text and structurally identical to the *Tolkāppiyam*’s division.

The architectural correspondence is too precise to be coincidental. Vaḷḷuvar inherits an existing literary architecture and adapts it to his aphoristic, didactic medium. The chapter sequence traces a complete *akam* arc: beauty, union, the testimony of love, the inevitable separation, the long nights and pallor of longing, the dream-visit, the reunion, the playful quarrel, and the joy of reconciliation. No Jain, Sanskrit *Kāmaśāstra*, or pan-Indian *nīti* text has any structure remotely like this.

The *Kāmattuppāl* is not narrated; it is spoken in the same way *akam* poetry is spoken by named character-types whose identity is signaled by convention and situation rather than by an authorial frame. Parimēlaḻakar’s commentary and Maṇakkuṭavar’s earlier commentary both annotate speaker-assignments for nearly every couplet, identifying which lines belong to the *talaivi*, which to the *talaivaṉ*, which to the *tōḻi*, and so on.

**Kuṟaḷ 1101**, voiced by the *talaivaṉ*:

> ***கண்டுகேட்டு உண்டுயிர்த்து உற்றறியும் ஐம்புலனும்  
> ஒண்தொடி கண்ணே உள***  
> *kaṇṭu kēṭṭu uṇṭu uyirttu uṟṟu aṟiyum aimpulaṉum  
> oṇ toṭi kaṇṇē uḷa*
>
> *”All the five senses, that know by seeing, hearing, tasting,
> smelling, and touching reside in her, the woman of bright bangles
> alone.”*

The couplet collapses the five-fold *jñānendriya* schema into a single object of perception, the beloved. The *talaivaṉ* speaks from the moment of union itself.

**Kuṟaḷ 1167**, voiced by the *talaivi*:

> ***காமக் கடும்புனல் நீந்திக் கரைகாணேன்  
> யாமத்தும் யானே உளேன்***  
> *kāmak kaṭum puṉal nīntik karai kāṇēṉ*  
> *yāmattum yāṉē uḷēṉ*
>
> *”Swimming the fierce flood of love I find no shore; even at midnight
> I am alone.”*

The flood-without-shore image and the midnight loneliness situate the speech in the *neytal* mood, anxious separation by the shore. Compare Caṅkam *neytal* poetry, the heroine standing on the beach, the sea standing in for the depth of feeling, the absent *talaivaṉ* gone beyond the horizon.

**Kuṟaḷ 1221**, voiced by the *talaivi*:

> ***மாலையோ அல்லை மணந்தார் உயிருண்ணும்  
> வேலைநீ வாழி பொழுது***  
> *mālaiyō allai maṇantār uyiruṇṇum*  
> *vēlai nī vāḻi poḻutu*
>
> *”O evening! But you are not evening, you are a spear that drinks the
> life of those whose lover is away.”*

The trope of evening as the hour of acutest separation-grief is a classical *akam* convention. Compare *Kuṟuntokai* 234 (Allūr Naṇmullaiyār) and *Naṟṟiṇai* 152.

**Kuṟaḷ 1311**, voiced by the *talaivi*:

> ***பெண்ணியலார் எல்லாரும் கண்ணில் பொதுவுண்மை  
> நண்ணேன் பரத்தநின் மார்பு***  
> *peṇṇiyalār ellārum kaṇṇiṟ potu uṇmai*  
> *naṇṇēṉ paratta niṉ mārpu*
>
> *”I have no business approaching you, since all women are equally
> enjoying you with their eyes.”*

The *Kāmattuppāl* deploys a technical vocabulary that belongs to Tamil literary culture, and that has no equivalent in the Sanskrit *Kāmaśāstra* or Jain *śrāvakācāra* literature, *kaḷavu* (களவு), clandestine, pre-marital love, *kaṟpu* (கற்பு), wedded chaste love; the wife’s quasi-sacral power, *puṇarcci* (புணர்ச்சி), union, embrace or copulation, *pirivu/pirital* (பிரிவு / பிரிதல்), separation or longing, *pulavi* (புலவி), sulking, the mild lovers’ quarrel, *ūṭal* (ஊடல்), the lovers’ quarrel proper, often pleasurable, *nāṇam* (நாணம்), modesty, shame; both restraint and erotic charge and *alar* (அலர்), the village gossip that exposes clandestine love.

These are not technical terms of the Sanskrit *kāmaśāstra* loaned into Tamil. They are autochthonous Tamil terms with deep roots in the Caṅkam corpus. The Tolkāppiyam’s *Meyppāṭṭiyal*, the chapter on the bodily/emotive expressions, supplies further technical vocabulary for the *Kāmattuppāl*’s analysis of love’s somatic effects, pallor, sleeplessness, wasting, and sighing.

### Vātsyāyana’s *Kāmasūtra*



Vātsyāyana’s *Kāmasūtra* is the obvious Sanskrit text against which to read the *Kāmattuppāl*, and the contrast is illuminating. Vātsyāyana most likely wrote in the 3rd century CE in northern India (Doniger and Kakar, *Kāmasūtra*, Oxford World’s Classics, 2002, Introduction); the *Kuṟaḷ* is conventionally dated to the 4th–5th centuries CE in Tamilakam. The two texts are roughly contemporary and roughly subcontinental in cultural reach. They are not, however, doing the same kind of work.

The *Kāmasūtra* consists of seven books and thirty-six chapters, addressed primarily to the *nāgaraka*, the urban gentleman of leisure. It is, in Doniger and Kakar’s phrasing, the oldest extant Hindu textbook of erotic love. Its mode is prescriptive and instructional. It catalogs sixty-four arts, eight types of embrace, nine types of bite marks, and the typology of unions classified by size and intensity. It is a manual.

The single most penetrating contrast was made by Zvelebil himself, in *The Smile of Murugan* (Brill, 1973):

> “While Vātsyāyana’s work (and all later Sanskrit erotology) is
> *śāstra*, that is, objective and scientific analysis of sex, the third
> part of the Kuṟaḷ is a poetic picture of eros, of ideal love, of its
> dramatic situations.”

Vātsyāyana classifies, instructs, and prescribes. Vaḷḷuvar dramatizes, voices, and evokes. There is not a single instructional couplet in the *Kāmattuppāl*, no advice on positions, no taxonomies of partners, no calibrated proportions of bodily features. Instead, there is the heroine on the shore at midnight, the village gossip rising, the *talaivaṉ* answering the sulking *talaivi* with playful provocation. The two texts are not in different modes of the same enterprise; they are different enterprises altogether.

The *Kāmattuppāl* does not prescribe to *nāgaraka*, no urban gentleman of leisure, as the implied addressee. The implied speakers are the *talaivaṉ*, *talaivi*, *tōḻi*, and the *akam* cast. It does not catalog sexual practices. The closest the text comes to physical description is the heroine’s pallor, the wasting of her limbs, the trembling of her bandaged wrist. Where the *Kāmasūtra* explicitly situates itself within *dharma–artha–kāma* and treats *mokṣa* as outside its scope, the *Kuṟaḷ* discusses *aṟam* and *poruḷ* in its first two books but, in Book III, treats iṉpam as a *human experiential field* rather than a *life goal to be regulated*, with no *puruṣārtha* framing. *Kāmasūtra* doesn’t use landscape symbolism, but *Kāmattuppāl* contains pervasive landscape symbolism, drawing directly on the *akam* convention. No heroine’s voice in the *Kāmasūtra*; the *Kāmattuppāl* gives the *talaivi* roughly as many couplets as the *talaivaṉ*, and in the long sections on separation and longing, hers is the dominant voice.

Vātsyāyana writes a manual for a male connoisseur, Vaḷḷuvar dramatizes the interior life of lovers who speak for themselves.

A genuine internal-tradition tension complicates this picture. Parimēlaḻakar’s 13th-century commentary on the *Kāmattuppāl* explicitly reads the Kaḷaviyal/Kaṟpiyal division through the Sanskrit *saṃyoga* (union)/*vipralambha* (separation) categories of the *Kāmaśāstra* and *alaṃkāraśāstra* traditions, and seeks Sanskrit correspondences throughout. Parimēlaḻakar was a Vaiṣṇava Brahmin commentator writing nine centuries after Vaḷḷuvar, and his interpretive horizon was the pan-Indian Sanskritic literary culture of late-medieval Tamil Nadu. As Norman Cutler demonstrated (Interpreting Tirukkuṟaḷ, *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 112.4, 1992), Parimēlaḻakar systematically interprets and maneuvers the Kuṟaḷ text within his own context, grounded in the concepts and theological premises of Hinduism. His *saṃyoga/vipralambha* reading is no exception. The *Kāmattuppāl*’s native conceptual framework is the *kaḷavu/kaṟpu* of the *Tolkāppiyam*, not the *saṃyoga/vipralambha* of Sanskrit literary theory; Parimēlaḻakar’s Sanskritization is a reception-history fact, not a reading the text itself supports.

### Jain Renunciation and the *Kāma* Problem



A reader persuaded by previous discussions finds this argument uncomfortable. If Vaḷḷuvar was a Tamil Jain author writing within the Drāviḍa Saṅgha milieu, how can he devote a fifth of his text (250 of 1330 couplets) to a celebratory treatment of erotic love? Jain ethical literature is famously renunciatory. The *Tattvārtha Sūtra* of Umāsvāti treats *kaṣāya* (passion) as one of the five causes of karmic bondage (alongside *mithyātva*, *avirati*, *pramāda*, and *yoga*), and the four kaṣāyas are *krodha* (anger), *māna* (pride), *māyā* (deceit), and *lobha* (greed). *Rāga* (attachment) and *dveṣa* (aversion) are defiling attachments closely paired with the kaṣāyas; the discipline of *brahmacarya,* the fourth *vrata*, regulates sexual conduct, demanding *maithuna-virati* (abstention from sexual activity) for mendicants and *svadāra-santoṣa*/*para-dāra-virati* (contentment with one’s own wife/abstention from others’ wives) for householders. The fifth *vrata*, *aparigraha*, governs non-attachment to possessions more broadly.

How does a Jain author write 250 couplets celebrating *kāmak kaṭum puṉal*, the fierce flood of love?

The most probable position that I would like to hold here is that a Jain scholar who inherited the Tamil *Akam* inheritance, while operating under what Jain *śrāvakācāra* permits and regulates householder *kāma* within marriage. *Kāmattuppāl* is overwhelmingly about chaste married love (*kaṟpu*) rather than promiscuous *kāma* and never depicts adultery as praiseworthy, never celebrates promiscuity, never advocates extra-marital union. It is within its *akam* framework a celebration of marital love, exactly the form of *kāma* that Jain *śrāvakācāra* sanctioned. A. Chakravarti, in his 1953 Jain-affiliated translation, argued that the *Kāmattuppāl* is not a generic Sanskrit *kāmaśāstra* import but the common Tamil cultural inheritance of Jains, Buddhists, and Hindus alike. *Akam* poetry was not theologized; it was the secular literary culture of Tamilakam. A Tamil Jain author writing in the 4th or 5th century CE inherited *akam* as a literary medium, just as a Tamil Jain composer of the *Cīvaka Cintāmaṇi*, the indisputably Jain epic by Tiruttakkatēvar, inherited the Tamil epic form. The vehicle was indigenous Tamil; the ethical framework was Jain householder ethics.

The strongest precedent for a Jain author treating love within an *akam* framework is the *Cīvaka Cintāmaṇi* itself, which alternates between renunciatory teaching and elaborate erotic episodes; the hero, Cīvakaṉ, marries multiple consorts before ultimately renouncing the world. The *Nāladiyār,* the indisputably Jain anthology, devotes its third Part to themes structurally parallel to the Kuṟaḷ’s third book, including treatments of women’s beauty and the experience of love. The Tamil Jain literary tradition was not erotophobic. It inherited and used *Akam* conventions, sometimes for didactic and renunciatory framing, sometimes simply as the natural Tamil literary medium for the human experience of love.

### Bhakti Viraha-Devotional Poetry



A third comparison sharpens the picture. From the 6th century CE onward, the Tamil Bhakti corpus, the twelve Āḻvārs (Vaiṣṇava) and sixty-three Nāyaṉārs (Śaiva) developed a *viraha*-devotional mode in which the soul is figured as the heroine (*talaivi*) longing for God as the absent lover (*talaivaṉ*). Āṇṭāḷ’s *Nācciyār Tirumoḻi* (early ninth century), Nammāḻvār’s *Tiruvāymoḻi*, and Māṇikkavācakar’s *Tiruvācakam* (9th century) all deploy this convention systematically. Friedhelm Hardy demonstrated in *Viraha-Bhakti: The Early History of Kṛṣṇa Devotion in South India* (Oxford University Press, 1983) that the entire Tamil Bhakti *viraha*-devotional mode derives its conventions from the *akam* tradition.

This means the Bhakti corpus uses the same *akam* DNA as the *Kāmattuppāl*, but with a critical transformation: the *talaivaṉ* is no longer a human lover but a deity, Kṛṣṇa, Murukaṉ, Śiva. The *Kāmattuppāl* makes no such transformation. The lovers in Vaḷḷuvar’s third book are human beings; the love is interpersonal, not theological; the longing is for the absent human husband, not for the deus absconditus.

This is a decisive negative datum. If Vaḷḷuvar had composed the *Kāmattuppāl* within a Bhakti theological framework, the *talaivaṉ* of those 250 couplets would be God, and the Hindu commentarial tradition (Parimēlaḻakar especially) would have read it that way. Parimēlaḻakar does not. Even the Vaiṣṇava Brahmin commentator, eager to find Hindu correspondences, reads the *Kāmattuppāl* as describing human love between human spouses. The text resists theologization.

The chronological implication is informative. Bhakti theologized *akam* convention; the *Kāmattuppāl* uses *akam* convention untheologized. This is more easily explained if the *Kāmattuppāl* is earlier than the Bhakti theological turn — i.e., if it was composed when *akam* was still a secular literary medium, before the sixth-century Bhakti synthesis began to transform it into devotional allegory. Vaḷḷuvar’s *Kāmattuppāl* thus stands as the pre-Bhakti monument of the *akam* tradition’s secular use, a fact entirely consistent with the conventional dating of the Kuṟaḷ to the fourth–fifth centuries CE.

## *Akam* as Cultural DNA



George Hart’s *The Poems of Ancient Tamil: Their Milieu and Their Sanskrit Counterparts* (University of California Press, 1975) made the foundational case that ancient Tamil culture was saturated with concepts of sacred power that had no Sanskrit equivalent. The two most important are *aṇaṅku* (அணங்கு, sacred power, often dangerous and especially associated with women) and *kaṟpu* (கற்பு, the chaste wife’s quasi-magical inner power). Both are pre-Sanskritic Tamil conceptions; both are central to *akam* poetry; both are present in the *Kāmattuppāl*. K. R. Norman and others have criticized Hart’s broader comparative method, but the *aṇaṅku*/*kaṟpu* thesis itself stands.

The *Kāmattuppāl* opens with a chapter titled *Takai Aṇaṅku Uṟuttal* (chapter 109), the beloved inflicting overwhelming-power beauty. The word *aṇaṅku* appears in the chapter title itself. The heroine’s beauty is not merely attractive; it carries the sacred-dangerous power that Hart documented as a defining feature of pre-Sanskritic Tamil female sacrality. This concept does not exist in the Sanskrit *kāmaśāstra*, in the Jain *śrāvakācāra*, or in the Sanskrit *kāvya*. It is autochthonous Tamil, and Vaḷḷuvar opens his entire Book of Love with it.

*Kaṟpu*, the chaste wife’s sacred power, is a Tamil conception equally absent from Sanskrit *strīdharma* literature. In Sanskrit thought, the wife’s virtue is *pātivratya*, having a husband as one’s vow, and is a moral category. In Tamil *kaṟpu*, the wife’s chastity is a quasi-magical inner power that can affect the physical world: the famous case is Kaṇṇaki in the *Cilappatikāram*, whose breaking *kaṟpu* burns down the city of Madurai. The Kuṟaḷ does not invoke this magical force directly. Still, it inherits *kaṟpu* as the organizing principle of its second half (the *Kaṟpiyal* division), eighteen chapters in which the wife’s faithfulness and longing structure the entire narrative arc. *Kaṟpu* is the indigenous Tamil ethical-emotive category used by Vaḷḷuvar. In contrast, a Sanskrit author would use *pātivratya* or *strīdharma,* and a Bhakti author would use the soul’s surrender to God.

Sanskrit/Vedic literature treats *kāma* as principle, deity, sacrament, law, technique, myth, and aesthetic category, never, in one sustained work, as the voiced interior drama of nameless human lovers. The Vedas make it the first cosmogonic seed (*kāmas tad agre samavartata*, ṚV 10.129.4) and a god (AV 9.2); the Upaniṣads ritualize intercourse (BU 6.4; ChU 2.13); the dharmaśāstras regulate marriage and wifely duty (Manu 3.20–34; 5.151–155; 9.1–3); the Kāmasūtra is a technical manual addressed to the male *nāgaraka*; the Purāṇas/epics dramatize *divine* (Kṛṣṇa–gopī, Śiva–Pārvatī) and *named royal* (Rāma–Sītā) love. Tirukkuṟaḷ’s every couplet is a dramatic monologue of the *akam* type, spoken by an anonymous archetypal persona.

## Discussion



I want to conclude by stating that Tiruvaḷḷuvar must have been a Tamil Jain author, well-versed in the major Indic philosophical literatures available to him, whose ethical foundation was distinctively Jain and whose eclectic leanings operated upon and presupposed a strong Tamil cultural milieu.

The Kuṟaḷ’s treatment of *ahiṃsā*, three full chapters (26, 32, 33), elevating non-violence to the foundational definition of virtue, the explicit subordination of *satya* to *ahiṃsā* in *Kuṟaḷ* 323 (*piṉcāra*), the polemic against Vedic animal sacrifice in *Kuṟaḷ* 259, the redefinition of truth itself as harm-free utterance in *Kuṟaḷ* 291, places Vaḷḷuvar unambiguously on the Śramaṇic side of the great fault line that divides Brahmanical *dharmaśāstra* from Jain and Buddhist ethics. The structural ranking of *ahiṃsā* over *satya* is uniquely Jain. The omission of *mokṣa* as a separate *puruṣārtha* aligns with Jain householder ethics, in which liberation is implicit in the cultivation of *aṟam*. The Sāṃkhya-resonant epistemology of chapter 36 (*Meyyuṇartal*) sits at the four-way intersection of Sāṃkhya, Jain, Vedānta, and Buddhist conceptions of liberation-through-discrimination, and the Tamil scholar Devaneya Pavanar correctly noted that without the twenty-five *tattvas* and without atheism, the Kuṟaḷ is not Sāṃkhya proper.

The *Kaṭavuḷ Vāḻttu* (chapter 1) deploys the standard Tamil Jain *arhat*-epithet vocabulary: *malarmicai ēkiṉāṉ*, the lotus-walking Tīrthaṅkara, attested in Jain hagiographic motif of gods strewing lotuses beneath the moving *kevalin*; *vēṇṭutal vēṇṭāmai ilāṉ*, the *vītarāga* who has conquered passion; *poṟivāyil aintavittāṉ*, the *jitendriya* who has conquered the senses; *āti pakavaṉ*, Ādi Bhagavān, Ṛṣabhadeva, the first Tīrthaṅkara; *eṇ kuṇattāṉ*, the eight-qualitied Siddha of *Niyamasāra* doctrine; *aṟavāḻi antaṇaṉ*, the lord of the *dharmacakra*. Parimēlaḻakar himself, the Hindu Vaiṣṇava commentator, conceded that *eṇ kuṇattāṉ* is very well applicable to the Jain Arhat-Siddha. The institutional setting is the Drāviḍa Saṅgha at Madurai, traditionally founded in 470 CE; it was a Digambara monastic order that accommodated householder agricultural life, claimed the prestigious Kundakundānvaya lineage, and produced the major post-Saṅgam Tamil Jain literary works. Later condemned by the Mūla Saṅgha as *jainābhāsa* for its bathing, dietary, and settled-life accommodations, the Drāviḍa Saṅgha represents precisely the kind of Tamil Jainism, flexible, integrated, agriculturally accommodating, that could produce a text celebrating the householder farmer and the dignity of married love. The Kuṟaḷ may well be the literary monument of the very Tamil Jainism that orthodox Mūla-Saṅgha gatekeepers considered insufficiently Jain.

The *Kāmattuppāl*, 250 couplets of erotic love-poetry that no Jain, Sanskrit *Kāmaśāstra*, or Bhakti source can explain, is the indigenous Tamil *akam* tradition transposed into aphoristic *veṇpā* couplets. The architecture, Kaḷaviyal/Kaṟpiyal, mirroring the *Tolkāppiyam*’s two great divisions, the *dramatis personae* (*talaivaṉ*, *talaivi*, *tōḻi*, *pāṅkaṉ*, *cevili*) with their proper voicings, the *akam* technical lexicon (*pulavi*, *ūṭal*, *nāṇam*, *alar*, *puṇarcci*), and the indigenous Tamil ethical-emotive categories (*aṇaṅku*, *kaṟpu*), all are unmistakably autochthonous Tamil. The *Kāmattuppāl* differs sharply from Vātsyāyana, whose *śāstric* prescription has no parallel in Vaḷḷuvar’s poetic dramatization, from Jain renunciatory *brahmacarya*, with which Vaḷḷuvar’s celebration of *kaṟpu* marital love is fully consistent within *śrāvakācāra* accommodation, and from the later Āḻvār-Nāyaṉār Bhakti theological allegorization of *akam*, which Vaḷḷuvar’s pre-theological human-lovers framework strikingly precedes. The Tamil Jain author of the *Kāmattuppāl* worked within a literary inheritance older than his religious affiliation, one shared by all Tamil-speaking communities of his era, Jain, Buddhist, and Hindu alike.

## Final Position



What emerges is the portrait of an author of remarkable integration and reach, a Tamil voice speaking in a Tamil idiom to all humanity, rooted in the autochthonous literary inheritance of his land, fluent in the major Indic philosophical literatures of his age, and grounded in a Jain ethical commitment that he chose never to name. The Tirukkuṟaḷ is, in the deepest sense, a Tamil *nīti* text, Tamil in language, prosody, and the *akam* poetic DNA of its third book; *nīti* in its aphoristic *kuṟaḷ veṇpā* form and its address to the practical conduct of all human life. Its ethical foundations are Jain; its invocatory chapter draws on Jain hagiographic imagery; its institutional milieu was the Tamil Jain establishment at Madurai; and its philosophical commitments, absolute *ahiṃsā*, the harm-defined conception of truth, the omission of *mokṣa* as a separate aim, the *jñāna*-mediated soteriology, are recognizably Jain. Yet none of this is announced. No couplet names the Jina, no chapter invokes the *tīrthaṅkara*, no doctrinal term (*kevala-jñāna*, *jīva*, *karma-pudgala*) appears in the text.

This absence is not concealment; it is universalism by deliberate craft. Vaḷḷuvar inherited the Tamil cultural genius for non-sectarian humanism, the same genius that produced an *akam* tradition shared by Jains, Buddhists, and Hindus alike, a *Tolkāppiyam* whose grammar served every Tamil-speaking community, and a Madurai literary culture in which a Jain ascetic could compose a Tamil epic without renouncing his tradition and a Tamil could write about love without theologizing it. The Kuṟaḷ extends this Tamil universalism into ethical aphorism. Its Jain base is not a sectarian closure but the disciplined ground from which a universal address becomes possible.

Vaḷḷuvar’s eclecticism, then, is not the dilution of his Jain base; it is the *manifestation* of a Tamil cultural confidence that ethical truth belongs to all who can recognize it. He drew freely from *Arthaśāstra* statecraft, from Sanskrit *nīti* aphorism, from Sāṃkhya-Jain-Buddhist-Vedānta language of liberation-through-knowledge, from the pan-Indian *trivarga* organization, and from the deepest Tamil substrate of *aṇaṅku* (sacred power), *kaṟpu* (chastity-power), *uyir* (the life-soul whose protection is the root of virtue), and the *akam* grammar of voiced human experience. He did not synthesize these; he selected from them, transformed them, and let them speak in a Tamil register that belonged to no sect and to every human being.

This is the author who earned the title *Ulaka Potu Maṟai*, the common scripture of the world, *because* he was a Tamil Jain who refused to be only a Jain, *because* he wrote in Tamil for all who could read or hear, *because* his ethics rested on a foundation deep enough to bear universal weight. The Tirukkuṟaḷ’s claim on humanity is not despite its Tamil and Jain particularity but through it. The Tamil cultural genius for non-sectarian humanism, the Jain ethical rigor, the universal address, these are not three competing identities but one integrated achievement. Vaḷḷuvar was a Tamil voice speaking from a Jain ethical center to the whole human world, and the *Kuṟaḷ* remains what its tradition has long called it: not a sectarian text and not a syncretic text, but a Tamil *nīti* scripture whose particularity is the very source of its universality.

And the bedrock on which all of this rests is announced by Vaḷḷuvar himself in a single half-line that no dharmaśāstrin could have written:

> ***பிறப்பொக்கும் எல்லா உயிர்க்கும்**   
> piṟappokkum ellā uyirkkum   
> “by birth all lives are equal” (Kuṟaḷ 972)*

And he was not alone. Centuries before him, the Pāṇṭiya king Āriyappaṭai Kaṭanta Neṭuñceḻiyaṉ had already sung the same Tamil ethic in Puṟanāṉūṟu 18, *Kaṟṟal Naṉṟē*, Learning is Excellent, declaring that even a mother turns toward the learned son over her elder, that even kings call the wise over the firstborn, and that *"of the four divisions by birth, if one born low has learned, the one born high will bow to him"* (*mēṟpāl orūvaṉum aṟkoṇṭu oḻukum*). Two Tamil voices, separated by centuries, a Caṅkam king and a *nīti* poet, converge on a single conviction: *birth confers no rank, uyir and learning do.* This is precisely where Tamil ethical DNA parts company with Sanskrit *dharmaśāstra*, where Manu opens with the *varṇa* taxonomy that divides humanity at the moment of birth, the Tamil tradition opens with the equal *uyir* of every living being and the educable mind of every human being. Vaḷḷuvar's Jain *ahiṃsā* found its perfect ground in this Tamil soil, because both traditions began from the same axiom, that the worth of a life is what it does, not what womb bore it, and that single Tamil word *uyir*, the life-soul, is the whole moral architecture of the *Kuṟaḷ* in seed, the indigenous Tamil ground on which a Jain *ahiṃsā* could flower into a scripture for all humanity.
