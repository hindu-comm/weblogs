# The Sung World of Tuḷunāḍu

### An Introduction to Pāḍdana, Bhūta Kōla, and Yakṣagāna

The coastal strip of Karnataka known as Tuḷunāḍu, the Tuḷu-speaking country of the Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa and Uḍupi districts, is among the richest surviving oral-performance landscapes in South Asia. Its culture is built, as B. A. Viveka Rai puts it, on the ideology of the region's agriculture, a world of paddy, areca, and coconut, whose work, kinship, and worship are carried in song. Three interlocking traditions dominate this world: the *pāḍdana,* the sung oral epic, the *bhūta kōla,* the spirit-possession ritual the epic serves, and *yakṣagāna,* the all-night dance-drama of the same coast. They are distinct genres, but they draw on a shared reservoir of narrative, deity, and performance, and they are best understood together.

## ***Pāḍdana*****: the sung epic**



The *pāḍdana* (also *paddana*, *pārdana*) is the most widely distributed genre of Tuḷu oral poetry, the sung narrative that, in Rai’s words, is the vehicle for the genesis and dissemination of the local deities. Its name derives transparently from the Dravidian verb *pāḍu*, “to sing” (Proto-Dravidian *\*pāṭ-/pāḍ-*, DEDR 4065): a *pāḍdana* is literally “the thing sung.” Scholars classify it as a blend of myth and legend; Peter Claus describes the corpus as legends of the local spirits and deities, *bhūta*s and *daiva*s, and Heidrun Brückner stresses that these myths make up a mythological, linguistic, and poetic corpus which strengthens the cultural identity of major sections of the non-Brāhmaṇ Tuḷu-speaking population.

What makes the *pāḍdana* unusual is the breadth of contexts in which it lives. It is sung in the paddy fields by women transplanting seedlings, a women’s genre of work-song (closely related to the shorter *kabita* / *ōbēlē*) in which, as Rai shows, female singers are the true tradition-bearers even where men later perform the public ritual. It is sung in the *bhūta* shrine during the making-up of the impersonator, to summon the right atmosphere for possession. And in its longest forms, it becomes a full oral epic: the *Siri* cycle (some twenty-five hours as sung by Gōpāla Naika) and *Kōṭi-Cennaya* (about fourteen) are vast narratives, the former centered on a three-generation lineage of wronged women and bound to the mass-possession Siri cult, the latter on twin hero-brothers of the toddy-tapping Biḷḷava community who die in battle and are deified as *daiva*s. The *pāḍdana* is thus simultaneously a work-song, a ritual script, a social charter, and epic literature, and, crucially, the “referential script” without which the possession ritual has no story to enact.

## ***Bhūta kōla*****: the spirit made present**



If the *pāḍdana* is the text, the *bhūta kōla* is the performance it serves. *Bhūta*s (in Tuḷu, *būta*s) are the local deities and spirits of Tuḷunāḍu: apotheosized heroes and heroines who met tragic deaths; the spirits of wild animals (the boar *Pañjurli*, the tiger *Pilicāmuṇḍi*); serpent-deities; and guardian powers of land and lineage. Miho Ishii captures their essence: they embody *śakti*, “wild, dangerous, and fertile” divine power, the force of the forest (*guḍḍe*), and violent death. Every household and village in Tuḷunāḍu has its *bhūta*s; the people are said to worship some five hundred of them.

The *kōla* (literally “tying” the spirit to the earthly plane) is the night-long ritual in which a *bhūta* is made present. Its medium, the impersonator, is invariably drawn from designated low castes such as Naḷike, Parava, Pambada, with priest-mediums (*pātri*, *mukkāldi*) from the Biḷḷava and Baṇṭ communities. Baindur and Tapaswi describe the transition closely: the performer fasts and observes purity rules, sings the *pāḍdana*, then undergoes an elaborate make-up and costuming, face-painting, anklets (*gaggara*), a great halo-like *aṇi* of brass and coconut frond, until, at the moment he faces the deity’s metal mask (*mukha*/*muga*), “the *bhūta* awakens within him.” Possessed, he dances to drumming (*tembare*), delivers oracles, meets the villagers one by one to hear and resolve their troubles, and blesses them. As Yogitha Shetty and K. Cinnappa Gowda emphasize, the *kōla* is no mere theatre: it is “a composite system” that has functioned as an instrument of the social, judicial, and political order of Tuḷunāḍu, in which an otherwise marginalized low-caste man becomes, for the ritual night, the voice and authority of a god. The deity’s wild *śakti* is received and, through the ritual, “transformed into a calmer, more controllable form” and distributed to devotees as grace (*prasāda*).

## ***Yakṣagāna*****: the dance-drama of the coast**



Alongside these ritual genres stands yakṣagāna, the theatrical dance-drama of coastal and adjacent Karnataka, a form that, as Shetty notes, “combines dance, music, dialogue, costume, make-up and stage techniques with a unique style and form,” traditionally played from dusk to dawn. The name is a relatively recent scholastic term, of roughly the last two centuries, for the performance arts formerly known as *kēḷike*, *āṭa*, *bayalāṭa*, and *daśāvatāra*. Where the *bhūta kōla* enacts a local spirit’s own myth in a ritual frame, *yakṣagāna* more often dramatizes pan-Indian Paurāṇic and epic material (the *Rāmāyaṇa*, *Mahābhārata*, and *daśāvatāra* cycles) as public entertainment.

Yet the boundary between ritual and theatre on this coast is porous, and the genres feed one another. The *Kōṭi-Cennaya* epic, Rai records, has been carried into *yakṣagāna* folk theatre and into a satirical *puruṣa* dance that even parodies*bhūta* performances. The result is a continuum rather than a clean division: the same coast that produces the possessed, ritually-charged *kōla* also produces the spectacular, narrative-driven *yakṣagāna* stage, with shared resources of music, costume, mask, and story moving between them.

## **A single performance ecology**



Taken together, these three traditions form one performance ecology rooted in the agrarian, matrilineal society of Tuḷunāḍu. The *pāḍdana* supplies the narrative and the sung word; the *bhūta kōla* turns that word into living, possessing divinity bound to land and lineage; and *yakṣagāna* carries the coast’s appetite for sung story onto the public stage. Across all three runs, the common themes, the dangerous local deity, the hereditary performing castes, the oral epic as charter and script, and the night-long transformation of song into presence, make Tuḷunāḍu’s sung world a coherent whole.
