# Pardhān Bards of the Gond - An Origin History

### The Dravidian Memory Inside a Three-Stringed Instrument

A man walks into a Gond village in the hills above the Narmada. He is poor, poorer than the people who receive him, and they know him for it. He will eat their food, but they will not eat his; his touch is considered impure; he ranks below the farmers who greet him. And yet when he unwraps the cloth bundle on his shoulder, every head in the courtyard inclines, because what he carries is not an instrument. It is a god.

This is the central paradox of the Pardhān, and it is worth sitting with before anything else, because every other fact about them is a commentary on it. The Pardhāns are the hereditary bards, genealogists, and priest-musicians of the Gond (Koitur/Koya), the second-largest tribal people of India, and they are simultaneously indispensable and despised. No Gond clan-deity can be summoned, no marriage sealed, no funeral closed, without the Pardhān and his fiddle. And for precisely that service, he is held the lesser man. Sacred and low, in one body. Hold onto that inversion; it is the thread that runs through everything below, and it is also the single sharpest clue that the Pardhān are not a local accident but the central-Indian survivor of something very old and very widespread.

## A Caste that is not a Tribe



The Pardhān (also *Pradhan, Parghan, Pathari, Desai, Parganiha*) are not Gonds, exactly, and not not-Gonds. They are a satellite caste embedded in Gond society, sharing its clans and its gods, but endogamous and separate, occupying the niche a court might call the bards. Around them sit the other Gond service groups: the *Ojhā* diviners, the *Nagarchi* and *Dholi* drummers, the *Thoṭi* musicians of the Telugu borderland. What sets the Pardhān apart from all of them is a monopoly: only the Pardhān holds the *bānā*, and only the Pardhān may recite the myth and genealogy of the Gond.

Their geography is not their own; it is their patrons’. Where there are Gonds, there are Pardhāns, distributed in lockstep across Mandla, Dindori, Chhindwara, Betul, Seoni, Balaghat, into Gondia and Chandrapur and Bastar, and down to Adilabad in Telangana. But one valley matters above the rest, the Upper Narmada around Patangarh and Karanjia, the heartland that Shamrao Hivale and Verrier Elwin documented in the 1930s and 40s, and, half a century later, the birthplace of Jangaṛh Singh Shyam, the cattle-herd’s son who would turn this dying bardic world into one of the most celebrated art movements India has produced. The bardic country and the painting country are the same country.

R. V. Russell, cataloging the caste in 1916, derived their name from the Sanskrit *pradhāna*, “minister,” and noted they were also called *Dīwān*, the courtier’s title. Russell documented their internal architecture too: the *Rāj Pardhāns*, the highest, able to officiate as priests, said to descend from Rāj Gond–Pardhan unions; the*Ganda Pardhāns*, whose women serve as midwives; the*Thoṭhia Pardhāns;* and a scatter of territorial and craft groups. Like the Gonds, they tattoo a dotted figure on the inner left elbow as the totem of their sept.

## The God in the Wood



To understand the Pardhān, one must understand one object and one image.

The object is the *bānā*, a three-stringed fiddle, sometimes bowed, sometimes confused in the colonial record with the gourd-bodied *kiṅgrī*, strings of woven horsehair on a stick through a gourd. But to call it an instrument is to miss the entire point. Hivale, in *The Pardhans of the Upper Narbada Valley* (1946), gave it its truest name: a *portable temple*. The bānā is the *seat* of the Gond high god *Baṛā Dev* (Baṛā Pen, Budhadev, Persa Pen). The Pardhān does not play with the god. He carries the god, and his music is the door the god walks through.

This is why the bānā is fenced with rules that read like temple law. It may be played only for a Gond, never an outsider. It belongs to the patron, who may command its playing. It is kept wrapped in cloth among the household gods. Every Dassera, it is worshipped with a red rooster; every few years, it is given a goat. And the detail that says everything, in the old days, a Pardhān would not salute even a Brahmin if a bānā was hanging from his shoulder, for he could not bend his head to anyone while Thakurdeo was on his shoulder. The lowest man in the village will not bow to the highest *varṇa* in Hinduism because what rides on his shoulder outranks the Brahmin’s whole cosmos. The man is nothing. What he carries is everything.

The image is this: *Baṛā Dev* lives inside the trunk of the *sāja* tree (*Terminalia*), latent in the wood, present but withheld, and only the Pardhān’s music can draw him out*.* The god is not summoned from the sky. He is coaxed from the grain of a tree, and the coaxing is a song.

The origin myth, told in versions that line up across a century of sources, makes the logic brutally clear. In Hivale’s telling, the Gond brothers, hunted by forest spirits, go looking for a god strong enough to shelter them. They find that the great Gond god lived in the sāja tree. They fast beneath it for months and then suddenly saw the fierce form of the great god, who greets them not with blessing but with contempt: *“How can creatures like you ever worship me properly?”* They try to feed him. They offer "chickens, goats, bullocks, everything they could find, and Bara Pen refuses to come. Nothing on earth can buy his presence. Until one of them made a bānā with a small stick of *khirsānī* wood, a bow with a hair from his own head, and began to play it. And the god moves. Suddenly, upon hearing the music, the god came upon the younger brother. He fell into a trance, and thus it was known that it was the result of the making of the bānā that Bara Pen came to earth.

Elwin’s *Songs of the Forest* (1935) tells it again, independently, and the structure is identical; the offerings fail, and nothing could tempt him until the reluctant youngest brother makes the first kingri or fiddle from a gourd, a piece of wood, and a strand of wire and plays so beautifully that Burra Deo comes down. The Sheik Gulab version (via Udayan Vajpeyi) lends it cinematic color: Baṛā Dev rides a white horse through the brothers’ field, vanishes into the sāja tree, and refuses lentils, liquor, and a sacrificed rooster alike until the youngest cuts a *khirsānī* bough, makes a one-stringed instrument, and sings. Then the god speaks from inside the trunk: *“Whenever you sing my song playing this instrument, I’ll make an appearance. This instrument of yours will be called Bana.”*

Three things in this myth define the Pardhān forever. First, the god is dangerous; his first form is fierce, ringed with nine hundred evil spirits and twelve hundred kinds of defamations. The music does not merely call him; it *pacifies* him. The Pardhān is at once the one who opens the door and the one who keeps the thing behind it calm. Second, the mechanism is trance; the god came upon the younger brother… he fell into a trance. The bānā is the technology by which an invisible, tree-dwelling power is made to inhabit a human body. And third, the myth is a charter for the bard’s degradation: the very music that makes the youngest brother indispensable is why his brothers looked down on him and refused to take food from his hands. His indispensability is his humiliation. He gave up the plow to carry the god, and for that, he eats last.

That is the whole structure of the Pardhān life, folded into a single story about a god hiding in a tree.

## Lingo, and the War Over a Cave



If Baṛā Dev is the god of the bānā, *Lingo* is the hero of the people, *Pahandi Pāri Kupār Lingal*, revered as the first priest, the bringer of music, and, in the modern Gondi reckoning, the founder of *Koya Punem*, the Gond religion itself. And here we must be careful, because Lingo’s story comes to us in two versions.

The oldest recorded version is Hislop’s, taken down from a Pardhān priest at Nagpore and printed in his *Papers* (1866). It is stark: the primordial Gond gods are sealed in a cave, *Kachikopa Lohagarh*, by Mahādeo (Śiva), behind a stone sixteen cubits long. Pārvatī searches for the lost gods. And Lingo, the culture-hero, simply lifts the stone and frees them. No music-master. No named pilgrimage site. A hero and a rock.

The modern version, documented by Motiravan Kangali (*Pāri Kupār Lingo*, 1986) and anchored to the revived Kachargarh pilgrimage in Gondia, is a far richer thing. The goddess *Mātā Kālī Kankālī* bears thirty-three children, the *Saga Deva*, imprisoned twelve years in the Kachargarh cave by *Shambhu Mahādeo*; the bard *Hirasuka Patalir* plays his kiṅgrī until the music fills the captives with strength; and at the urging of the goddess *Raitad Jungo* *(Jangu Bāī)*, Lingo frees them. They pour out in groups, founding the four *saga* phratries and the clan-god (Persa Pen) system.

These are not the same story, and the difference is not a matter of pedantry; it is the line between ethnographic record and living revival. *Hirasuka*, the music-master, appears in *none* of the old ethnographers, not Hislop, not Elwin, not Hivale. He is a creation of the modern movement. Elwin’s *Myths of Middle India* (1949) sits exactly on the seam: there Jangu Bāī moves the boulder, and Lingo’s bānā wakes the gods, but still no Hirasuka. Whoever tells you, Hislop recorded a bard named Hirasuka freeing the gods with music is telling you something that is true of 1986 and false of 1866.

What is common across the traditions is that the Lingo organizes Gond society, kinship, *gotra*, agriculture, music, dance, and founds the *ghoṭul*, the youth dormitory (principally a Muria/Bastar institution). The Pardhāns carry the bānā, though note carefully that the bānā’s gift attaches to *Baṛā Dev*, not to *Lingo*. The cycle is still alive, it is sung today, above all by Pardhāns, at the annual Kachargarh pilgrimage at Māgh Pūrṇimā. And John Bowles’s *Painted Songs and Stories* (2009) makes the crucial connection that this epic is the single reservoir from which both the sung bardic tradition and the painted Jangaṛh Kalam draw their images. The song and the painting are the same myth in two media.

## The tour, the due, and the dragged fiddle



How did a Pardhān live? Not by begging, and the distinction is one he would defend with a curse.

The institution is known as Mangteri, a collecting tour the Pardhān makes *every third year*, visiting only the Gonds of his own clan. On it, he is the *Dasondī*, who gathers the *dasond*, his customary due; his patron is the *Ṭhākur* or *jajmān*; the gift is the dān, with a special death-due, the muar dān, claimed from a dead patron’s house. Hivale is emphatic that this is not begging but the realization of a debt or the inheritance of a rightful share, which is why the Dasondī is also theBirathiā, a word also used of the village watchman entitled to his fixed grain. The claim is hereditary, hedged, and serious; a Dasondī who begs outside his clan can be excommunicated; so can a Gond who fails his Dasondī.

And what happened when a patron cheated? Here, the Pardhān reveals his kinship with the dreaded bards of the northwest. The Cāraṇ of Rajasthan enforced their dues with *trāgu*, ritual self-wounding whose blood-guilt fell on the defaulter. The Pardhān had his own version, and it ran through the instrument-god: a cheated Dasondī would tie a string to the bānā and drag it out of the patron’s courtyard to the road, "*Thus will Bara Pen leave your house,”* he says, and walks away cracking his fingers. He does not curse the man. He removes the god. When the Pardhān Dasu of Patangarh was insulted as a beggar and left cursing, the offending Ṭhākur died within six months, and his widow frantically sent for Dasu and loaded him with gifts. The bard is low. But the bard can take the god away, and everyone knows what follows.

Then the world that sustained all this collapsed. The Gond rajas fell; patronage withered; the practical significance of their story-singing gone, the Pardhāns turned to agriculture and labor. And out of that wreckage came the strangest second act in modern Indian art, the bardic line, deprived of its patrons, picking up brush and ink and putting the gods of the song onto paper. The Jangaṛh Kalam was born from the death of the Mangteri.

## The Dravidian Thread



Now the deep question, the one that makes the Pardhān matter beyond their own hills. Are they a local curiosity, or the central-Indian survivor of an institution that once stretched across the whole Dravidian-speaking south?

The Gonds speak Gondi, a conservative South-Central Dravidian language. And when you set the Pardhān beside the most famous bards of the Dravidian world, the ancient Tamil Pāṇar/Pāṇaṉ of the Caṅkam poems, the resemblances stop looking like coincidence:

- The sacred string-instrument. The Pāṇaṉ sang to the *yāḻ*, an arched
  harp of jackfruit wood. The Pardhān plays the bānā. Each caste is
  *defined* by its chordophone.

- The patron economy. The Caṅkam guide-poems (*āṟṟuppaṭai*) literally
  send a bard down the road toward a generous patron; the Pardhān walks
  the Mangteri to collect his dasond. The same institution, two thousand
  kilometers and two thousand years apart.

- The bardess. The Pāṇar traveled with the *viṟali* and *pāṭini*, their
  women-performers; the Ganda Pardhan women serve as midwives and
  tattooers and take their parts in the song-and-dance.

- The sacred-yet-degraded rank. The Tamil Pāṇar sank, over the medieval
  centuries, into a low and stigmatized caste, exactly the inversion
  that defines the Pardhān. The bard who handles the dangerous holy
  things absorbs their impurity. He is essential and unclean, and both
  for the same reason.

Widen the lens, and the pattern repeats: the Kerala*Pāṇan*, the Tulu–Kannada*Pana*, ritual singers of the*paddana*epics, performing*teyyam*with stringed instruments such as the *nantuṇi*. Instrument-bearing, hereditary, ritually charged, socially low — across Tamil, Malayāḷam, Tulu, and Gondi country alike. This is not four unrelated castes. It looks like one old Dravidian institutional type, of which the Pardhān are the surviving central-Indian branch.

## The word that survived



The Tamil bard-name is securely Dravidian to its root: *Pāṇaṉ/Pāṇar* “bard” ← *pāṇ* “song, melody” ← *paṇ*, the melodic-mode word attested from the *Tolkāppiyam* itself, descending from the Proto-South-Dravidian root *\*pā̆ṇ-*, “song, to sing” (the cognate verb *\*pāṭ-* is secure as DEDR 4065). Palaniappan (2021) shows from *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 348.4, *pāṇcēri*, the bards' street, that *Pāṇaṉ* is a genuine Dravidian musical term and not, as was once supposed, a Tamilization of Sanskrit *Bāṇa*. (The traffic, in fact, ran the other way: the minor *Bāṇa* dynasty most plausibly *rose from* the bardic *Pāṇar*, a documented case of a singer-name climbing all the way to a throne. File that beside the Pardhān’s own *Dīwān*; bards become ministers more than once in this story.)

For a long time, the safe conclusion stopped at the institution, a structural, areal parallel, no more. The name itself, *Pardhān*, was Indo-Aryan, *pradhāna*, “minister,” the courtier’s title from the Gond kingdoms, and there the linguistics seemed to end.

But the Gond bards’ *own* name for themselves was never *Pardhān*. Hivale recorded it plainly: “The Gondi word seems to be Pana.” And DEDR 4068, the standard *Dravidian Etymological Dictionary* of Burrow and Emeneau, groups the Tamil *pāṇ/pāṇar* etymon with a set of cognates that includes an attested Gondi *pāṇāl*.

Sit with what that means. The cognation of the Gond bardic word with the Tamil one is no longer a comparison. It is the classification of the standard reference work of the field. The indigenous Dravidian name of the Gond bard, *pāṇāl*, *Pana,* belongs, on Burrow and Emeneau’s own authority, to the same song root as the Tamil *Pāṇaṉ*, the Kerala *Pāṇan*, the Tulu *Pana*. What had looked like a tempting resemblance turns out to be a lexicographic fact.

So the true shape of the thing is a two-layer name, and it tells the whole history in miniature, a Dravidian functional core, *pāṇāl*, the song-man, cousin to every other *\*pāṇ-* bard from Kanyakumari to the Narmada, overlaid by an Indo-Aryan administrative title, *Pardhān*, minister, picked up in the courts of the Gond rajas, with Maratha-era accretions (*Desai*, *Parganiha*) laid on last. Strip the layers, and you reach, at the bottom, a Dravidian singer whose name means *song*.

## Discussion



Two cautionary cognates,*pāṇāl*and*pāṇar,*make the point. The Tulu folk-epic is called *pārdana*, and it *looks* like *Pardhan*, but it descends from *pāḍu* “to sing” (retroflex *-ḍ-*), not from *pradhāna* (dental *-dh-*). It is possible that a song name and a minister title collided by accident at the same time; a common origin for the resemblance is also possible. And the Tulu high god *Bermer* sits beside the Gond *Baṛā Dev* in a genuinely striking parallel, both autochthonous Dravidian high gods, worshipped aniconically under trees and on unworked stone outside the village, both later eclipsed by Śiva and Śakti, both preserved in the oral epic of a hereditary singer caste, both extended into a serpent cult. A beautiful structural rhyme and the parallel is real as a type.

What remains, after the walls are built, is still remarkable enough to need no inflation: a poor, despised, indispensable caste of central India carries, in a fiddle they call a portable temple and in a name that means song, the recognizable form and now, by the dictionary’s own reckoning, the cognate vocabulary of an institution that once spanned the Dravidian south. The younger brother gave up the plow, took up the god, and was scorned for it. Three thousand years of South Asian history are folded into that resentment. And when his patrons finally fell, and the songs went quiet, he picked up a brush, and the dotted tattoo on his elbow became the dotted skin of a painted god on a wall in Paris.

He was always carrying something larger than himself. He still is.

I would like to leave it with the artwork that has been transformed from the bardic tradition of the Pardhāns into paintings in the twentieth century, in which one artwork depicts*Baṛā Dev*as the sāja tree, flanked by four wild animals resembling the Pashupati Seal (M-304).
