The question of Tiruvaḷḷuvar’s religious identity has generated more than a century of scholarly debate. Every major Indian religious tradition, such as Jainism, Śaivism, Vaiṣṇavism, Buddhism, and even secular rationalism, has at some point claimed the author of the*Tirukkuṟaḷ*as its own. In his comparative studies of Tamil literature, Kamil Zvelebil articulated what remains the most defensible position: Tiruvaḷḷuvar was “probably a learned Jain with eclectic leanings and intimate acquaintance with the early works of the Tamil classical period and some knowledge of the Sanskrit legal and didactic texts (*subhāṣita*)” (Zvelebil, *Tamil Literature*, Wiesbaden: Harrassowitz, 1975, p. 157). The author of the Kuṟaḷ, in this reading, deliberately composed a non-sectarian ethical work drawing on multiple philosophical traditions while remaining rooted in the ethical commitments of Jain *dharma*.

## The Anonymous Masterpiece



The *Tirukkuṟaḷ* (திருக்குறள்) is, in Monsieur Ariel’s celebrated phrase, the book without a name by an author without a name. The text itself names neither its author nor its title. The name “Tiruvaḷḷuvar” (திருவள்ளுவர்) first appears in a later, undated anthology, the *Tiruvaḷḷuva Mālai*, a Śaivite text whose nineteenth-century commentary introduced the colonial-era claim that Vaḷḷuvar presented the esoteric wisdom of the Vedas to the world. Zvelebil states flatly, almost no authentic information is available about Valluvar (*Tamil Literature*, 1975, p. 157).

This anonymity has made the text uniquely susceptible to sectarian appropriation. Śaivites revere Vaḷḷuvar as the sixty-fourth Nāyaṉmār. Vaiṣṇavites cite the Kuṟaḷ’s references to Viṣṇu (*māl*) and Lakṣmī (*tiru*). Jains point to the *arhat*-resonant epithets in the opening chapter and the text’s absolute *ahiṃsā*. Buddhists note the extensive parallels with the *Dhammapada*. Christians once claimed Thomas the Apostle as Vaḷḷuvar’s teacher. Dravidianists read the text as a form of rationalist humanism.

The text’s non-sectarian surface is undeniable; the question that lingers is whose ethical architecture lies beneath that surface.

## From Pope to Zvelebil: The Scholarly Debate



The Jain reading has the longest continuous scholarly support of any religious attribution. G. U. Pope, in his 1886 translation, observed: The Jains especially consider him their own, and he has certainly used several of their technical terms, and seems to have been cognizant of the latest developments of that system. H. A. Popley (1931) wrote: There is no doubt that the author of Kuṟaḷ fully accepted the Jaina teaching on *ahiṃsā*. A. Chakravarti’s 1953 English translation, the first by a Jain scholar, declared: Kuṟaḷ is an exposition of the fundamental principles of Jainism. S. Vaiyapuri Pillai (1956) concluded: Descriptions of God found in the chapter ‘Praise of God’ lead one to conclude that Valluvar must have been a Jain. K. N. Subramanyam (1987) and P. S. Sundaram (1990) reached similar conclusions.

The most authoritative formulation came from Zvelebil. In *The Smile of Murugan* (Brill, 1973) and *Tamil Literature* (Harrassowitz, 1975), he characterized Vaḷḷuvar as probably a learned Jain with eclectic leanings. He further observed that the Kuṟaḷ’s ethics are rather reflective of the Jaina moral code. Against this stands the counter-tradition, grounded in the commentarial line from Parimēlaḻagar in the thirteenth century through R. Nagaswamy (*Tirukkural: An Abridgment of the Śāstras*, 2017), which reads the Kuṟaḷ as fundamentally Hindu.

## Ahiṃsā as the Ethical Core



Vaḷḷuvar devotes three complete chapters to non-violence and vegetarianism: Chapter 26 (*Pulāl Maṟuttal*, புலால் மறுத்தல், Abstinence from Flesh), Chapter 32 (*Iṉṉā Ceyyāmai*, இன்னா செய்யாமை, Not Hurting), and Chapter 33 (*Kollāmai*, கொல்லாமை, Not Killing). Unlike the *dharmaśāstra* tradition, which treats non-violence as a contextual or secondary virtue and freely overrides it for ritual purposes, the Kuṟaḷ elevates *ahiṃsā* to the foundational definition of morality.

**Kuṟaḷ 321:**

> ***அறவினை யாதெனின் கொல்லாமை கோறல்  
> பிற வினை எல்லாம் தரும்**  
> aṟaviṉai yāteṉiṉ kollāmai kōṟal  
> piṟa viṉai ellām tarum*
>
> *“What is the essence of virtue? It is not to kill. For from killing,
> every other sin is born.”*

For Vaḷḷuvar, *ahiṃsā* is not one item on a checklist but the root virtue from which all others grow and from whose violation all sins derive. H.A. Popley (1931) wrote without equivocation taht there is no doubt that the author of Kuṟaḷ fully accepted the Jaina teaching on *ahiṃsā*. The *Tattvārtha Sūtra* (7.1) of Ācārya Umāsvāti (c. 2nd–4th century CE), foundational for both the Digambara and Śvetāmbara sects, anchors the entire Jain ethical edifice in *ahiṃsā paramo dharmaḥ* (अहिंसा परमो धर्मः), non-violence is the supreme *dharma*. The Kuṟaḷ’s structural commitment aligns precisely with this Jain priority.

### Śramaṇic–Brahmanical Fault Line



The distance between the Kuṟaḷ’s position and the Brahmanical tradition on *ahiṃsā* is not a matter of emphasis; it is a structural fault line.

**Manusmṛti 5.39:**

> ***यज्ञार्थं पशवः सृष्टाः स्वयमेव स्वयम्भुवा । यज्ञस्य भूत्यै सर्वस्य तस्माद्यज्ञे वधोऽवधः
> ॥  **
> svayam eva svayaṃbhūr āsṛjat paśūn yajñārtham \|  
> yajñārthaṃ paśuvadhash ca ato hiṃsā na hiṃsā syāt \|\|*
>
> *”Animals were created by the Self-existent (svayambhū) himself for
> the sake of sacrifice; sacrifice is for the well-being of the whole
> world; hence slaughter in sacrifice is no slaughter at all.” (Olivelle
> 2005, p. 140)*

The closing compound *vadho’vadhaḥ*, “slaughter is non-slaughter,” constitutes a definitional override; the act of killing is linguistically reclassified as non-killing when performed within the ritual frame. *Manu* 5.44 generalizes the principle (*yā vedavihitā hiṃsā… ahiṃsām eva tāṃ vidyāt*, violence prescribed by the Veda should be understood as non-violence. *Manu*5.35 actuallypunishesvegetarian refusal; a man duly engaged to dine at a sacred rite who declines consecrated meat becomes, after death, an animal during twenty-one existences.

In the Śramaṇic world (Jaina and Buddhist), *ahiṃsā* is an absolute; no context, no ritual, no authority can transform a killing into a non-killing. Vaḷḷuvar’s position is unambiguously on the Śramaṇic side of this line.

> **A note on the place of** ***ahiṃsā*** **in Manu’s lists.** Manu
> offers two well-known summary lists of *dharma*. The ten-fold
> *dharma-lakṣaṇa* of Manu 6.92, *dhṛti, kṣamā, dama, asteya, śauca,
> indriya-nigraha, dhī, vidyā, satya, akrodha* *does not include
> ahiṃsā*. The five virtues common to all four *varṇas in Manu 10.63
> ahiṃsā, satya, asteya, śauca, indriya-nigraha* does. Earlier polemical
> readings of Manu’s treatment of *ahiṃsā* sometimes conflate these two
> passages; the precise picture is that Manu lists *ahiṃsā* as a
> universal cross-varṇa duty (10.63) while simultaneously sanctioning
> ritual killing as a *dharmic* exception (5.39–44). The contradiction
> is intrinsic to the *dharmaśāstra* itself, not a misreading.

### Anti-Sacrifice Polemic



**Kuṟaḷ 259:**

> ***அவிசொரிந்து ஆயிரம் வேட்டலின் ஒன்றன்  
> உயிர்செகுத் துண்ணாமை நன்று**  
> avicōrintu āyiram vēṭṭaliṉ oṉṟaṉ  
> uyircekut tuṇṇāmai naṉṟu*
>
> *”Not to kill and eat the meat of an animal is better than the pouring
> forth of ghee in a thousand sacrifices.”*

The word *avi* (அவி, from Sanskrit *हविः* *haviḥ*) specifically denotes the clarified butter (*ghṛta*) poured into the Vedic sacrificial fire. The couplet does not merely prefer vegetarianism; it directly subordinates the entire Vedic ritual apparatus to the principle of non-killing. This polemic is incompatible with Brahmanical *dharmaśāstra* but entirely consistent with the Jain tradition of anti-Vedic critique that runs from Mahāvīra through the *Ācārāṅga Sūtra* (I.1.2–6) to Jinasena’s *Ādi Purāṇa* (c. 838 CE), which contains extended passages dismantling the logic of ritual animal sacrifice, the same tradition that Kuṟaḷ 259 distills into a single couplet.

### Hierarchical Subordination of Truth to Non-Violence



In the Jain framework, speech ethics rests on the *Hita-Mita-Priya* principle: speech must be *hita* (beneficial), *mita* (measured), and *priya* (pleasing). When truth would harm, a Jain practitioner is counseled to silence or skillful speech (*sūnṛta*). Truth, in other words, is a derivative virtue operating within the field established by *ahiṃsā*, never an independent value that can override it.

**Kuṟaḷ 323:**

> ***ஒன்றாக நல்லது கொல்லாமை மற்றதன்  
> பின்சாரப் பொய்யாமை நன்று***  
> *oṉṟāka nallatu kollāmai maṟṟataṉ  
> piṉcārap poyyāmai naṉṟu*
>
> *“The first and foremost good is not killing. Next to it in rank comes
> not lying.”*

The Tamil verb *piṉcāra* (பின்சார) literally means “to come after in sequence.” Vaḷḷuvar encodes a hierarchy in which truthfulness trails behind, walks in the shadow of, *kollāmai* (not killing), structurally subordinate to it, the way a consequence follows its cause. *Poyyāmai* (not-lying) does not stand on its own foundation; it *piṉcāra*, trails behind, walks in the shadow of, *kollāmai* (not-killing).

This ordering has no parallel in the Brahmanical tradition. The legend of Hariścandra placed *satya* above *ahiṃsā* (Subramanian and Rajalakshmi, 1984). In Buddhism, the *pañcaśīla* lists abstention from killing first and from false speech fourth, but without explicitly subordinating one to the other. Patañjali’s *Yogasūtra* (2.30) lists *ahiṃsā* first and *satya* second among the *yamas*, but does not articulate a logical subordination of the second to the first; Zydenbos (2006) and Worthington (1982) have noted that this Yoga-sequence reflects Jain influence on Patañjali. Only in classical Jain doctrine is the second *vrata* (truthfulness) *explicitly* derivative of the first *vrata* (*ahiṃsā*) (Sharma 1991) and the *Tattvārtha Sūtra* 7.4–7.5. Vaḷḷuvar’s *kollāmai*-first, *poyyāmai*-second hierarchy mirrors this Jain logic.

Vaḷḷuvar proceeds to articulate one of the most technically precise definitions of truth in the history of Indian ethics. He defines truth not as correspondence between statement and fact, but as the consequential impact of speech on other beings, anchoring the entire chapter of *Vāymai* (truthfulness, Chapter 30) in the foundational primacy of *ahiṃsā*.

**Kuṟaḷ 291:**

> ***வாய்மை எனப்படுவது யாதெனின் யாதொன்றும்  
> தீமை இலாத சொலல்**  
> vāymai eṉappaṭuvatu yāteṉiṉ yātoṉṟum  
> tīmai ilāta colal*
>
> *”What is truthfulness? It is nothing but utterance wholly devoid of
> ill.”*

This is not a casual remark; it is a formal definition. The opening phrase *vāymai eṉappaṭuvatu yāteṉiṉ* (”what is called truthfulness? if you ask…”) signals a deliberate act of redefinition. The answer strips truthfulness of its conventional content of factual accuracy, correspondence with reality, and replaces it with a single criterion: does the utterance cause *tīmai* (harm)? If it does, it is not the truth; if it does not, it is.

The Jain canonical parallel is preserved in *Daśavaikālika Sūtra* 7.11, a *Mūlasūtra* of the Śvetāmbara canon; the verse is also collected as Sūtra 400 in Jinendra Varṇī’s twentieth-century *Saman Suttam* compilation:

> ***तहेव फरुसा भासा गुरुभूओवघाइणी ।  
> सच्चा वि सा न वत्तव्वा जओ पावस्स आगमो ॥  **
> taheva pharusā bhāsā gurubhūovaghāiṇī \|*  
> *saccā vi sā na vattavvā jao pāvassa āgamo \|\|*
>
> *”Likewise, harsh speech that gravely injures other beings, though
> true, it should not be spoken, since sin proceeds from it.”*

Vaḷḷuvar does not merely say, "avoid harmful truth"; rather, harmful utterance is not Truth.

Vaḷḷuvar does not merely say “avoid harmful truth”; he reclassifies harmful utterance as falling outside the very category of truth. Subramanyam (1987) observed that, in this chapter, the poet implies the *Jainas'*doctrine of ahiṃsā.

### Compassionate Lie — Within or Beyond Jain Doctrine?



The most important divergence that Vaḷḷuvar took from the Jain position could be inferred from the below kuṟaḷ.

**Kuṟaḷ 292:**

> ***பொய்மையும் வாய்மை யிடத்த புரைதீர்ந்த  
> நன்மை பயக்கும் எனின்***  
> *poymaiyum vāymai yiṭatta puraittīrnta  
> naṉmai payakkum eṉiṉ*
>
> *"Even falsehood has the nature of truth, if it produces a faultless
> benefit"*

This couplet has sometimes been read as Vaḷḷuvar’s departure from Jain doctrine. Jainism counsels silence when truth would harm; Vaḷḷuvar elevates a beneficent falsehood to the status of truth itself. On closer examination, however, this is not a departure but an articulation well within the Jain interpretive horizon. Three pieces of evidence support this.

First, the *Pañcatantra* (Book III, “Crows and Owls”), a corpus in which the popular recensions are largely Jain redactions (Jain, 1999), articulates the same principle: “Even truth should be concealed if causing sorrow when revealed.” Second, the same reasoning underlies *Manu* 8.103, which permits false witness in a pious cause. The harm calculus governing truthful speech is widely shared across Indian *nīti* literature. Third, and most strikingly, the sixteenth-century Jain commentator Vāmana Munivar (சமய திவாகர வாமன முனிவர்), commenting on the Jain epic *Nīlakēci*, *cited Kuṟaḷ 292 directly* and appended the phrase *em ottu ātalin* (எம் ஒத்து ஆதலின்), so says our scripture (Zvelebil, 1975; Shanmugampillai, 2005). This is the earliest known Jain claim to the Kuṟaḷ as a Jain text, made by a commentator who recognized the *ahiṃsā*-grounded definition of speech as bearing the hallmark of his own tradition. Kuṟaḷ 292 is therefore best read as the Tamil aphoristic crystallization of a position already implicit in Jain ethical reasoning, not as a rebellion against it.

The Jain Hita-Mita-Priya concept advises silence or skillful speech when Truth would cause harm; it counsels restraint, avoidance, and withholding a harmful truth. But Vaḷḷuvar performs a categorical inversion by saying a factual falsehood can be elevated to the status of Truth itself (*vāymai yiṭatta*) when its outcome is faultless good (*puraittīrnta naṉmai*). This is not permission to remain silent, as the Jain canon says; it is a philosophical redefinition in which the truth/falsehood binary is dissolved altogether when *ahiṃsā* demands it.

The Jain position says *if truth harms, withhold it,* but Vaḷḷuvar goes on to say *if falsehood heals, it becomes truth.* Throughout the text, he draws on Jain ethical foundations and pushes them toward more radical, universal formulations.

### Absolute Prohibition



**Kuṟaḷ 327:**

> ***தன்னுயிர் நீப்பினும் செய்யற்க தான்பிறிது   
> இன்னுயிர் நீக்கும் வினை***  
> *taṉṉuyir nīppiṉum ceyyaṟka tāṉpiṟitu  
> iṉṉuyir nīkkum viṉai*
>
> *”Even if it means the loss of your own life, do not perform the act
> that takes away the sweet life of another.”*

Kuṟaḷ 327 pushes *ahiṃsā* beyond even *mahāvrata* rigor, no killing even to save your own life, a standard that exceeds what Jain *śrāvakācāra* demands and approaches the vows of Jain monastics, who must not even defend themselves against attack (The Jaina Path of Purification, 1979).

### Speech-Economy



Finally, Vaḷḷuvar devotes an entire chapter to *Payaniḷa Collamai* (பயனில சொல்லாமை)(Chapter 20: Against Useless Speech). He argues that speaking useless words is a violation of the "economy of virtue,” which aligns perfectly with *Mita* (Measured) speech. The Hita-Mita-Priya framework explains why Vaḷḷuvar ranks *Kollāmai* above *Poyyāmai* by ensuring that speech is Beneficial, Measured, and Pleasing.

Vaḷḷuvar devotes Chapter 20 (*Payaṉilla Collāmai*, பயனில சொல்லாமை, Avoidance of Useless Speech) to the discipline of measured speech. While no scholar has yet mapped this chapter formally onto the *Hita-Mita-Priya* triad of Jain speech-ethics, the parallel should be taken as suggestive; *hita* (beneficial) appears in Chapter 30 (*vāymai* defined as harm-free utterance); *priya* (pleasing) appears in Chapter 10 (*iṉiyavai kūṟal*, pleasant speech); and *mita* (measured) corresponds structurally to Chapter 20’s condemnation of useless words. Whether Vaḷḷuvar consciously drew on the *Hita-Mita-Priya* framework, which appears in *Daśavaikālika* 8 and the later *bhāṣā-samiti* literature, is an interpretive judgment, not a documented fact. The structural fit is striking and deserves further scholarly examination.

Most scholars who discuss the *ahiṃsā*-*satyā* relationship in the Kuṟaḷ focus narrowly on Chapters 30 and 33. But by recognizing Chapter 20 as the structural expression of *Mita,* Vaḷḷuvar’s enumeration maps precisely onto the Hita-Mita-Priya framework of the Jain speech-ethic.

## Liberation Through Discriminative Knowledge



### The Sāṃkhya Resonance



Chapter 36 (*Meyyuṇartal*, மெய்யுணர்தல், Knowledge of Truth) introduces epistemological concepts that resonate with Sāṃkhya philosophy. The resonances are real but multi-locatable; they sit at the intersection of four major Indian traditions, and disentangling which tradition Vaḷḷuvar drew from requires attention to what is present, what is absent, and what the Tamil intellectual milieu itself contributed.

**Kuṟaḷ 351:**

> ***பொருளல்ல வற்றைப் பொருளென்று உணரும்  
> மருளானாம் மாணாப் பிறப்பு  **
> poruḷalla vaṟṟaip poruḷeṉṟu uṇarum  
> maruḷāṉām māṇāp piṟappu*
>
> *”The misery of birth arises from the delusion which mistakes the
> unreal for the real.”*

This is the closest approach to Sāṃkhya’s *viveka* doctrine anywhere in the *Tirukkuṟaḷ*. The formulation, suffering originating in the confusion of the non-self with the self, the unreal with the real, closely parallels Sāṃkhya’s core doctrine that bondage results from the failure to discriminate (*aviveka*) between *puruṣa* (consciousness) and *prakṛti* (matter). The *Sāṃkhyakārikā* of Īśvarakṛṣṇa (verse 44) identifies *viparyaya* (misapprehension) as the root of bondage. Patañjali’s *Yogasūtra* (2.5) elaborates the same principle as *avidyā*; taking the non-eternal for the eternal, the impure for the pure, pain for pleasure, and the non-self for the self.

But this concept is not Sāṃkhya’s exclusive property. In Jain epistemology, *mithyā-darśana* (false perception) is one of the three root causes of bondage, alongside *mithyā-jñāna* (false knowledge) and *mithyā-cāritra* (false conduct); the *Tattvārtha Sūtra* (1.2) lists *samyag-darśana* (right perception) as the first jewel of liberation, the *ratnatraya*. The *ISJS-Transactions* journal (Ashok, 2023) explicitly links Kuṟaḷ 355 to *tattva-jñāna* using Jain epistemological terminology.

**Kuṟaḷ 355:**

> ***எப்பொருள் எத்தன்மைத் தாயினும் அப்பொருள்** **  
> மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்ப தறிவு  **
> epporuḷ ettaṉmait tāyiṉum apporuḷ  
> meypporuḷ kāṇpa taṟivu*
>
> *”Whatever the nature of a thing may appear to be, wisdom consists in
> seeing the ultimate reality within it.”*

In Advaita Vedānta, Śaṅkara's foundational doctrine of *adhyāsa* (superimposition) holds that the non-self is mistakenly projected upon the self, producing the illusion of bondage. *Avidyā*, meaning ignorance of Brahman, is the root cause of*saṃsāra*. In Buddhism, *avijjā* is the first link in the chain of Dependent Origination (*paṭicca-samuppāda*), the root condition from which all suffering arises. In Tamil grammar tradition, it encodes a soul-body dualism: vowels are*uyir eḻuttu*, consonants are*mei eḻuttu*, and their combination produces*uyirmei eḻuttu*. The word *meypporuḷ* (true reality) Vaḷḷuvar uses in this very chapter functions analogously to the Sanskrit *tattva.*

A few other couplets in Chapter 36 reinforce the theme of liberation through discriminative knowledge, each resonating across multiple traditions.

**Kuṟaḷ 352:**

> ***இருள்நீங்கி இன்பம் பயக்கும் மருள்நீங்கி** **  
> மாசறு காட்சி யவர்க்கு  **
> iruḷnīṅki iṉpam payakkum maruḷnīṅki  
> mācaṟu kāṭci yavarkku*
>
> *”Darkness disappears and bliss descends upon men of clear vision free
> of delusion.”*

Vaḷḷuvar’s term, *maruḷ* (மருள், “delusion”), maps simultaneously onto *viparyaya*, *mithyā-darśana*, *avidyā*, and *avijjā* without committing to any single system’s technical architecture. This is not vagueness; it is philosophical precision of a different kind, the precision of a thinker who grasps the shared insight beneath the sectarian vocabularies. This echoes Sāṃkhya’s *viveka-jñāna*, which leads to *kaivalya* (isolation/liberation). But the Tamil phrase *māsaṟu kāṭci* (flawless vision) also corresponds to Jain *samyag-darśana* (right perception), the first jewel of the *ratnatraya*.

**Saman Suttam 143:**

> *micchā-daṃsaṇa-maṇṇāṇaṃ, avirati-bhāva-puvvagam…*
>
> *"Delusion (maruḷ) and Ignorance lead to the lack of restraint
> (avirati), which is the source of all violence."*

**Sāṃkhya Kārikā 63:**

> *ūpaiḥ saptabhir eva tu badhnāty ātmānam ātmanā prakṛtiḥ...*
>
> *"Prakṛti binds herself by seven forms... but she releases herself by
> one form, which is Knowledge (Jñāna)."*

**Dhammapada 174:**

> *andhabhūto ayaṃ loko, tanuk’ettha vipassati...*
>
> *"Blind is this world; few are those who see clearly. As a bird
> escapes from a net, so few go to a good state."*

The distinction between sensory knowledge and discriminative knowledge parallels Sāṃkhya’s epistemological hierarchy, where the *jñānendriyas* (sense-organs) provide lower-order knowledge that must be transcended by *buddhi*-level discernment.

**Kuṟaḷ 354:**

> ***ஐயுணர்வு எய்தியக் கண்ணும் பயமின்றே** **  
> மெய்யுணர்வு இல்லா தவர்க்கு  **
> aiyuṇarvu eytiyak kaṇṇum payamiṉṟē  
> meyyuṇarvu illā tavarkku*
>
> *("Even if one has attained mastery over the five senses, it is of no
> use to those who lack the perception of the True Essence
> (Meyyuṇartal).")*

In the evolutionary sequence of Sāṃkhya, *pañca-jñānendriya, pañca-karmendriya, pañca-tanmātra, and pañca-mahābhūtas*give sense organs their systematic philosophical meaning. Sāṃkhya heavily influenced both Śrāmaṇika and Brāhmaṇika thought. The Kuṟaḷ mentions the five senses (*aimpoṟi/aintu pulaṉ*) in Kuṟaḷ 24–27 and in 343-354, where it discusses the exact Sāṃkhya-specific emanation chain.

*Tolkāppiyam* (தொல்காப்பியம்), the foundational work of Tamil grammar, contains a sophisticated proto-scientific and philosophical layer that mirrors the Sāṃkhya and Śrāmaṇika taxonomies. It defines the universe as a mixture of five elements, the *pañca-mahābhūtas.*

**Tol. Poruḷatikāram 635:**

> ***நிலம் தீ நீர் வளி விசும்போடு ஐந்தும்** **கலந்த மயக்கம் உலகம் ஆதலின்  **
> nilam tī nīr vaḷi vicumpōṭu aintum / kalanta mayakkam ulakam ātaliṉ*
>
> *(”Earth, Fire, Water, Wind, and the expansive Sky, since the world is
> a fused state of these five, it is an integrated mixture.")*

*Tolkāppiyam* further classifies living beings based on their developed senses - *oṉṟaṟivatu*, *īraṟivatu*, *mūvaṟivatu*, *nāṉkaṟivatu*, *aintaṟivatu*, *āṟaṟivatu*, as in the*pañca-jñānendriya*mentioned in Sāṃkhya or*theJīva* classification found in the *Tattvārtha Sūtra.* While the *Tolkāppiyam* does not present the*pañca-karmendriya*or*pañca-tanmātra*in a formal list as Sāṃkhya does, it uses them as the basis for *Meippāṭu (மெய்ப்பாடு)***.** The *Meyppāṭṭiyal in* *Tolkāppiyam* describes how internal emotions (*Akam*) are expressed through the *pāṇi* (gestures), *vāk* (speech), and *cakṣus* (glance), which reflect *pañca-karmendriya,* and the description of *Tiṇai* (five landscapes) relies on the sensory evocative power of *Śabda*, *Sparśa*, and *Rasa,* which reflect *pañca-tanmātra.*

The deeper claim that the conceptual substrate for Sāṃkhya-type philosophical analysis was indigenous to Tamil intellectual culture, not merely imported from Sanskrit, is plausible (Hart, 1975; Shulman, 2016) and complicates simple narratives of philosophical borrowing.

**Kuṟaḷ 357:**

> ***ஓர்த்துள்ளம் உள்ளது உணரின் ஒருதலையாப்** **  
> பேர்த்துள்ள வேண்டா பிறப்பு  **
> ōrttuḷḷam uḷḷatu uṇariṉ orutalaiyāp  
> pērttuḷḷa vēṇṭā piṟappu*
>
> *”When the mind investigates and perceives the True Reality, it need
> not think of rebirth again; for the cycle is definitively broken.”*

**Kuṟaḷ 358:**

> ***பிறப்பென்னும் பேதமை நீங்கச் சிறப்பென்னும்  
> செம்பொருள் காண்ப தறிவு  **
> piṟappeṉṉum pētamai nīṅkac ciṟappeṉṉum  
> cemporuḷ kāṇpa taṟivu*
>
> *”Wisdom consists in seeing the ‘Perfect Essence’ (Cemporuḷ), so that
> the folly of rebirth and the ignorance that causes it, may vanish.”*

Both Kuṟaḷ 357 and 358 affirm a *jñāna-mārga* (path of knowledge) soteriology, liberation through insight rather than ritual or devotion. In Sāṃkhya (*Sāṃkhya Kārikā 68*), one who renounces *aviveka* and embraces *puruṣa* will achieve *Kaivalya,* the permanent separation of the *Puruṣa* (Consciousness) from the entanglements of *Prakṛti* (Matter). In Jainism, one who renounces *mithyātva* and becomes the *Śuddha-ātman* will attain *Mokṣa.* Similarly, in Buddhism, one who removes *avijjā* and attains*nibbāna*ends*Saṃsāra.* In Vaḷḷuvar’s world, *cemporuḷ is* left unprescriptive, and the outcome of ultimate knowledge is deliberately left unspecified, whereas the liberation that comes through knowledge is shared across all the traditions mentioned earlier.

**Kuṟaḷ 346:**

> *யான்எனது என்னும் செருக்கு அறுப்பான் வானோர்க்கு  
> உயர்ந்த உலகம் புகும்  
> yāṉeṉatu eṉṉum cerukku aṟuppāṉ vāṉōrkku  
> uyarnta ulakam pukum*
>
> *"He who cuts off the pride of 'I' and 'Mine' will enter a world
> (state of liberation) that is higher than the heavens of the gods."*

Sāṃkhya philosophy underlines *Ahaṃkāra* (I-ness) and *Mamakāra* (Mine-ness), the primary distortions of Prakṛti that keep the Puruṣa trapped in the cycle of birth.

The distinction between *yāṉ* (I/self) and *eṉatu* (mine/possessions) mirrors Sāṃkhya’s overcoming of *ahaṃkāra*. The *Sāṃkhyakārikā* 64 articulates the realization arising from disciplined practice: *nāsmi na me nāham* (I am not; nothing is mine; there is no ‘I’).

**Sāṃkhya Kārikā 64:**

> *tattvābhyāsān nāsmi na me nāham...*
>
> *”Through the practice of truth, the wisdom arises: ‘I am not, naught
> is mine, I do not act.’”*

The *Bhagavad Gītā* 12.13 commends one who is *nirmamo nirahaṅkāraḥ* (”free from possessiveness and ego-sense”).

**Bhagavad Gītā 12.13:**

> **अद्वेष्टा सर्वभूतानां मैत्रः करुण एव च ।***  *
> **निर्ममो निरहङ्कारः समदुःखसुखः क्षमी ॥ १२-१३॥***  
> adveṣṭā sarva-bhūtānāṃ maitraḥ karuṇa eva ca \|* *  
> nirmamo nirahaṅkāraḥ sama-duḥkha-sukhaḥ kṣamī \|\| 12.13 \|\|*
>
> *"One who is free from malice toward all living beings, who is
> friendly and compassionate, free from 'mine-ness' (possessiveness) and
> 'I-ness' (ego), balanced in pleasure and pain, and ever-forgiving..."*

The fifth *mahāvrata* (*aparigraha*, non-possession) of Jainism targets precisely the same pair, the internal attachment of ego (*māna/ahaṃkāra*) and the external attachment to possessions (*parigraha*). In Vedānta, *ātman* must be distinguished from the illusory *anātman*.

What differs across traditions is what remains after “I” and “Mine” are severed. In Sāṃkhya, the isolated *puruṣa* *(viveka-jñāna)* abides in its own nature (*kaivalya*). In Jainism, the *jīva* attains *kevala-jñāna* and *mokṣa*. In Vedānta, *ātman* is recognized as Brahman. Vaḷḷuvar simply says that one shall enter a world higher than the heavenly ones (*vāṉōrkku uyarnta ulakam*), a soteriological destination described in deliberately non-sectarian terms.

Larson, Bhattacharya, and Potter, in *Sāṃkhya: A Dualist Tradition in Indian Philosophy* (Princeton, 1987), observe that early Sāṃkhya doctrines in the oldest Upaniṣads provided the contextual foundations and influenced Buddhist and Jaina doctrines, and these became contemporaneous, sibling intellectual movements. This genealogical intertwining means that Sāṃkhya-resonant concepts in the Kuṟaḷ do not refute the Jain thesis; they reflect the shared philosophical substrate from which Jainism, Buddhism, and Sāṃkhya all emerged.
