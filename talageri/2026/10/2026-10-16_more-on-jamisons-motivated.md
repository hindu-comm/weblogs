I had earlier written and uploaded an article “**An Incredibly Blatant Mistranslation in Jamison’s Translations of the Rigveda**” on **2 May 2022**:

[https://talageri.blogspot.com/2022/05/an-incredibly-blatan-mistranslation-in.html](https://talageri.blogspot.com/2022/05/an-incredibly-blatant-mistranslation-in.html)

In this I pointed out how **Jamison** deliberately mistranslates a word “**aráti**” in the **Rigveda** as “**spoked wheel**” instead of, as done by all other scholars, as “**messenger**”, “**servant**” or “**ruler**”:



As an illustration, see how the other eminent western academic translators of the **Rigveda** have translated this word. For that we will take the first half of one of the verses, **I**.**59**.2: **mūrdhā divo nābhir agnih pṛthivyā athābhavad *aratī* rodasyoh**.

*Griffith*: "**The forehead of the sky, earth's center, Agni became the *messenger* of earth and heaven**".

*Wilson*: "**Agni the head of heaven, the navel of earth, became the *ruler* over both earth and heaven**".

*Grassmann*: **Des Himmels Haupt, der Erde Nabel, agni, ist beider Welten Diener er gewarden**:

"**The head of heaven, the navel of earth, Agni has become the *servant* of both worlds**".

*Geldner*: **Das Haupt des Himmels, der Nabel der Erde ist Agni, und er war der Lenker beider Welten**:

"**The head of heaven, the navel of the earth is Agni, and he was the *ruler* of both worlds**".

Contrast this with Jamison's **motivated mistranslation**:

"**The head of heaven the navel of the earth is Agni. And he became the *spoked wheel* of the two world-halves**".



Note also what two prominent western **Sanskrit**-**English** **dictionaries** have to say:

*Monier Williams*: "'**moving quickly'; a servant, assistant, manager, administrator**".

*Cappeller*: "**1. assistant, minister, disposer. 2. discomfort, uneasiness**".

**My interpretation of the word *arati* is based on the analysis of the German Vedicist Paul Thieme, and it has been incorporated into the standard etymological dictionary of Sanskrit, that of Manfred Mayrhofer 1986-96, *Etymologisches Wörterbuch des Altindoarischen*, 3 vols, Heidelberg, Carl Winter \[=Mayrhofer *EWA*\].**”

This verbatim reply reached me through other (reliable) sources as an explanation for **Jamison’s** translation of the word.



Let us examine this reply in detail:

1. “**Dictionaries: Monier-Williams, etc. are seriously out of date and they never were accurate for the meanings of Vedic words (whose meanings often differ significantly from those of their much later Epic and classical counterparts)**”.

This sweeping dismissal of the (extremely sincere even when often wrong) traditional western **Indologists** is exactly in line with similar dismissals (particularly of **Monier**-**Williams** and **Griffith**) by unscholarly **Hindu** critics. And what she writes is **blatantly false**:

Firstly, **Monier**-**Williams** is perfectly aware of the difference between “**Vedic** **words**” and “**their much later Epic and classical counterparts**”, and frequently points out these differences in his work. About this particular word, he points out that it is a word occurring only in the **Rigveda**, and that it means “**‘moving quickly’, a servant, assistant, manager, administrator**”(**MONIER**-**WILLIAMS** 1899:87), **sharply distinguishing it very clearly** from its “**much later Epic and classical counterpart**” which means “**dissatisfaction, discontent, dullness, languor**”(**MONIER**-**WILLIAMS** 1899:86)!

Further, it is not only **Monier**-**Williams**. As we saw above, **Cappeller**, whose dictionary is even more emphatically **Vedic** but also incorporates **Classical** **Sanskrit** meanings, gives us in one place both the “**Vedic**” and the “**much later Epic and classical**” meanings. Further, see above, **Wilson**, **Griffith**, **Grassmann** and **Geldner** also *fully* *agree* with the above meaning in their translations. Likewise, **Louis** **Renou**, in his monumental etymological study, also gives the same meaning and specifically distinguishes it from the **post**-**Vedic** meaning. Are all these extremely scholarly and erudite **Indologists** also “**outdated**”?



2. “**Rigvedic lexicography has proceeded word by word, and much of our understanding of the meanings of those words has changed in the last half century or so.**”.

Oh really! What *exactly* is the revolutionary new etymological discovery from “**the last half century or so**” which shows that the word **aráti** in the Rigveda means precisely and specifically “**spoked wheel**”? And how does it override and disprove the *unanimous* *interpretation* of all those earlier Indologists? It is unfortunate that **Jamison** chooses to make such sweeping and unsubstantiated statements without deeper elaboration. 



3. “**My interpretation of the word *arati* is based on the analysis of the German Vedicist Paul Thieme, and it has been incorporated into the standard etymological dictionary of Sanskrit, that of Manfred Mayrhofer 1986-96, *Etymologisches Wörterbuch des Altindoarischen*, 3 vols, Heidelberg, Carl Winter \[=Mayrhofer *EWA*\].**”

Yes, it is true that **Thieme** suggests that the word “**arati**” is derived from the word “**ara**”, *thereby going against the opinions of all other Indologists*. But his ****purely**** ****subjective**** method, as a search on **Google** shows us, was to treat the word as “**a structural and concrete physical metaphor rather than an abstract ritual concept …. He analyzes it as a secondary collective abstract noun formed by adding the suffix -ti- to *ará- ….* He argued that the "spoked wheel" serves as a vivid visual symbol for the ritual fire pit or the radiating, moving nature of the flames …. By viewing Agni as the *aratí-*, the Rigvedic poets were conceptualizing the fire god as the central hub or the driving wheel connecting the earthly ritual domain with the heavenly realm**”. And, in fact, the only reason his interpretation is considered is because Jamison took it up: “**Thieme's structural derivation from *ará-* ("spoke") has been widely adopted by modern Vedic translators—such as Stephanie Jamison and Joel Brereton—who consistently render *aratí-* as the "spoked wheel" in their definitive English translation of the *Rigveda***” (**Joel** **Brereton** is the **co**-**author** of **Jamison’s** book!).

In the process, **Jamison** pushes in another half-truth. She cites Thieme’s interpretation as having been “**incorporated into the standard etymological dictionary of Sanskrit, that of Manfred Mayrhofer 1986-96, *Etymologisches Wörterbuch des Altindoarischen*, 3 vols, Heidelberg, Carl Winter \[=Mayrhofer *EWA*\].**”

*But Mayrhofer merely *mentions* **Thieme’s** interpretation*. He not only does not *endorse* it, but in fact *he *endorses* the opposite viewpoint of *all* *the* *other* *Indologists**. **1. The Morphological Structure**
