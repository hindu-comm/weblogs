The purpose of this article is to list out all the mathematically and theoretically possible scales (even if the word may not seem, or indeed may not actually be, very appropriate for a scale which has only one, two or three, or perhaps even four notes). The scales obviously take into consideration only the normal twelve notes known to both western and Indian music: they do not take into consideration variations in the notes as represented by the 22 **śrutis** of ancient Indian music, still functioning even now in the case of many specific **rāgas**, where some particular note(s) may be slightly higher or lower than the basic note (i.e., some “cents” above or below the regular notes, which are normally now standardized in terms of a distance of “100 cents” between any two consecutive notes).

Let me make it very clear that this article is about **musical scales in the sense of thāt**, and ****not** *musical scales in the sense of rāga***. As I have pointed out in my earlier articles as well, a ***thāt*** represents ***a bare set of all the notes used in a melody, and *only* *that set****. There can be many **different** **rāgas** ***using those very same notes***, but ***representing totally different melodies or musical expressions*** in terms of notes used in **āroha** and **avaroha** (ascending and descending scales), **vādī** and **saṁvādī** (dominant and second-dominant) notes, areas of primary movements (whether in the upper octave or in the lower octave or in both), in sets of typical signature sets of notes (**pakaḍ**) which distinguish the particular **rāga**, the use of particular **ornamentations** (**alankāras** and **gamakas**, etc.), the time of day and season, the emotion (**rasa**) primarily sought to be expressed through the melody, the movements (**śuddha** or **vakra**) between consecutive notes or the length of steps between consecutively played notes, etc.

Thus, the **rāga** **Śivarañjanī** normally has the notes **SRgPDS**. When a particular song in this **rāga** uses only these five notes, it is in both ***rāga*** **Śivarañjanī** as well as in ***thāṭ*** **Śivarañjanī**. If it uses extra notes (as most songs in ***rāga*** **Śivarañjanī** generally do) then *it should be recognized as in **rāga** **Śivarañjanī** but in a **different** **thāṭ** which uses that extra note or those extra notes (as per the list given in this article)*.

All names are from this source, unless marked by asterisk(s).

It must be kept in mind that this article intends:

1. To provide a complete list of all mathematically and theoretically possible **scales** (i.e. **thāṭs** or full sets of the notes of the 12-note octave used in any melody or **rāga**), whether or not any particular one of these scales is used in music or not.

2. To provide traditional names for these scales, as far as such names exist, from what the first source above calls the “traditional corpus”.

3. It does not matter if the **rāga** with the same note as that **thāt** actually uses *all* those notes, or sometimes uses some other *extra* note(s) for ornamentation, or strictly uses only *those* notes: *the only aim is to provide a complete list with as many as possible traditional names to provide a base for classifying melodies notes-wise*. The two main sources given above often differ in their list of notes. Thus, according to the the second source (George Howlett), **Basant**-**Bahār** contains *all the twelve notes of the octave*. However, according to the first source (Prithwindra Mukherjee) **Basant**-**Bahār** contains *nine notes*, **SrGMmPdDNS**, and does not contain **R**,**g** and **n**. Regardless of what is the actual position, and regardless of who is wrong (*and even if both are wrong*), I give the name **Basant**-**Bahār** to the 12-note **thāṭ**, simply because the first source does not name any **thāṭ** with 12 notes; and after going through all the names from the first source, I am giving names from the second source (and then from other sources named above) for the remaining scales wherever I can.

The purpose of this article is to provide a framework where anyone can check out the scale by typing the notes in “Search” (e.g. type “SRgPDS” to find the name of the scale or **thāt** to which the **rāga** belongs). In fact, the aim is to provide a *standard naming scheme (from Indian sources)* for the different **thāṭs** (so obviously there has been a pick-snd-choose process in selecting *one name out of many* for the same set of notes: for example, from all the **rāgas** with the notes **SRGMPDnNS** above, the name **Alhaiyā**-**Bilāval** is given to the **thāṭ**: *it is only in respect of the 36 “**Meladalan**” **thāṭs** that I give multiple names*). *If anyone can point out sources or names for any of the **thāṭs** which still remain unnamed in the list in this article, I will be happy to add those names, since this process is obviously still far from over and must be continuously updated*. At the moment of uploading, the list has names for 723 of the possible 2048 scales. If more are added later, the number of scales will be amended below (alongwith the addition of the new names). Any scale may be identified by the name (if there is a name) or by the serial number (1-2048) as per this list.

6. Another purpose of this article is to make very clear the difference between **thāt** and **rāga**. People (at least those interested in music in any technical sense) always like to know the name of the **rāga** to which any song belongs. The character of a **rāga** is based on many other factors (**vādī**-**anuvādī**, **calan**, **saral**-**vakra** movements, intervals or steps, **pakaḍs** and typical phrases peculiar to the particular **rāga**, etc.) and the **thāt** or set of notes that the **rāga** contains is just *one* (although the most important) of these factors. *I feel that it is necessary to note both the **rāga** and **thāṭ** of any particular song. Thus many songs in the **rāga** **Śivarañjanī** (**SRgPDS**) have additional notes for ornamentation: in that circumstance, a song having, for example, only one extra note **G** should be considered as being in **rāga** **Śivarañjanī**, but in **thāṭ** **Surañjanī** (see list below)*. 

\[This huge number of **723** **scales** (**thāṭs**), each containing many distinct **rāgas**, has no parallel outside India in its sweeping vastness. And doubtless a study of Vedic chanting/singing and other traditional forms of Indian singing, and of all Indian folk and tribal genres, will provide a very much bigger number of **thāṭs**\].
