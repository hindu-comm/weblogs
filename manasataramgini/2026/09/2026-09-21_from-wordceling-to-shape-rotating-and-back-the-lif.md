From “wordceling” to “shape-rotating” and back: the life of the American Robin

The sociobiology of some ordinary extant dinosaurs can be fascinating. For example, the sparrow or the crow. So too with the American Robin, found throughout North America.

1\. Male robins emerge in spring onto urban lawns and gardens to stake a territory of up to 60-70 × 60-70 meters (≈1 acre) or more through aggressive fights and proclamations of victory with a loud song.

2\. Then the females appear, and the males start courting them to acquire a “wife” by puffing up their red chests, singing, offering food and performing a tail dance.

3\. Once mated, they stay together throughout the rest of spring and summer. However, both partners engage in occasional extra-pair sex with neighbors to hedge their bets.

4\. The male guards his territory aggressively, while the female single-handedly builds a nest from grass and mud and lays eggs.

5\. When the chicks hatch, both parents feed them.

6\. They try to maximize their fitness by fledging 2-3 broods from spring to the end of summer, each time in a new nest.

7\. Just before autumn sets in, they molt and acquire a new set of feathers and stop singing.

8\. As soon as autumn sets in, the male robins start dramatically lowering their testosterone and consequently lose their territoriality entirely. The pair-bonds dissolve.

9\. Both males and females form large flocks and, by late October-November, start retreating to the woods where they roost communally at night.

10\. In summer, they have an almost entirely faunivorous diet, eating worms and insects. As they retreat to the woods, they make a complete switch and start subsisting entirely off berries that last through the winter. Moving as flocks, their numbers allow them to defeat the more militant Northern Mockingbirds and Catbirds in the contest for berries.

Dramatically, this behavioral change is achieved by growing and losing parts of their brain, depending on the season. In spring, the surge in testosterone from the testes causes the birth of new stem cells in the brain’s ventricular zone. These differentiate into new neurons in the brain region called the HVC (High Vocal Center), which resides within part of the cerebral hemisphere known as the nidopallium. These neurons are loaded with the (epi)genetic programming for the core song, and they are integrated into the rest of the architecture through wiring with other centers responsible for controlling the voice box of the bird. In contrast, at the end of summer, as testosterone falls, those neurons die off, the HVC shrinks dramatically, and singing ceases. On the other hand, the onset of autumn triggers the growth of new neurons in the hippocampus. This enhances the spatial memory required for navigating unfamiliar geography, remembering berry patches and localizing to communal roosts when roving in nomadic flocks. Thus, in the internet lingo of the age, the robin flips from “wordceling” to “shape-rotating” and back.

The brain is ultimately an energy-demanding organ. That is probably one reason why nature does not automatically select for constantly increasing IQ, unless a ratchet is set up for it via its entanglement with biological conflict. By cycling between different mental capacities, the robin probably saves energy and circumvents the problem of keeping whole neural networks alive when they are not required and may even drive counterproductive behaviors.

While we are not well-conversant with this field, there seems to be an analogy in artificial neural nets in the form of Dynamic Sparse Training, where instead of training a fully-connected (dense) network from the start, a sparse network is maintained during training. One of these methods, RigL (Rigging the Lottery), prunes weights with the smallest magnitude and regrows weights specifically based on which connections the network’s error signal indicates would be most useful at the moment. If there is hardware like a bird’s brain that can implement it, then it might be a potential energy-cutting mechanism in the age of high-demand data centers.
