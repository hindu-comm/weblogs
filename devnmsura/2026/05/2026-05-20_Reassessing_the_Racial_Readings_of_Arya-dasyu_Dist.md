# Reassessing the Racial Readings of Ā́rya-Dasyu Distinctions in the Ṛgveda-Saṃhitā

### And a Note on the Blonde Indra \[X. 96.8\]

Since at least the latter end of the Victorian age, the conflict between the Ā́rya-s of the Veda-s and their Dasyu foes has been perceived in the popular understanding to be one between two racially distinct groups, with an implied emphasis on distinction by skin-colour within the text. This particular racial representation of the Dasyu as the ‘bull-lipped, snub-nosed, aborigine’ of the Indo-Gangetic plains props itself up on the interpretation of eleven verses wherein words meaning ‘black’ or ‘dark’ appear as referential to opponents of the Ā́rya:*  
  * 1. *ā́ryam prā́vad . . .svàrmīḷheṣv...\|... **tvácaṁ kṛṣṇā́m** arandhayat* \[RV 1.130.8\]  
  
Geldner: 'Indra helped the Aryan in the battles for the sunlight . . . he made the **black skin** subject...'  
  
Jamison: 'Chastising those who follow no commandment, he made the **black skin (=barbarians)** subject to Manu'  
  
  
2. *pañcāśát **kr̥ṣṇā́** ní vapaḥ sahásrā..\|...átkaṃ ná púro jarimā́ ví dardaḥ* \[RV 4.16.13cd\]  
  
Geldner: 'Fifty thousand **Blacks** you defeated. You slit up the forts like age \[slits up\] a garment.  
  
Jamison: 'You scattered down the **dark fifty thousand**. You shredded their fortresses, like worn-out ages a cloak'  
  
  
3. *tvád bhiyā́ **víśa** āyann **ásiknīr** asamanā́ jáhatīr bhójanāni* \[RV 7.5.3ab\]  
  
Geldner: 'Out of fear of you the **black tribes** moved away, leaving behind their possessions without fight...  
  
Jamison: The **dark clans** went breaking ranks, leaving their supplies, from fear of you....  
  
  
4. *antáḥ **kr̥ṣṇā́m̐** aruṣaír dhā́mabhir gāt* \[RV 3.31.21b\]  
  
Geldner: 'He excluded the **Blacks** with the fiery beings (...)'  
  
Jamison: 'He has come between the **black (nights)** (and bright days) with the ruddy manifestations (of the cows \[=dawns\]).  
  
  
5. *ghnántaḥ **kr̥ṣṇā́m** ápa **tvácam*** \[RV 9.41.1c\]  
  
Geldner: 'driving away the **black skin**'  
  
Jamison: 'smashing away the **black skin \[=Dasyus\]**'  
  
  
6. *sá vr̥trahéndraḥ **kr̥ṣṇáyonīḥ** puraṃdaró dā́sīr airayad ví / ájanayan mánave kṣā́m...* \[RV 2.20.7ac\]  
  
Geldner: 'The killer of Vrtra, Indra, broke open the dasic (forts) which protected the **Blacks** in their wombs, he, the breaker of forts. He created land for Manu...  
  
Jamison: 'Smasher of Vr̥tra, splitter of fortresses, Indra razed the Dāsa (fortresses) with their **dark wombs**. He gave birth to the earth and the waters for Manu.'  
  
  
7. *yáḥ **kr̥ṣṇágarbhā** niráhann..* \[RV 1.101.1b\]  
  
Geldner: '. . . who made the ones who were **pregnant with the Blacks** abort (their embryos)...'  
  
Jamison: '...who, .....aborted the (strongholds) with their **black embryos**.'  
  
  
8. *índradviṣṭām ápa dhamanti māyáyā **tvácam ásiknīm** bhū́mano divás pári* \[RV 9.73.5cd\]  
  
Geldner: 'the pressing stones, through magical power, blow away from earth and heaven the **black skin** hateful to Indra.'  
  
Jamison: 'they blow away by magic power the **black skin** hated by Indra, from earth and from heaven..'  
  
  
9. *satrā́ khédām **aruśahā́** vr̥ṣasva* \[RV 10.116.4d\]  
  
Geldner: 'The killer of the **Blacks**'  
  
Jamison: 'As smasher of the **non-luminous**..'  
  
  
10. ***anā́so** dásyūm̐r amr̥ṇo vadhéna ní duryoṇá āvr̥ṇaṅ **mr̥dhrávācaḥ*** \[RV 5.29.10cd\]  
  
Hock: You destroyed the **noseless** (*anā́s* = *a-* 'negative' + *nā́s-* 'nose') dasyus with your weapon; you smashed those of **evil speech** in their abode.  
  
Jamison: ‘You crushed the **Dasyus mouthless (***anā́s = an-* ‘negative’ + *ā́s-* ‘mouth’) with your murderous weapon’  
  
  
11. *dā́sasya cid vr̥ṣaśiprásya māyā́ḥ* \[RV 7.99.4c\]  
  
Hock: You have destroyed the tricks even of the **dasa "bull-lipped"** (?) in the battles, O lords.  
  
Jamison: 'The magical wiles even of the **Dāsa Vr̥ṣaśipra** did you smite in the battle drives, you two superior men.'  
  
of *anā́s* as *an-* 'negative' + *ā́s-* 'mouth' – that is 'mouthless' or 'speechless; barbarian'.  
  
It is more prudent to evaluate these in the context of the text so as to note how these terms reveal their implied connotations, the association of ‘darkness’ or ‘blackness’ with the ‘foreign’ and ‘alien’ Dasyu-s against the righteous Ā́rya associated with the ‘light’ appears to us as being metaphorical, using colour-association as a signifier. Representing the contrast between good/light and evil/dark that pervades the Veda*-s* and many other traditions \[Such a fear of the black and the dark comes from very far back in time, from an age when humans had not yet mastered fire or make light, and when they feared the dangers that came from it, whether it be the creatures of the dark, of other, strange men emerging from it, or the deprivation of the thorough use of one’s vision\]. Note that this is further strengthened by neighbouring verses using the Sun, broad light, divine fire and red or fiery beings and the deliverance of lands, positive benefits to contrast with "black" or "dark", if this was a case of colourist or racialised xenophobia, one must ask why the supposed "whiteness" of the Ā́rya is not mentioned in opposition to the "dark" ones. This is the case for \[1.130.8, 4.16.13cd, 7.5.3ab, 3.31.21b, 9.41.1c, 2.20.7ac, 9.41.5 (for 9.41.1c)\].  
  
The word-form *tvácaṁ* \[skin\] that has become so crucial to the formulation of racialised portrayals of the Dasyu, we argue*,* is a metaphor for the earth or land \[has been used in 1.79.3, 1.145.5, 10.68.4, 4.17.14\], we have seen plants being referred to as *rómā pr̥thivyā́ḥ* \[body-hair of the earth\] in 1.65.8, thus the 'black skin' is more likely to be the lands, pastures and fields \[the so-called "earth of darkness"\], belonging to (in the eyes of the Vedic poet) uncultured Anārya \[non-Ā́rya\].  
  
A closely-read philological examination, particularly through RV.1.130.8, will enable this reading to be both elaborated and textually verified:  
  
*índraḥ samátsu yájamānam ā́ryam  
prā́vad víśveṣu śatámūtir ājíṣu  
svàrmīḷheṣv ājíṣu  
mánave śā́sad avratā́n  
tvácaṃ kr̥ṣṇā́m arandhayat  
dákṣan ná víśvaṃ tatr̥ṣāṇám oṣati  
ny àrśasānám oṣati*  
  
Geldner: Indra helped the sacrificing Aryan in his feuds, bringing a hundred aids in all battles, in the battles for sunlight. Chastising the disobedient, **he made the black skin subservient to Manu**. Like burning \[fire\] everything that is withered, he corches the greedy, he scorches down the Arśasāna \[Malicious Ones\].  
  
Jamison-Brereton: Indra aided the Ā́rya sacrificer in battles, affording a hundred forms of help in all contests—in contests whose prize is the sun. Chastising those who follow no commandment, **he made the black skin \[=barbarians\] subject to Manu**. As a burning \[fire\] scorches everything dried up, he scorches the thirsty - scorches Arśaśāna \[Malicious Ones\] down to the ground.  
  
To interpret it as a genuine and actual flaying of black-skinned foes does not fit well with the verse, the contrast between the sunlight \[which seems to be desired and obtained here\] and black skin does not make sense here, whose subjection is qualified by a description that is more fitting for the arson and destruction of hostile lands and properties. When one sees *tvácaṃ* for what it is, the ‘Aryan sunlight’ here starts to make more sense.  
  
Thus, Indra protects and aids the Ā́rya \[whose quality as a ritualist is emphasised as seen in the two translations - a religious distinction, not a racial one\], bringing all kinds of help, for the illumination of sunlight \[representing the light of the Ā́rya, and Aryan tradition and custom\], chastising those that did not follow Vedic rites, and he burns and destroys the skin \[the lands and pastures\] of the dark \[evil or ignorant or foreign\] ones.  
  
It's far more accurate to say that this verse praises and thanks Indra for aiding them in capturing and destroying lands of the non-Ā́rya, and bringing the sunlight \[representing the capture of "dark" \[barbarian\] lands and new presence of Ā́rya tradition, custom and speech in this land\].  
  
The case is further problematised for the noseless and bull-lipped characterisation, as Hock (2004) more accurately analysed, *anā́s* = *an-* 'negative' + *ā́s-* 'mouth' – that is 'mouthless', 'speechless; barbarian'. Representing either ritually incorrect speech and/or non-Arya speech.  
  
Considering the label 'bull-lipped', as Macdonell and Keith (1912) acknowledge - *śiprā* has uncertain interpretation, but even as one tentatively accepts this translation, it must be noted that the bull as a figure does not have negative connotations in the Veda-s, and represents masculinity and male-strength, unlike in modern parlance, and seems difficult to consider this a hostile “racialist” description. The correct interpretation of this is upto anybody at this point.  
  
What then, is the Ā́rya or the Dasyu as far as the text of the Ṛgveda-Saṃhitā is concerned? To consider this we may examine the descriptors of the Dasyu as mentioned in the text:  
1. *ávrata* - lacking commandments  
2. *ábráhman* - not having sacred formulations  
3. *māyávat* - Maya possessing  
4. *akarmán* - not doing the rituals  
5. *amantú* - not knowing the truths of the hymns  
6. *anyávrata* - following other commandments  
7. *ámānusa* - not of Manu’s lineage  
8. *áśiva* – inauspicious  
9. *ánāsa* - mouthless \[non-Arya speech\]  
10. *adēvayu –* godless  
11. *ayajvan -* non-sacrificer  
12. *devapīyu* - defaming the godless  
13. *ayajyu* - non-sacrificer  
  
Dāsa is often used interchangeably with Dasyu, but are additionally referred to as *vasnayántā* \[ransom-demanding\].  
  
The Dasyu or the Dāsa then, were those that didn’t follow Ā́rya custom, religion, language or tradition. The defining traits as evident in the Vedic corpus emphasise tradition and custom far more than the shade of the skin as a distinguishing criterion.  
  
Ā́rya identity was based on common cultural norms based in ancestral tradition, on common speech, custom and religion. We may ‘reverse’ the appellations applied to Dasyu*-s* to arrive at what an Ā́rya was since the Dasyu*-s* are defined and called by what they are not:

1. “of thought” \[For he knows the right thought of Deva-s\]  
2. “of action/rites” \[For he does the rites and sacrifices\]  
3. “Son of *Manu*“ \[Belonging to the Ā́rya peoples\]  
4. “Of vows/commandments” \[Believer of divine principles\]  
5. “Of right speech” \[Vedic Sanskrit - Ā́rya speech\]  
6. “of *bráhman*“ \[Possessing the sacred formulations\]  
7. “auspicious” \[Blessed by the *Deva-s* (Gods)\]  
8. “Mouth-possessing” \[Speaking the *Ā́rya Vāc* - the Aryan Speech, that being Vedic Sanskrit and its dialects or alternatively, the right ritual speech\]  
  
We do not assert that they never took notice of the differences in the physical characteristics of peoples among their own and around them and it is quite likely they were aware of a variety of traits and may have used it as a distinguishing factor, it was perhaps not central to the themes discussed in (or the purpose of) the Ṛgveda, therefore claims using the existing corpus are unfounded in our opinion and do not lend support to the idea that the composed hymn-collection and its commentaries intended to establish such a racialist notion. While lineage was crucial as it was what defined social identity in an age where kinship was central to it, and would have been prized. It was possible for one to ‘become’ an Ā́rya by adopting the Ā́rya ways.   
  
Much evidence implies extensive acculturation of the Anārya into Ā́rya society prior to development of well stratified four-fold *varna-s*. There are a number of Ā́rya lords mentioned in the Ṛgveda whose names are thought to be not of Indo-Aryan origin \[linguistically\], like Sumilha, Sanda, Peruka, and so on.

#### On a 'Blonde Indra'



In line with the racialisation of the Ā́rya-s is that of the Devá-s, some detractors and ill-wishers of our people and tradition assert that the Véda-s characterise Indra as possessing blonde hair and beard akin to a Northern European \[or a “White” man\] with the (malicious) intent of denying us our cultus of him and to appropriate him, as such, an oft cited verse \[RV.X.96.8\] is brought up to back their claims. It is clear upon an examination of the hymn that this is not the case:  
*  
háriśmaśārur hárikeśa āyasás   
turaspéye yó haripā́ ávardhata  
árvadbhir yó háribhir vājínīvasur  
áti víśvā duritā́ pā́riṣad dhárī*  
  
Jamison-Brereton translate this as:  
  
***Golden-bearded, golden-haired, made of metal**, who, drinking the golden (soma), has grown strong on the drink of surpassing, who with his golden steeds, rich in prizewinning mares, will deliver his two golden (horses) beyond all difficult ways.  
  
Āyasás* here translates to "metal(lic)", and was the term for the common metal of the age: Bronze. Iron, when appearing in Vedic literature is referred to as ‘*Kṛṣṇāyasa', 'Kārṣṇāyasa'* or *'Śyāmayasa* \[Black/Dark Metal\].   
  
We presume that we wouldn’t be overstating when we say that the orange\[-ish\] hue of bronze led to *āyasás* being used as a metaphor for flames, for such an association was hardly uncommon across the nations of the past \[…“The ash-wood spear above his right shoulder, rocking in the light that played from **his bronze (armour) in gleams of fire and the rising sun**” - Iliad, 22.131 - 35\] A golden, enflamed, made-of-fire Indra does not necessarily imply his physical manifestation had a particular affinity to a Northern European phenotype, especially in this hymn \[as will be evident in the next paragraph\]. Furthermore, this recalls his identity as an Āditya \[The class of solar-affiliated Devá-s governing over men by upholding innocence and who are homologised with the Sun, it being their instrument of rule and presence\], who possess sun-like hue and radiance. But in this hymn, we suggest that it is a more subtle allusion to Indra as Agni, especially Agni at the Yajña.  
  
The next verse \[10.96.9\] then states:  
*srúveva yásya háriṇī vipetátuḥ  
śípre vā́jāya háriṇī dávidhvataḥ  
prá yát kr̥té camasé mármr̥jad dhárī  
pītvā́ mádasya haryatásyā́ndhasaḥ  *
  
Jamison-Brereton: ***Whose golden lips, which are like two (golden) spoons**, have flown apart, as he sets his two lips to twitching for the prize, when, after the cup is done, he will groom his two golden (horses), upon drinking of the exhilarating drink, the gladdening stalk.*  
  
Would it make sense to identify Indra as possessing lips physically shaped like spoons here so as to remain consistent with identification in the previous verse? It would seem that this is Indra as the the fire of the sacrifice, that is to say, this is a poetic homology between Indra and Agni Jātavedas \[The Divine Fire in the mundane realm as the fire of the Yajña\].  
  
***The bronze-bodied Indra*** \[**the brownish-to-orange base of the fire**\] with his ***golden beard and hair*** \[**the wavy, hair-like tips of the fire with a yellowish hue**\] with ***two srúva-spoons*** \[**the spoons used to offer libations in the sacrificial fire**\] twitch and reach ** for ***the prize*** \[**Soma - implied by the next words**\], when after the \[**Soma**\] cup is done \[**offered**\], he'll groom his horses, upon drinking the ***"exhilarating drink"*** \[**Soma**\], and the gladdening \[**golden**\] stalk \[**of the Soma plant**\].  
  
The hymn itself is designed so as to be an extended pun between the words *hári/hárita* "gold-colored, tawny" \[used regularly for Soma - Soma extracted and pressed from the stalks of its plant, emerges as a yellowish juice - and for Indra's "fallow-bay" (a bright reddish-brown) horses\] and the verb *hárya* "enjoy, gladden" and its derivatives as part of a praise-hymn to Indra's two horses, the theme of Soma being drawn in here for the purpose of the punning.  
  
Thus, nowhere does this hymn seem to indicate that Indra and \[by implication\] his Ā́rya devotees and the Ŕ̥ṣi poet-priests described him\[self\] as "blonde" or "fair", there may be other hymns were he's described as *hári* which are not dealt with here, and must be considered separately.  
  
We must note that Indra, being an active, dynamic and an inspiring divinity, assumes many forms just as the Divine in many traditions has \[obviously\] done, and beyond just a humanoid visage \[such as a Ram (Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa 2.79, 3.233–4)\]. As such it’s unsurprising that he appears as ‘Piśaṅgarūpam Maghavan’ \[Brown/Tan (as in the colour of tanned leather or tree bark) and bountiful - The Brown Bounteous One\] in RV.8.33.3; Its clear that such descriptors in many of these cases cannot be taken as necessarily referring to a physical one given its ritual and theological context.
