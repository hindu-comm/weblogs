# A Note on the Ásura- in the Śruti Corpus

### Using Wash Edward Hale's Ásura- in Early Vedic Religion (1999)

[](https://substack.com/@ushapatih)

It appears to be the case that *Ásura-s* weren't necessarily a category of anti-gods in the earliest layers of native literature and did not exist as a designation for a specific group of gods, instead it was an appellation assigned to both mortals and the divine, to both enemies and allies, that is today most resembling of the term "Lord".  
  
Being an *ásura-* seems to not have been an inherent quality, but something that was bestowed, established, assigned or earned. These lords in the RV are also typically associated with keen insight (*krátu-*) and command over a force of fighters (*vīra-*), basically the characteristics of a good leader.  
  
Agni is the *ásura* (lord) of/among the wise \[3.3.4\], as the beloved *ásura* (lord) in \[5.15.1\], as “the *ásura* (lord) worthy of being praised by us” in \[7.2.3\], as the “all ruling” *ásura* (lord) in \[7.6.1\]. And in \[2.1.6 - see below\]

He is also described as the *hotr* (The principal priest) who sits down like an *ásura* (lord) \[7.30.3\].

Savitṛ is referred to as an *ásura* (lord) twice \[4.53.1, 5.49.2\], the same goes for Varuṇa \[2.27.10, 2.28.7\], so is the case for Mitra-Varuṇa \[7.36.2, 7.65.2\] (in the latter referred to as the “lords of/among the gods” - *devānām ásurā*).

Rudra is *ásura* (lord) in \[5.42.11\], where he is also called a *deva* in the same verse.

He is also called the “*ásura* (lord) of *maho divás* (great heaven)” and is simultaneously identified with Agni in the same verse, making Agni, also a lord over great heaven. \[2.1.6\] and then again in \[5.41.3\] he is referred to as the “*ásura* (lord) of the great sky”.

Aryaman is called the *ásura* (lord) who “dwells in the sacrificial butter” \[5.42.1\] and Parjanya is referred to as the “Father *ásura*“ \[5.83.6\], and Pusan is similarly referred to as an *ásura* (lord) \[5.51.11\].

Dyáus, the Sky Father, is possibly an *ásura* in \[3.53.7\] and possibly, but not convincingly could be the one referred to as an *ásura* in \[3.56.8, 3.29.14\].

More interestingly \[3.53.7\] tells us that an *ásura* could have heroes/fighters (*vīra-*), as the Angirasa priests are referred as the heroes/fighters of Dyáus. \[3.56.8\] alludes to the three heroes of the *ásura* who rule the threefold heavens (The Vedic envisioned the sky/heavens as being threefold - rising in three tiers or layers), hence the unsure but possible identification with Dyáus, the Daylight Sky himself, or either Rudra or Agni \[see above\].

The *ásura* in \[3.10.14\], emerging out of the “belly of the lord” was identified by Geldner as Vṛtrá, but he’s never explicitly called as one until the late Vedic period. Luders interprets it as Dyáus and the verses referring to the heavenly origin of earthly fire, which is more likely, but one cannot be sure.

Then there’s hymns where we are unsure as to who is referred to as the *ásura*, \[3.38.4\] calls an *ásura* with the epithet *Viśvárūpa*, which has been used for many gods, most prominently Tvaṣṭṛ, but the hymn itself is dedicated to Indra, either of then could possibly be the Lord here.

Both \[5.63.3\] and \[5.63.7\] as part of the hymn to Mitra-Varuṇa use the term *ásurasya māyā*, in the case of the former, it is either Parjanya, Varuṇa or the power of *māyā* (magic, illusion) that an *ásura* possesses, hence the *māyā* used by Mitra-Varuṇa. The latter is in a similar position.

There are four times when *ásura* is possibly used for mortals in the RV, \[2.30.4\] calls on Indra to slay the heroes (*vīra-*) of the *ásura* Vrkadvaras, either a hostile human non-human lord and his fighters. Although it has been interpreted to refer to a human in the context of the hymn.

\[7.99.5\] mentions Indra and Viṣṇu smiting the hundreds and thousands of heroes (*vīra*) of the *ásura* (lord) Varcin, who is identified as a *dāsa* elsewhere.

\[7.56.24\] prays to the Maruts, asking for a powerful *ásura* (lord) to lead the poet’s people to peaceful dwelling, and \[5.27.1\] is very straightforward, the poet praises his patron, the Vedic *rājan* (chief, king) Tryaruṇa Traivr̥ṣṇa, possibly a descendant of Trasadasyu Paurukutsya, who is a prominent *rājan* of the Puru super-tribe in the RV hymns. The poet praises Tryaruṇa’s generosity and calls him the “*ásura* (lord) who is more excellent than any other”.  
  
In the later books of the RV (Mandala-s 1, 8, 9 and 10), *ásura* appears multiple times, twice \[10.11.6, 8.19.23\] or thrice \[10.74.2\] for Agni, thrice for Varuṇa \[1.24.14, 8.42.1, 10.132.4\].

Indra appears four times as an *ásura* \[1.174.1, 8.90.6, 10.96.11, 10.99.12\], twice in the case of Soma \[9.74.7, 9.99.1\].

Dyáus, at least twice \[1.131.2, 10.67.2\] and possibly three more times \[10.10.2, 10.31.6, 1.54.3\], though the identification is not absolute.

Once for Tvaṣṭṛ \[1.110.3\], twice for Savitṛ \[1.35.7, 1.35.10\] as “good-leading lord”. Twice for Rudra \[8.20.17, 1.122.1\]. Twice for Mitra-Varuṇa \[8.25.4, 1.151.4\]. The Maruts \[1.64.2\], and the Víśve Devā́ḥ/All Gods \[8.27.20\]

Four occurrences where the referent is unclear \[10.92.6 - Dyáus or Rudra, 10.177.1 - unsure; possibly Svarbhānu, 10.56.6 - unsure; possibly Dyáus, 9.73.1 - unclear\]

*ásura* is used for mortals thrice again in the late books, \[1.126.2\] has the poet Kakṣīvant Dairghatamasa praise his patron, the *rājan* Svanaya Bhavya as a “lord” for his gifts, \[10.93.14\] has the poet refer to one of his patrons, Rama, as the “lordly”, the *ásura*. And \[10.138.3\] recalls the narrative of Rjisvan shattering strongholds of the *ásura* (lord) Pipru, who is possibly a non-Aryan.

\[10.82.5\] draws up a parallel opposition as part of the poetic structure of the hymn, expressing the contrast between the gods who rule from the heavens, and the *ásura-s* (lords) that rule from the earth. \[10.151.3\] mentions the gods creating *sraddha* (trust, faith in efficacy of the tradition) among the powerful *ásura-s* (lords).

And \[8.96.9\] refers to *ásura-s* who are godless, \[8.97.1\] has the poet ask he receive a share of the booty that Indra helped his patron obtain from enemy lords (*ásura-s*). \[10.53.4, 10.157.4\] refer to supernatural hostile lords, though not a class of such beings.  
  
Looking at the derivatives of *ásura* in various hymns also clears any hypothesis associating *ásura* status as inherent to someone by birth or pedigree. The necessity of *krátu-* (insight) and the need for lordship or lord-ness to be established and earned through support of his followers \[which also maintains it\] is pretty evident.

If we understand *asuryám* and *asuratvá* as the quality of being an *ásura*, that of “asura-ness” and “asuraship” and if we read *āsurá* and *asuryá* as “asuric”, of being *ásura* or *ásura*-like by nature, and *asurahán* as “asura-slayer”, we have some idea of how to interpret the verses where these are used.

\[5.10.2\] tells us that Agni ascended to asuraship (*asuryám*) by his insight and generosity, \[7.5.6\] has the Vasus bestow or place asuraship (*asuryám*) onto Agni for his *krátu* (insight).

Varuṇa was bestowed with *asuryám* (asuraship) in the beginning by the gods who follow his insights (*krátu*) \[4.42.2\], Mitra-Varuṇa attain everlasting lordship and dominion \[5.66.2, 7.65.1, 7.66.2, 8.25.3\].

Rudra is bestowed with *asuryám* (asuraship) \[2.33.9\] and Soma-Rudra is asked to maintain their *asuryám* (asuraship) \[6.74.1\].

The Āditya-s are described as protecting asuraship/lordship (asuryám) in \[2.27.4\], they are the divinities who are sovereign over the social realm and responsible for maintaining *rta* (cosmic order), part of which is protecting moral and worldly order, the lordship of mortal men over others and the righteous hierarchy that governs human society².

Indra is described as having been bestowed with *asuryám* (asuraship or lordship) akin to that of Dyáus, by the gods when he, assisted by Viṣṇu slayed Vṛtrá \[6.20.2\]. Which is interesting, his heroism earns him his lordship through the support of the gods, which is on equal terms with the lordship of the Sky Father.

Indra is again ascribed *asuryám* (asuraship or lordship) in \[6.30.2) and in \[6.36.1\] he is described as distributing booty to the gods after having assumed lordship among them.  
  
Paruchepa Daivodāsi’s hymn to Vāyu \[1.134.5\] is rather unclear, it either asks him to protect the ṛ́ṣi from “Lordship” (of another hostile Lord?) or to him drinking Soma by virtue of his right to lordship.

*asuratvá* occurs once for Indra \[10.99.2\] and for Uṣás \[10.55.4\], and once in a hymn \[3.55\] in the last line of every verse, dedicated to the Víśve Devā́ḥ (All Gods - all the gods represented as an undifferentiated group). This one in particular is another obstacle for those who try to maintain that Ásura-s and Deva-s were separate groups in the Rigvedic age, as each last line repeats the same thing: *mahád devānām asuratvám ékam* (great is the one and only lordship/asuraship of the gods).

As for the term *asuryá* (asuric), it is ascribed to Indra six \[4.16.2, 7.21.7, 7.22.5, 10.50.3, 10.54.3, 10.105.11\] or seven times \[3.38.7\]. Once each with Bṛhaspati \[2.23.2\], Apā́ṁ Nápāt \[2.35.2 - asuric or asura-like powers\], Sūrya \[8.101.12 - the asuric Purohita of the Devá-s\], Ródasī \[1.167.5\] and Sarasvatī \[7.96.1 - asuric or asura-like/lord-like among rivers\]. Perhaps once more for Ródasī implicitly \[1.168.7\] and in a rather obscure verse, to várṇa, “colour” or “appearance” \[9.71.2\] being taken or cast off, the hymn being to Soma Pavamāna (Self- purifying Soma), it is only concerned with a small episode in the preparation of that drink, when the pressed Soma liquid is poured over a sheep’s wool filter to remove its impurities and is collected in vessels and mixed with other ritual substances. In the context of the hymn, the last foot of the verse “*upaprútaṃ kṛṇute nirṇíjaṃ tánā*” or “He makes what floats nearby in its full measure into his fresh garment” refers to the milk to be mixed with the Soma, thus the ‘taking off of the colour’ refers to the yellowish-golden colour of the unmixed Soma extract disappearing upon the mixing.

*āsurá* (asuric) occurs five times, once with Agni \[3.29.11\], Varuṇa \[5.85.5\], the malevolent dāsa Namuci \[10.131.4\] and twice with Svarbhānu \[5.40.5, 5.40.9\], who is a hostile celestial entity, or if you agree with Jamison (1991)¹, Svarbhānu is Agni himself piercing/punishing the Sun for violating his daughter.

*asurahán* (asura-slayer) is used thrice, once for Indra \[6.22.4\], Sūrya \[10.170.2\] and Agni \[7.13.1\], which is pretty straightforward, Indra and Agni are killers of lords and conquerors of men and king.  
  
In the later Saṃhitā-s, like the Atharvaveda (of the Śaunaka recension), Agni is called *ásura* \[5.27.1\], Varuṇa is addressed as a *deva*-ruling *ásura* \[1.10.1\] and as the great *ásura* \[5.11.1\], once to Yama \[19.56.1\] and Prajā́pati \[4.15.11-12a\]. An unknown *ásura* is referred to in a charm for virility \[6.72.1\] and as a feminine malevolence \[8.6.5\] of a supernatural kind, and men are contrasted to the *deva*-s, as the *ásura-s* \[13.7.13-14\]. Indra slays the *ásura*-s \[2.27.3, 2.27.4, 6.65.3, 8.5.3, 11.7.7, 19.13.1, 10.3.11\] who here are hostiles, supernatural or otherwise. We are made aware of the *deva-*s as overlords of the *ásura*, who are contrasted eight times as distinct classes \[6.7.3, 6.100.3, 8.9.24, 10.10.26, 19.56.3, 6.108.3, 6.109.3, 8.13.1-4\] and who are depicted as hostile each other seven times \[11.12.15, 4.19.4, 10.6.10, 12.1.15, 10.3.2, 9.2.17, 9.2.18\]. Thrice the *ásura* appear as foes of men \[6.7.2, 4.23.5, 19.66.1\] and once as a malevolence that prevents cures \[2.3.3\].   
  
The Paippalāda recension of the Atharvaveda recalls twenty-one odd references, of which ten \[6.2.6, 7.3.9, 8.12.5, 8.16.8, 11.5.11, 15.23.1, 19.52.15, 20.46.5, 20.48.7, 20.54.2\] are incomprehensible due to textual corruption. Indra is a foe unto these five times \[19.31.8, 9.25.14, 20.20.3, 17.28.3, 14.3.22\], thrice they are contrasted to and as foes to men and the *deva*-s \[4.18.4, 5.26.9, 17.12.8\]. The *ásura* buries the herb Bhadrikā \[1.89.1\] and has his *dāsa* witchcraft spurred away \[17.15.4\] and appears to refer to a human chief as a “Lord of Lords” \[10.3.6\]. At this layer of the corpus, the epither *ásura-* seems to have begun morphing into a distinct, separate and hostile class from that of the *deva*-s, a particularly revealing verse \[8.13.1-4\] in the Atharvaveda (Śaunaka) names the *ásura-*s Virocana and Prahlāda.   
  
*āsurá* (asuric) occurs five times, once with Agni-Jātavedas \[3.22.4\], four times in the feminine as *āsurī́*, twice involved with remedies for leprosy \[1.24.1-2\], once as a seductress enticing Indra \[7.39.2\] and once more as a form of witchcraft \[8.5.9\].   
  
The compounds *asuramāyā́* (Lordly Magic/Illusion) and *ásurakṣiti* (*ásura-*destroying) are used once each, the former in a charm as a means by which hostile *ásura*-s roam the earth like the *deva*-s \[3.9.4\] and the latter descriptive of an amulet created by Bṛhaspati \[10.6.22\], *asurakṣáyaṇa* (*ásura-*destroying) occurs thrice as descriptive of Bṛhaspati’s weapons \[11.12.10, 11.12.12-13\]. In the Paippalāda, there are five instances, three of which are too corrupted to read \[15.18.6, 17.13.6, 17.28.2\], the other two are *āsurá* (asuric) strength bestowed by Indra \[19.36.15\] and *asuryám* (lordship) of Somārudrā \[1.109.3\].  
  
*ásura* occurs about ten times in the Saṃhitā-s of the Sāmaveda and Yajurveda and in the Khilāni (appendix; supplement) of the Ṛgveda, four times in the archaic sense of “lord” - Agni Tanūnapāt \[VS²27.12\], Āditya ** once \[MS³4.14.14\], to Indra and the Maruts \[VS 8.55\] and to an unknown Lord \[13.44\]. Twice they appear as hostiles to Agni \[VS 2.29-30\] and Indra \[SV⁴ 2.1219, TS**⁵** 4.3.11.3h\] and twice as a distinct class \[MS 2.9.1, MS 4.14.11\].   
  
*asuryám* occurs once in reference to Apām Napāt (identified with Agni) protecting it \[VS 8.24\], *āsurá* occurs thrice and always in reference to Namuci and his asuric nature \[VS 19.34, VS 20.67-68\], its feminine form *āsurī́* occurs once to positively refer to the earth as ‘asuric’ or ‘lordly’ \[VS 11.69\]. The derivative *asuryá* occurs once to condemn suicide as leading to asuric worlds \[VS 40.3\] and *asurahán* is used once to praise Tārkṣa Ariṣṭanemi \[RVKh 2.4.1\]. It is in the Brāhmaṇa-s that the classical form of *Ásura*-*Deva* distinction reaches its maturity and traces of an older connotation ceases to exist.   
  
In light of this, it would be useful to ponder about the etymology and meaning of this epithet. The term *ásura-* is etymologically derived from Proto-Indo-Iranian *\*Hásuras*, which comes from Proto-Indo-European *\*h₂ḿ̥suros* which is formed from the PIE root *\*h₂ems-*, which means “to give birth” or “beget”. It is related to *ásu-* (life, breath, essence etc), and in this context, when used as word for “Lord” can possibly be seen as one who is “Lord over life/breath/essence” or “Lord who gives life/breath/essence”.

Likely meaning and implying something along the lines of “one who begets many progeny” \[and rules over them\], it may have originally referred to tribal patriarchs and their lineage, taking on the meaning of “Lord” (as used by the Indo-Iranians), which was probably then transposed onto divine lords as well as human lords or alternatively it may been a referential to the ‘Lordliness’ of the Divine and thus extended onto mortals occupying a similar role over their kind.

1.  Svarbhānu pierces the sun with darkness. The sun can’t shine and the
    *deva*-s fail to fix it, the seer Atri either finds or heals the
    sun, or the gods smash away the darkness. While he is commonly read
    as proto-Rāhu, Jamison argues that the verb *vyadh* (“pierce”)
    implies a sudden strike at the centre of the sun and not a gradual
    swallowing, and that the remedy of smashing away the darkness in
    several strokes doesn’t sufficiently describe the ending of an
    eclipse. She reads the name Svarbhānu (Splendour/Light \[of\]
    Heaven/Radiance) as pointing to Agni, whose *bhānu* (Light) is most
    often mentioned in the Ṛgveda (about forty times) and the only one
    whose *bhānu* is compared with the Sun.

2.  Vājasaneyisaṃhitā - This is the “White” or Śukla-Yajurveda, with the
    mantra-s (saṃhitā) kept separate from the prose Brāhmaṇa commentary,
    unlike the Black or Kṛṣṇa-Yajurveda. Tradition holds that the sage
    Yājñavalkya, after a dispute with his teacher Vaiśampāyana, received
    this knowledge directly from the Sun (Āditya). The name comes from
    Vājasaneya, a patronymic of Yājñavalkya.

3.  Maitrāyaṇīsaṃhitā - One of the oldest recension of the
    Kṛṣṇa-Yajurveda and from the Kuru Country, the Maitrāyaṇīya Śākhā
    (schools of the Veda-s with their recensions specialising in
    preserving and intepreting it in their light) were formerly known as
    the Kalāpaka, after their preceptor Kalāpin, who is said to have
    been a disciple of Vaiśampāyana. Having gained the new name due to
    the patronage of the Maitraka-s of Gujarāt (475 - 776 AD). Today it
    survives only in Gujarāt and northern Mahārāṣṭra.

4.  Sāmaveda - The Veda of Songs. Most of its verses are arranged for
    chanting by its udgātṛ priests in Soma rituals. Today only three
    śākhā-s have survived, these being the Kauthuma (most widespread; in
    Gujarāt/Mahārāṣṭra and partly the Nambūtiri-s of Kerala), Jaiminīya
    (Karnāṭaka, Tamiḻnādu, Kēraḷa), and Rāṇāyanīya (rare; Karnāṭaka,
    Mahārāṣṭra).

5.  Taittirīyasaṃhitā - A later recension of the Kṛṣṇa-Yajurveda from
    Pāñcāladeśa, its origins lies in the preceptor-disciple lineage that
    stemmed from Tittiri, who through Yaska, has Vaiśampāyana in his
    teaching-lineage. Traditionally it was believed that Yājñavalkya
    renounced the teachings transmitted to him from Vaiśampāyana after
    the revelation from the Sun and thus “spat it out”, following which
    the students turned into partridges (Tittiri) and ate it up. It is
    still widely recited in South India and is perhaps the most
    widespread śākhā.
